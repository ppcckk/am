Chapter 266: The Screaming Box

# Chapter 266

"Mr. Kowalski," the man on the porch said, tapping a pen against his clipboard. "I'm here for your audit."

Dale didn't move. His knuckles whitened around the claw hammer. The concrete of the driveway felt brittle under his boots.

"Show me your credentials," Dale said. His voice came out flat. Professional.

The man in the suit smiled again. It wasn't a friendly smile.

It was the smile of a tax auditor who has already found your hidden offshore accounts.

He flipped the clipboard. A seal stamped with the Authority's insignia sat there, glowing with a faint, sickly violet light.

"Inspector Vane," the man said. "Level IV Compliance Officer. You are currently operating under a provisional suspension due to unverified elimination of a Class-V entity. I am here to determine if the infestation is still active."

Dale looked past the man.

The darkness behind Vane writhed. Not physically.

Just a trick of the light.

Or wasn't it?

"I verified the elimination," Dale said. "I documented the void mimic. I updated my Field Guide."

"Did you?" Vane stepped onto the porch. The wood didn't creak. "Because the Authority's telemetry shows a residual signature. A heat map that looks remarkably like a nest site."

"That's a sensor glitch."

"Glitches are rare at this altitude," Vane said. He raised his pen. "May I inspect the interior? I have a warrant, but I prefer to do this as a courtesy inspection."

Dale glanced at the garage window. The box was still there.

Humming.

If Vane saw it, the audit would turn into a seizure.

And if the Authority seized the box, they'd take everything. His truck.

His tools. His career.

"Enter," Dale said. He stepped aside.

Vane walked in. He didn't take off his shoes. His suit was charcoal grey, tailored to perfection. He moved with a fluidity that suggested he had never tripped over a cord in his life.

Dale locked the door. He picked up his own clipboard from the kitchen counter. He checked the time. 11:42 PM.

"Kitchen first," Vane said. He pulled a device from his pocket. It looked like a Geiger counter mixed with a laser level. "I'll need to measure the ambient dimensional pressure."

Dale watched the device. It clicked.

Once.

Twice. Then it screamed a high-pitched whine.

"High reading," Vane noted. He pointed it at the refrigerator. "You're running a compressor in here?"

"Standard model," Dale said. "It's not a portal. It's a Frigidaire."

Vane ignored him, and he walked to the living room. He pointed the device at the sofa.

The whine continued.

He pointed it at the coffee table. The whine stopped.

"Interesting," Vane murmured. He made a checkmark on his clipboard.

"You're looking for a leak," Dale said. He stepped into the room. "But you're checking the wrong places."

Vane looked up.

"And where would I look, Technician?"

Dale pointed to the baseboards.

"Void Mimics don't sit on furniture.

They mimic static objects, and they mimic clutter.

Check the pile of mail on the entryway table. Check the shoes by the door.

"

Vane blinked. He walked back to the entryway. He pointed the device at the stack of unopened bills.

The device went silent, and he pointed it at Dale's boots.

Silence.

He pointed it at the coat rack.

Silence.

Vane frowned. He walked back to the living room. He pointed the device at the sofa again. The whine returned.

"Your data is inconsistent," Vane said.

"Your calibration is off," Dale said. He kept his hand near the hammer. "You're using a Class-II sensor.

It picks up emotional resonance, not just dimensional radiation. The furniture isn't haunted.

You're just standing in a room full of stressed humans.

"

Vane paused. He looked at Dale. For the first time, the smile faded.

"Explain."

Dale pulled out his Field Guide. He flipped to the page for **VOID MIMIC**. The new line, **WEAKNESS: BUREAUCRATIC INERTIA**, glowed slightly.

"Void Mimics feed on attention," Dale said. "They feed on the fear of being caught. Your sensor isn't detecting a monster.

It's detecting your own anxiety.

You're afraid of finding nothing, so your machine is telling you there's something.

"

Vane stiffened. He looked at the device. He looked at Dale.

"That is not a documented mechanic," Vane said.

"It's a known variable," Dale said.

"Cross-contamination. You're using a human-centric protocol to detect a dimensional entity. That's a 40% debuff.

You're wasting time.

"

Vane lowered the device. He tucked it into his pocket.

"Prove it," Vane said. "Show me the nest."

Dale walked to the garage door. He opened it.

Vane followed, and he looked around the garage.

Shelves of jars. Canisters of bait.

A workbench with the humming box sitting on top.

"Where is the infestation?" Vane asked.

Dale picked up the box. He held it out.

Vane stepped back. His hand went to his jacket pocket.

He pulled out a small, silver pistol.

It wasn't lead. It was etched with runes.

"Drop it," Vane said.

"Drop what?" Dale asked.

He didn't move.

"This box is empty.

It's a container, and the mimic was inside the lid. I removed the contents.

"

"What contents?"

"Shards," Dale said. "Glass shards.

I threw them out the window.

Then I swept them up.

Then I disposed of them in the municipal compost bin outside.

"

Vane looked at the box. He looked at Dale.

"You disposed of a Class-V evidence?"

"I disposed of a hazard," Dale corrected. "Protocol dictates that if a threat cannot be contained, it must be removed from the habitat. I treated your home like a nest site. I cleared it."

Vane stared at him. The pistol didn't lower.

"You're lying," Vane said. "The telemetry doesn't lie."

"Telemetry is only as good as the input," Dale said. "You input fear. You got fear back."

Vane's jaw tightened. He looked at the workbench. He looked at the Field Guide.

"Open the Guide," Vane commanded.

Dale didn't hesitate. He opened the book. He held it out.

Vane looked down. He saw the entry for **VOID MIMIC**. He saw the weakness.

He looked up. His eyes were wide.

"Bureaucratic inertia?" Vane whispered. "That's not a weakness. That's a joke."

"It's accurate," Dale said. "It's the only thing that stops them. You stop looking.

You stop auditing. They starve.

They fade.

You're starving them right now, Inspector. Because you're too busy arguing to look at the real problem.

"

Vane looked around the garage. He looked at the shadows in the corners.

"Show me," Vane said. His voice was tighter.

Dale pointed to the far wall. Behind the shelving unit, there was a pile of old paint cans.

"The mimic didn't leave," Dale said.

"It moved. It's hiding in the clutter.

It's using your own inspection protocols against you.

You're looking for a big monster. You're not looking for a small, boring object.

"

Vane walked toward the shelves. He kept the gun raised. He pointed the sensor device at the paint cans.

The device whined.

Vane aimed the gun at the pile.

"Step back," Vane said.

"You'll contaminate the site," Dale warned. "If you shoot that, you trigger a fragmentation event.

The shards will scatter.

They'll get into the walls.

Into the foundation. Into the house's structural integrity.

"

Vane froze.

"If you don't shoot, what do we do?" Vane asked.

Dale pulled a pair of heavy-duty trash bags from his belt. He pulled a roll of duct tape.

"We isolate," Dale said. "We don't kill. We contain.

Then we transport. Then we document.

"

Vane lowered the gun slightly. He looked at Dale.

"You're proposing a Class-I containment for a Class-V threat?"

"I'm proposing a solution," Dale said. "You're proposing a panic."

Vane looked at the paint cans. He looked at Dale. He looked at the clipboard in his hand.

"If this fails," Vane said, "you lose your license. Permanently."

"If I fail, I'm dead anyway," Dale said. "But if you fail, your report gets flagged for incompetence. And you know what happens to incompetent auditors."

Vane's face went pale, and he knew.

"Go," Vane said. He stepped back.

Dale moved.

He didn't run. He walked. He approached the pile of paint cans.

He moved slowly.

Deliberately, and he picked up a can. It was heavy. He set it on the floor.

He picked up another.

He picked up a third.

Nothing happened.

Vane watched. His breathing was shallow.

Dale cleared a space on the floor. He placed a trash bag down. He opened it.

He reached into the pile. He pulled out a small, grey jar. It looked like a jar of nails.

The jar was vibrating.

Vane raised the gun again.

"Don't," Dale said.

He held the jar up.

"It's mimicking a nail jar. It's not dangerous unless you try to open it.

It's waiting for you to engage.

"

Vane lowered the gun. He stared at the jar.

"Put it in the bag," Vane said.

Dale slid the jar into the trash bag. He sealed it with duct tape. He tied it shut.

The vibration stopped.

The silence in the garage was absolute.

Vane exhaled. He looked at the bag. He looked at Dale.

"That's it?" Vane asked.

"That's it," Dale said.

"The mimic was using your hesitation.

You were afraid to act, so it hid. You stopped being afraid, so it exposed itself.

You stopped looking for a fight, so it stopped fighting.

"

Vane picked up his clipboard. He made a long, slow checkmark.

"Thoroughness rating?" Vane asked.

"Pending," Dale said. "I need a witness signature to verify the containment."

Vane looked at him. He looked at the sealed bag. He looked at the gun in his hand.

"I'll sign it," Vane said. He signed the bottom of his own report. He tore off the carbon copy. He handed it to Dale.

"You're under probation," Vane said. "One more slip, and I revoke your license."

"Understood," Dale said.

Vane turned to leave. He walked to the garage door. He paused.

"The box," Vane said. He pointed to the workbench.

Dale looked at it.

"What about it?" Dale asked.

"It's not empty," Vane said.

Dale froze.

"I checked the telemetry," Vane said. "The box is still emitting a signal. A low-level one. You missed it."

Dale looked at the box. It was humming. Louder now.

"It's just residual energy," Dale said.

"It's a beacon," Vane said. "You didn't remove the contents. You removed the *body*. The *mind* is still in the box."

Vane opened the garage door. The night air rushed in.

"You have ten minutes," Vane said. "Get that box out of your house.

Or I will authorize a Level III Purge on your property.

Fire and acid. No exceptions.

"

Vane walked out. He didn't look back.

Dale locked the door. He turned to the workbench.

The box was glowing brighter, and he picked up the trash bag with the mimic jar. He dropped it on the floor.

He walked to the box.

He opened his Field Guide.

He flipped to the page for **VOID MIMIC**.

He wrote a new line.

**STATUS: ACTIVE. CONTAINMENT FAILED. THREAT LEVEL: CRITICAL.**

He closed the book, and he grabbed the box.

He ran to the back door.

The motion-sensor light in the backyard flickered on.

It wasn't empty.

There were figures standing in the yard.

Ten of them. They weren't in suits.

They were in uniforms.

The Authority's enforcement division, and they were holding nets.

And they were looking at him.

Dale stopped. He held the box.

The lead enforcer stepped forward. He raised a hand.

"Drop the asset, Kowalski," the enforcer said. "This is now a federal seizure."

Dale looked at the enforcer. He looked at the box. He looked at his truck.

He had a lawyer on the phone. A witness. A notarized statement.

And a box that was screaming.

Dale tightened his grip on the box, and he didn't drop it.

The box screamed louder, and the net snapped open.
