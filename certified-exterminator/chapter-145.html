Chapter 145: Stamp of Extermination

# Chapter 145

The stamp hovered three inches from Dale's forehead.

It was red. Not the red of warning signs or fire extinguishers, but the deep, arterial crimson of a wound that refused to clot.

The handle was bone-white, carved with sigils that hurt to look at directly.

The Authority Auditor did not blink. Its eyes were blank parchment, smooth and yellowed, stretching across the face where a nose should have been.

Dale didn't flinch. He stepped back, the wet sludge of the tunnel sucking at his boots, and raised the clipboard higher. The plastic cover cracked under the pressure of his grip.

"Show me your license," Dale said again.

The Auditor tilted its head. The motion was jerky, like a marionette pulled by invisible strings. A sound emerged from its throat, dry and papery, like a file being slid across a desk.

"License expired," the Auditor said. "Violation Code: 88-B. Unauthorized use of improvised containment protocols. Failure to utilize approved Class III reagents."

Dale looked at the sealed drain. The cement he had mixed from rubble and saliva was still wet. It held. For now.

"I used what I had," Dale replied. His voice was flat, the same tone he used when explaining to a landlord why they couldn't just ignore the cockroach nest behind the drywall. "The supply drop was delayed. That's a logistics failure, not a compliance failure."

The Auditor's parchment face rippled.

"Logistics are irrelevant to the Act," it said. "The Act requires precision. You treated a Rattus profundus broodmother with a trash bag. Do you know the legal classification of plastic in a Class-9 rift environment?"

Dale knew. He had read the manual. Page 412, subsection D. *Plastic polymers act as conductive mediums for dimensional energy if not properly grounded.*

He hadn't grounded the bag. He had duct-taped it.

A red notification flashed in the corner of his vision.

**CROSS-CONTAMINATION ALERT: FIELD GUIDE ENTRY [Rattus profundus] CORRUPTED.** **DEBUFF: -40% EFFECTIVENESS FOR ALL PHYSICAL ATTACKS AGAINST RODENTIA SUBORDER.** **DURATION: 24 HOURS.**

Dale felt the weight of it settle in his chest. A physical heaviness, like a vest made of lead. He had just nerfed his own ability to fight the thing he had just killed. If another rat came out of that drain, his shovel swing—which was already broken—would hit with the force of a pillow fight.

"I need a citation number," Dale said.

The Auditor raised the stamp.

"Violation Code 88-B is a felony-level infestation," it said. "Penalty: Revocation of Class II Status. Immediate demotion to Class Zero. You will be unable to treat any monster above Class 1 for a period of thirty days."

Thirty days.

The Rift in Cleveland was expanding.

The infestation was spreading. Without a license, Dale couldn't legally enter the hot zones.

He couldn't access the Authority's supply caches.

He would be a civilian standing behind police lines, watching his neighbors get eaten while he held a clipboard that was legally worthless.

"That's not fair," Dale said.

"Fairness is not a metric of the System," the Auditor replied. "Thoroughness is."

The Auditor stepped forward. The shadows around it curled, trying to reach Dale's ankles.

"Sign here."

The Auditor extended a hand. It wasn't holding a pen. It was holding a quill made of black feathers, dripping with ink that smelled like burnt hair.

Dale looked at the clipboard. He looked at the quill.

He didn't take the quill.

Instead, he reached into his pocket and pulled out a small, battered notebook. His personal log. The one where he wrote down entry points, species counts, and weather conditions because he didn't trust the digital interface to capture the nuance of a job.

"Is my field data admissible in an audit?" Dale asked.

The Auditor paused. The parchment face smoothed out, becoming less aggressive, more bureaucratic.

"All primary source data is admissible," it said. "Provided it is legible and timestamped."

Dale opened the notebook. His hands shook, just slightly. He flipped to the page from three hours ago. The ink was smeared with sewer water, but the numbers were clear.

"I have a counter-claim," Dale said. "I filed a Hazard Report, Form 19-A, prior to the engagement.

I reported the structural instability of the sewer junction.

I reported the lack of ventilation.

I reported the presence of unlicensed dimensional resonance."

He held the notebook up.

"You ignored the report," Dale said. "The response time was forty minutes. During that time, the broodmother hatched.

If I had waited for your 'approved reagents,' the infestation would have reached the residential grid. I contained it.

I isolated it.

I documented it."

The Auditor's blank eyes focused on the notebook. The red stamp lowered an inch.

"Form 19-A is a suggestion, Mr. Kowalski. Not a mandate."

"It's a liability waiver," Dale said.

"If I follow protocol and the city burns down, the Authority is liable. If I deviate from protocol and save the city, the Authority is not liable.

You can't have it both ways.

You can't punish me for solving the problem because you didn't provide the tools to solve it legally."

The Auditor remained silent.

The silence stretched, heavy and oppressive. The darkness of the tunnel pressed in.

Somewhere in the distance, the skittering sound returned.

Closer this time.

Much closer.

Dale counted the seconds. One. Two. Three.

The System didn't like ambiguity. It liked rules. It liked boxes. And Dale was trying to fit the Auditor into a box.

"If you revoke my license," Dale said, "you create an unlicensed zone. That violates the Containment Act, Section 4. An unlicensed zone cannot be patrolled by licensed personnel.

You would have to close the sector. Evacuate.

That's a Thoroughness Score drop of two points for the Authority."

The Auditor's hand twitched.

Dale saw it. The glitch. The Auditor was programmed to protect its own rating.

Every job it processed affected the global Thoroughness Score.

A demotion of a high-performing technician due to Authority negligence? That would tank the score.

"You are threatening the Authority," the Auditor said. Its voice was colder now. Sharper.

"I am filing a grievance," Dale corrected. "And I am requesting a Field Audit to verify the cause of the delay."

"A Field Audit requires a Class IV Auditor," the Auditor said. "One is not present."

"Then you have two options," Dale said.

"You can revoke my license and suffer the score penalty. Or you can issue a Conditional Warning. I keep my license.

I pay the fine. I fix the contamination in my Field Guide by performing a secondary identification on a fresh sample within the hour."

The Auditor looked at the sealed drain.

"Fix the contamination?" it asked.

"I need to identify a weakness in a new specimen," Dale said.

"My guide is corrupted.

I can't get the targeted damage bonus. I need to reset it.

If you give me a Conditional Warning, I can go into the next chamber. Find a rat. Identify it.

Clear the debuff."

"That is inefficient," the Auditor said.

"It's necessary," Dale said. "If I don't clear the debuff, I can't fight the next wave. If I can't fight the next wave, the infestation spreads. If the infestation spreads, your audit fails."

The Auditor lowered the stamp completely.

The red ink dripped onto the wet concrete, sizzling.

"Conditional Warning issued," the Auditor said. "Penalty: 50 Treatment Points deducted. Probationary status for fourteen days. Any further deviation from protocol results in immediate revocation."

Dale exhaled. He hadn't realized he was holding his breath until his lungs burned.

He wrote the citation number on his clipboard. **88-B-Conditional.**

"Thank you," Dale said.

The Auditor turned. Its suit of shadows rippled.

"Do not thank me, Mr. Kowalski. The debt is not paid. The debt is accrued."

The figure stepped back into the darkness. The footsteps faded. The air grew still.

Dale stood alone in the tunnel.

The red notification in his vision pulsed.

**PROBATION ACTIVE. THOROUGHNESS RATING: 9.1.** **FIELD GUIDE CORRUPTED. RECOVERY REQUIRED.**

He looked at the sealed drain. The cement was cracking.

A small fissure appeared in the center of the patch. Dark liquid oozed out.

Dale raised his shovel. The handle was splintered. The blade was bent.

He looked at the Field Guide interface in his mind. The entry for *Rattus profundus* was grayed out. The text was scrambled.

He needed a new entry. He needed a fresh sample.

He kicked the sealed drain.

The concrete shattered.

Not because he was strong. Because he knew exactly where the stress points were. He had written the report on the structural integrity of this junction. He knew the load-bearing walls were compromised by the acid corrosion from the previous broodmother.

The hole widened.

Something moved in the dark.

It wasn't a rat.

It was too big. Too fast.

A claw, black and chitinous, punched through the hole. It grabbed the edge of the cement. Then another.

Dale stepped back, raising the clipboard.

"Identify," he whispered.

The creature pulled itself out.

It was not a rat. It was not a beetle.

It was a spider. But its legs were made of fused bone and human fingers. Its abdomen was a swollen sac of pulsating gray tissue. It had eight eyes, arranged in two rows, and each eye reflected Dale's flashlight beam with a predatory intelligence.

Dale's heart hammered against his ribs.

This wasn't a Class 1. This wasn't even a Class 3.

This was a Weaver.

He had read about Weavers in the advanced chapters of the manual. They were rare. They were architects. They built nests out of dimensional energy and human fear.

He hadn't encountered one before.

Which meant it wasn't in his Field Guide.

Which meant he had no weakness data.

Which meant he was blind.

The Weaver clicked. A sound like teeth chattering.

Dale looked at the System prompt.

**NEW SPECIMEN DETECTED: ARACHNIDA DIMENSIONALIS (UNCLASSIFIED).** **ACTION: DOCUMENT OR DIE.**

He didn't have time to write. He didn't have time to analyze.

The Weaver lunged.

Dale dodged. The bone-claws swiped through the air where his neck had been.

He swung the broken shovel. It connected with one of the leg-arms.

*Thud.*

Weak. So weak.

The -40% debuff was active. The cross-contamination penalty. He had treated a rat with a trash bag. He had tried to treat a spider with a rat's tools.

The Weaver screeched. It didn't seem hurt. It seemed amused.

It reared back, preparing for a second strike.

Dale scrambled backward, his back hitting the tunnel wall.

He looked at his clipboard. The blank sheet. The pen in his hand.

He had one shot.

If he didn't identify a weakness, he died.

If he identified it, he might live.

But how do you identify a weakness in something you've never seen?

Dale looked at the Weaver's abdomen. The pulsating gray sac.

It wasn't moving with the same rhythm as the rest of the body.

He looked at the legs. The bone-claws.

They were gripping the wet concrete.

He looked at the floor.

The acid. The old acid from the rats.

The Weaver's legs were sizzling slightly. Not from the acid. From the friction.

Dale realized it.

The Weaver wasn't immune to the environment. It was *using* it.

It was building a nest.

The concrete wasn't just a floor. It was the substrate.

The Weaver needed a hard surface to anchor its web.

Dale looked at the puddle of sludge between them.

"Isolation," Dale said.

He didn't attack the spider.

He attacked the floor.

He slammed the broken shovel into the wet concrete, not at the spider, but at the puddle. He kicked a loose chunk of rubble into the sludge.

The Weaver hesitated. Its legs slipped.

The anchor failed.

The System flashed.

**WEAKNESS IDENTIFIED: SUBSTRATE DEPENDENCY.** **TARGETED DAMAGE BONUS APPLIED: +150% AGAINST ANCHOR POINTS.**

Dale didn't hesitate.

He swung the shovel at the Weaver's front legs. The ones trying to grip the wall.

The shovel connected with a crack that sounded like a gun shot.

The Weaver shrieked. Its legs buckled.

It fell into the sludge.

Dale didn't stop.

He couldn't. He was on probation. He was bleeding.

His hands were shaking.

He jumped on the Weaver. He pinned it with his weight. He raised the shovel high.

"Document," he gritted out.

He slammed the shovel down.

Once.

Twice.

Three times.

The Weaver stopped moving.

The gray sac burst. Black ichor sprayed over Dale's face.

He sat there, panting, covered in the guts of a nightmare.

The System notification appeared.

**SPECIMEN ELIMINATED.** **FIELD GUIDE UPDATED: Arachnida Dimensionalis.** **THOROUGHNESS RATING: 9.3.** **POINTS AWARDED: 45.**

Dale wiped the ichor from his eyes.

He looked up.

At the end of the tunnel, where the Auditor had vanished, the darkness was thickening.

But it wasn't just darkness.

It was paper.

Thousands of pages.

They were flying out of the shadows, swirling in a vortex.

And from the center of the vortex, a voice boomed.

It wasn't the Auditor's voice.

It was older. Deeper.

"Mr. Kowalski," the voice said. "You have violated Code 88-B. But you have also violated Code 12-A."

Dale frowned. Code 12-A?

He didn't know Code 12-A.

He hadn't read it.

The vortex of paper coalesced. It formed a shape.

A throne.

And on the throne sat a figure wearing a crown of red stamps.

"I am the Chief Examiner," the figure said. "And you are not just out of compliance."

The figure smiled. It had too many teeth.

"You are the reason the system is failing."

Dale stood up. His legs felt like jelly.

"What do you mean?" he asked.

The Chief Examiner leaned forward.

"We created you, Dale," it said. "We created the System to handle pests. But you... you are treating the System itself as a pest."

The throne dissolved into a swarm of ink-black moths.

They descended on Dale.

"Run," the Chief Examiner whispered.

Dale looked at his clipboard. He looked at the moths.

He picked up his broken shovel.

He didn't run.

He wrote.

**SUBJECT: CHIEF EXAMINER.** **STATUS: THREAT.** **ACTION: EXTERMINATE.**

The moths hit him.

And the chapter ended with the sound of a pen scratching against paper, loud enough to be heard in hell.
