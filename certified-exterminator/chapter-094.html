Chapter 94: Bureaucracy Has Wheels

# Chapter 94

The mud was not solid. It was liquid, viscous, and it moved with a deliberate, predatory sluggishness that defied the laws of viscosity Dale had memorized in his third year of certification.

He ran.

His boots sank two inches into the slurry with every stride. The suction was immediate and aggressive, tugging at his shins like a hungry child pulling at a parent’s pant leg.

Dale didn’t try to sprint.

Sprinting in a swamp was a rookie mistake, the kind that got you stuck on page one of the manual before you even learned how to hold a flashlight. He switched to a wide, stomping gait. *Lift. Place.

Lift.

Place.*

Behind him, the Core rose.

It didn’t roar.

It didn’t scream. It exhaled. A low, subsonic thrum that vibrated in Dale’s molars and rattled the clipboard in his grip.

The entity’s form was a grotesque amalgam of the city’s infrastructure and its biological infestation. Rebar jutted from its shoulders like broken ribs.

Asphalt formed a pliable, black carapace over its torso.

And through the gaps in that carapace, pale, wet flesh pulsed, fused with the soil of the Rift.

*Builder,* it had said.

Dale didn’t look back. Looking back was for heroes. Heroes got eaten. Technicians got to work.

He scanned the perimeter as he ran. The Authority figure was gone. The purple mist had dissipated, leaving the air smelling of ozone and rotting vegetation. The street was empty, save for the abandoned cars and the spreading pool of mud that was now rapidly expanding outward from the Core’s position.

If the mud was expanding, the infestation was spreading.

Dale skidded to a halt near the curb, gasping for air that felt too thick to breathe. His lungs burned.

His heart hammered against his ribs, a frantic rhythm that threatened to disrupt his focus. He forced it down. *Protocol first. Panic later.*

He opened his Field Guide.

The entry for *Dimensional Vermin (Rattus profundus)* was grayed out. The entry for *Skitterer* was flashing red: **CONTAMINATED**.

The warning text hovered in his peripheral vision: *Cross-contamination penalty active.

Target identification degraded. Accuracy reduced by 40%.*

He couldn’t identify the Core.

The guide didn’t have an entry for it. It wasn’t a species.

It was a structure.

A nest.

Dale’s mind raced through the licensing exams he’d studied for. Level IV protocols covered structural infestations.

Level V covered dimensional anchors.

But this?

This was a Level VII anomaly. The Core wasn’t just an animal; it was a localized environmental hazard that behaved like an animal.

He looked at the mud. It was bubbling again. Smaller shapes were emerging from it.

Not rats. Not Skitterers.

They were larval forms.

Translucent, worm-like things made of compressed dirt and bone shards. They writhed in the muck, hundreds of them, detaching themselves from the Core’s body and swimming toward Dale.

He checked his van. It was fifty yards away. Too far.

He checked his belt. Empty.

He checked his pockets. A pen. A flashlight with dying batteries. A roll of duct tape.

The larvae were closing in. They moved fast, slipping through the mud with eerie grace.

Dale looked at the clipboard.

*Entry 405: Unknown Entity. Threat Level: Critical.*

He had written that down. He had documented the threat. But he hadn’t isolated it. And he certainly hadn’t eliminated it.

The Authority’s rules were clear. *Treatment Points are awarded only for completing full extermination protocols: identify, document, isolate, eliminate, and verify.*

He had failed the first three steps because he couldn’t identify it, and the contamination penalty was blocking his ability to analyze the weakness.

A loophole existed.

Dale remembered the fine print in the Level III licensing handbook. *Section 4.2: Structural Containment.* It stated that if a biological entity was physically fused with a non-biological structure, the infestation could be treated as a *structural* defect than a biological one, provided the technician had the appropriate certification for structural remediation.

Dale held a Level II license in Pest Control. He also held a Level I license in Environmental Safety, and did not hold a Level II license in Structural Remediation.

But the rule didn’t say he needed the license to *attempt* the containment; it said the points would be reduced if he wasn’t certified. It didn’t say he would fail.

It said: *Sloppy work earns reduced points but still counts toward certification progress.*

The larvae reached his boots, and they climbed up his pant legs, cold and gritty. Dale kicked them off, swatting at the mud.

He needed a barrier.

He needed to isolate the threat from himself.

He looked around desperately. The street was a disaster zone.

Debris everywhere.

Broken glass.

Twisted metal.

And there, half-buried in the mud near the sidewalk, was a commercial dumpster. Large, steel, industrial. The kind used by the grocery store on 4th Street.

It was heavy. It was empty. And it was right in front of him.

Dale didn’t think. He acted.

He ran to the dumpster, grabbed the side handles, and heaved; it groaned, the wheels grinding against the uneven asphalt. He dragged it fifteen feet, placing it directly between himself and the advancing tide of larvae.

The mud surged against the metal. The larvae hit the dumpster’s wheels and started climbing.

Dale didn’t stop. He ran back to the van. He popped the side door open.

His gear was scattered. He didn’t have a weapon. But he had tools.

He grabbed the industrial-grade sealant gun. It was meant for sealing cracks in foundations against moisture. It was loaded with a polyurethane foam that expanded thirty times its volume when exposed to air.

He grabbed the canister of expanding foam, and he ran back to the dumpster; the larvae were pouring over the top rim. They were in the dumpster.

Dale didn’t spray them. That would be direct engagement with an unidentified entity, risking further contamination penalties.

Instead, he sprayed the *rim*.

He aimed the nozzle at the inner lip of the dumpster. He squeezed the trigger. The thick, orange foam expanded instantly, foaming up and over the edges.

It caught the larvae that were trying to climb out. They were trapped in the viscous mess, their movements slowed, then halted as the foam hardened into a rigid, plastic-like barrier.

The mud continued to push against the dumpster. The Core was still behind it, still generating more larvae. But the flow was restricted. The dumpster acted as a bottleneck.

Dale watched the foam harden. He checked his Field Guide.

The entry for *Structural Containment* flickered. A new line appeared: **Partial Isolation Achieved.**

*Points Awarded: 15.*

It wasn’t much. It counted for something.

The Core roared again.

This time, it was louder. The ground shook violently. The dumpster shifted.

The foam barrier cracked.

Larvae poured through the gaps in the foam.

Dale needed more. He needed to seal the source.

He looked at the Core. It was watching him.

Its purple eyes were fixed on the dumpster.

It didn’t seem angry. It seemed... Curious.

*Builder,* it had said.

Dale had a hunch. A dangerous, career-ending hunch.

The Authority recognized thoroughness. But it also recognized *context*.

The Core was fused with the city’s infrastructure, and the city was the nest.

The city was the host.

If he treated the city as a structure, he could apply structural protocols; he needed to disrupt the connection.

He needed to sever the conduit.

Dale ran to the nearest utility pole. The base was cracked, the wood splintered, and the Rift had warped it, twisting the metal guy-wires into a tangled knot.

He didn’t have bolt cutters. He didn’t have a saw.

He had his clipboard. And he had his knowledge of municipal codes.

He looked at the pole. He looked at the Core. The mud connecting them was thickest at the base of the pole.

The pole was an anchor. A load-bearing point for the dimensional breach.

If he removed the anchor, the structure should collapse. The infestation would lose its foothold.

He ran to the base of the pole. The mud was deep here. He sank to his knees.

The larvae were everywhere. They crawled over his hands, his arms. He ignored them. He focused on the task.

He pulled out his multi-tool. He didn’t try to cut the pole. He couldn’t.

He looked at the guy-wire. It was frayed, hanging loose.

He grabbed the wire. He pulled.

It didn’t move. It was anchored in the earth, but the earth was soft.

He pulled harder. He dug his boots into the mud for leverage.

The wire groaned, and he pulled again.

The wire came loose with a sickening *snap*.

The pole tilted.

The connection to the Core wavered. Mud at the base drained away, pulled into a vortex.

The Core shrieked. It was a sound of pure agony, a frequency that made Dale’s vision blur.

The pole fell.

It crashed into the ground, pulling a chunk of the Rift’s anchor with it.

The effect was immediate. The flow of larvae from the Core slowed. The mud stopped bubbling so violently. The purple light in the entity’s eyes flickered.

Dale scrambled back, wiping mud from his face. His hands were shaking.

He checked his Field Guide.

**Entry 405 Update: Structural Anchor Disrupted. Isolation Improved.**

*Points Awarded: 50.*

It wasn’t a kill. It wasn’t an extermination. But it had bought him time.

The Core was stumbling. Its movements were jerky, uncoordinated. The fusion between the city and the monster was weakening.

But it wasn’t dead.

And now, it was angry.

The purple light intensified.

The mud rose again, not in waves, but in a solid column. It wrapped around the Core, reinforcing it. The entity grew taller.

It grew stronger.

It looked at Dale. It didn’t speak this time. It raised a hand made of rebar and concrete.

Dale had no more tools. No more foam. No more wire.

He had one thing left.

His badge.

The Authority’s badge was a simple piece of plastic, laminated, with his photo and his license number. It was proof of his status. Proof of his authority.

In the old days, before the Rift, badges didn’t do anything. They were just ID.

But the System had changed. The Authority’s rules had changed.

*Section 1.1: Authority of the Licensed Technician.*

*A licensed technician carries the authority of the System. This authority can be invoked to override local environmental conditions, provided the condition is recognized as a public health hazard.*

Dale hadn’t tried this before. He didn’t know if it worked. He had never had to use it in combat.

He held up the badge.

"By the authority of the Municipal Licensing Authority," Dale said, his voice steady despite the terror clawing at his throat. "I hereby declare this area a biohazard zone. Evacuate."

He pointed the badge at the Core.

Nothing happened.

The Core stepped forward. The ground cracked under its weight.

Dale frowned. He tried again. "I declare this infestation a public nuisance. Cessation of activity is mandatory."

The Core lunged.

Dale dodged. He rolled into the mud, coming up on one knee. He raised the badge again.

"Code Violation: Unauthorized Dimensional Breach. Class A."

The badge glowed.

A faint, blue light pulsed from the plastic.

The Core paused. It tilted its head. It appeared to be reading the badge.

The System recognized the declaration. It wasn’t forcing the monster to stop. It was *tagging* the area.

A notification popped up in Dale’s vision: **Zone Flagged. Reinforcement Requested. Estimated Arrival: 12 Minutes.**

Dale’s blood went cold.

Twelve minutes.

The Core could kill him in twelve seconds, and he looked at the van. It was still there. The key was in the ignition.

He looked at the Core. It was preparing for another strike.

He looked at the badge. It was still glowing.

He had called for help. But help wasn’t coming fast enough.

Dale ran for the van, and he didn’t look back; he heard the crash of the asphalt splitting behind him. He felt the wind of the blow as the rebar hand swiped through the air where his head had been.

He threw himself into the driver’s seat. He slammed the door. He locked it.

The van shook as the Core slammed into it. The side panel dented. The window cracked.

Dale turned the key. The engine sputtered.

It caught, and he slammed the gearshift into reverse. The tires spun in the mud.

He floored the gas.

The van lurched backward, fishtailing wildly; the Core reached through the broken window, its clawed hand grasping for the steering wheel.

Dale jerked the wheel to the left. The claw missed him by inches. It punched through the dashboard, embedding itself in the passenger seat.

Dale didn’t try to drive normally. He drove like a man who had spent forty years navigating tight alleys and narrow driveways. He used the momentum, swinging the van around in a tight arc, throwing the Core off balance.

He reversed again, faster this time. The van shot backward, dragging the Core’s arm along with it.

The entity roared, tearing its arm free.

Dale didn’t stop. He kept driving in reverse, watching through the rearview mirror.

The Core stood in the middle of the street, watching him go. It didn’t chase. It appeared to be thinking.

Dale drove until he hit the main road. He turned the wheel hard and accelerated away from the neighborhood.

His hands were slick with sweat. His breath came in ragged gasps.

He checked his Field Guide.

**Entry 405: Core Entity. Status: Active. Threat Level: Critical.**

**Zone Flagged. Reinforcements Inbound.**

He looked at the map. The pin for the infestation was still there. But it was shrinking. The blue circle of the Authority’s jurisdiction was expanding, pushing back the purple haze.

He had survived.

But he hadn’t killed it.

And now, the Authority knew he had failed to exterminate the primary threat.

His Thoroughness Rating would plummet, and he looked at the dashboard. The clock read 11:42 PM.

His phone buzzed.

He pulled it out. It wasn’t a call. It was a text message.

Unknown Number.

*You flagged the zone. Good. But you didn’t finish the job.*

*Dale, look up.*

Dale looked up through the windshield.

The sky above the neighborhood was clear. But the clouds were moving. Fast. Swirling.

They were forming a shape.

A shape that looked like a face.

The Authority figure, which was watching.

And it wasn’t alone.

Behind the cloud-face, other shapes were forming. Dozens of them.

The System hadn’t just sent reinforcements, which had sent inspectors.

And they were coming to audit him.

Dale gripped the steering wheel. His knuckles were white.

He had survived the monster.

But he had just invited the bureaucracy, and he pressed the gas pedal to the floor.

The bureaucracy had a car too.
