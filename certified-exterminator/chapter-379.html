Chapter 379: Auditor Self-Extermination

# Chapter 379

The pen did not touch the paper. It hovered, a millimeter above the grain, suspended by a tremor that ran from Dale's wrist to his shoulder. The tip was dry. The ink had been spent hours ago, or perhaps it had simply dried out in the face of a void.

Dale Kowalski stared at the blank page of the Field Guide. The black cover seemed to breathe, contracting and expanding in rhythm with the wet, chewing sound emanating from inside his own chest.

*Voluntary Sacrifice.*

The protocol had been accepted. The system had acknowledged the input.

But the Authority, in its infinite bureaucratic malice, had not provided an output.

No notification. No chime. No confirmation of processing.

Just the silence.

And the child, and the girl who had been his daughter stood in the center of the library's ruined atrium.

Her eyes were wide, reflecting the gold light that had faded to a bruised purple. She held out her hand, palm up.

A gesture of offering.

Or perhaps a request for a receipt.

"Write me down," she whispered again. The voice was layered, a chorus of a hundred stolen memories trying to find a single throat.

Dale's throat worked. He swallowed, but there was no saliva.

His mouth felt like lined sandpaper, and he looked at the empty pen.

He looked at the girl. He looked at the black book in his other hand.

He dropped the pen.

It clattered against the tile floor, spinning on its axis. The sound was absurdly loud in the quiet.

"No," Dale said.

The word was flat. Procedural. The tone he used when telling a homeowner that their moisture readings were high and they needed a dehumidifier, not a exorcist.

"I don't write the subject," he stated. "I document the vector."

The girl tilted her head. The motion was too smooth, too bird-like. Her neck snapped to the side at an angle that would have broken a human spine.

"But you are the vector, Daddy. You are the nest."

"Then I treat the nest."

Dale opened the Field Guide, and he did not look at the entry for *Dale Kowalski*. He flipped past it, past *Story-Eater*, past *Rattus profundus*, past the dozens of entries that had accrued over three hundred and seventy-eight chapters of extermination. He went to the back.

The addendum pages.

The ones usually reserved for hazardous waste disposal and cross-contamination incident reports.

He found a blank line.

He reached into his pocket and pulled out a stub of graphite. He hadn't written in pencil since third grade, but the Authority allowed it for provisional entries. The system required legibility, not permanence.

He wrote:

*Subject: Self.* *Vector: Memory.* *Action: Containment.*

The book shuddered. The black cover rippled like oil on water. A notification appeared in his peripheral vision, not in gold, but in a sickly, flashing red.

**NOTICE OF NON-COMPLIANCE.** **PROTOCOL ERROR: UNAUTHORIZED SUBMISSION.** **CROSS-CONTAMINATION DETECTED.** **PENALTY: 40% EFFECTIVENESS DEBUFF APPLIED TO ALL ACTIVE ABILITIES.** **FIELD GUIDE LOCKED FOR 24 HOURS.**

Dale didn't blink. He knew the rule. *Cross-contamination penalty: deploying a protocol designed for one creature type against another inflicts a 40% debuff and contaminates your Field Guide entry for 24 hours.*

He had just deployed a *Self-Containment* protocol against a *Story-Eater*. They were technically different species. One was psychological, one was physical. The system had flagged it as a mismatch.

But the girl screamed.

It wasn't a scream of pain, which was the sound of a record skipping. A sharp, digital screech that shattered the remaining glass in the library windows. The shadows in the corners of the room detached themselves from the walls and poured toward Dale.

They looked like static, like television snow given mass.

The girl's form unravelled. Threads of light pulled from her skin, floating into the air like lint. The *Story-Eater* was trying to consume the host. It was trying to eat the man who had agreed to be eaten.

Dale stepped back. His boots scraped against the floor. He needed distance. He needed to isolate the threat.

*Identify.

Document.

Isolate. Eliminate.

Verify.*

He couldn't eliminate. He was the target.

He couldn't verify, and the entry was locked. But he could isolate.

Dale reached for his belt.

Not for a weapon. He didn't have a sword.

He didn't have a gun, and he had a canister of pressurized nitrogen and a nozzle. Standard Class II equipment for sealing cracks in insulation.

He twisted the nozzle. The seal broke with a hiss.

"Protocol: Vapor Barrier," Dale muttered.

He wasn't spraying the girl. He was spraying the floor between them.

A thick, white cloud of cold gas erupted from the nozzle, spreading rapidly across the tiles.

The moisture in the air froze instantly.

The floor became a sheet of ice.

The shadows hesitated. They were physical constructs, drawn by the gravity of the story. The Story-Eater fed on narrative weight, on the emotional resonance of the memory.

But a vapor barrier was a null zone. It was a space where nothing happened.

Where no story could be told because the environment was hostile to life.

The girl tried to step forward. Her foot slipped. She stumbled, her form flickering. The static in the air recoiled from the freezing fog.

Dale kept spraying. He moved in a circle, creating a perimeter. A containment zone.

His breath came in short, sharp puffs.

His fingers were numb. The cold was biting, seeping into the joints of his hands, but his mind was clear.

This was the loophole.

The Authority rewarded thoroughness.

Thoroughness meant understanding the environment. The *Story-Eater* wasn't just a monster; it was a parasite that required a narrative substrate. It needed a story to exist.

It needed a victim to believe in the horror.

Dale refused to believe. He treated the horror as a code violation. He treated his own heart as a leaky pipe.

By refusing to engage emotionally, by refusing to write the weakness, he had denied the parasite its fuel. He had introduced a foreign agent: the cold, dead logic of pest control.

The girl fell to her knees. The threads of light were snapping. Her face remained his daughter's, but the eyes were gone. Just hollow pits where memories used to be.

"Daddy," the voice was fading. "It's cold."

"Contamination risk," Dale said. His voice was steady, though his knees shook. "Do not breach the barrier."

He checked his watch. The Authority's interface hovered in his vision, a translucent overlay.

**STATUS: INFESTATION QUARANTINED.** **THOROUGHNESS RATING: PENDING.** **NOTE: SUBJECT EXHIBITS ABNORMAL BEHAVIOR. REVIEW REQUIRED.**

Review required, which was the threat.

Not the monster. The bureaucracy.

The Authority would send an auditor. A Level IV Inspector. Someone with the authority to revoke licenses.

To erase them.

Dale needed to finish the job. He needed to verify.

He couldn't kill the *Story-Eater*. It was inside him. But he could starve it. He could cut off its supply of story.

He stopped spraying. The fog dissipated, but the ice remained. He walked toward the center of the circle. Toward the girl.

She looked up. Her mouth moved, but no sound came out.

Dale pulled out his clipboard, and he had kept it. He always kept it. The Authority might take his weapon, his life, his memories, but it would never take the clipboard.

It was the primary tool of the trade.

He flipped to a new page.

He didn't write about the monster. He wrote a citation.

**VIOLATION K-12: IMPROPER STORAGE OF BIOLOGICAL MATERIAL.** **LOCATION: HOST BODY.** **CORRECTIVE ACTION: IMMEDIATE REMOVAL VIA INCINERATION.**

He looked at the girl.

"I am not incinerating you," he said softly. "I am incinerating the story."

He closed his eyes. He reached into the void where his wife's face used to be. He found the empty space. The gap in the file.

He didn't fill it with memory. He filled it with data.

He recited the numbers of his first job. The species of the ant.

The concentration of the borax bait.

The temperature of the crawlspace. The humidity levels. The code violations.

The inspection date. The signature of the tenant.

He recited them with perfect precision. He anchored himself in the procedural. He replaced the emotional narrative with a factual one.

The *Story-Eater* shrieked.

The shadows surged forward, desperate for the emotional connection, but they hit the wall of facts and shattered. Facts had no weight. They had no narrative arc. They were sterile.

The girl's form collapsed, and she didn't die.

She dissolved. She turned into paper.

Sheets of white paper fluttered to the ground, covered in his own handwriting. His reports. His citations.

His decades of work.

The paper piled up, forming a mound in the center of the library.

Dale opened his eyes, and the room was silent. The static was gone. The cold was gone.

He looked at the pile of paper.

It was growing. More pages were spilling out of the air, out of the walls, out of his own body. The Authority's punishment was not death.

It was documentation, and he was being written out of existence. Replaced by his own paperwork.

A new notification appeared. This one was not red. It was white. Bright, blinding white.

**CERTIFICATION LEVEL VI UNLOCKED.** **NEW PROTOCOL AVAILABLE: REDACT.** **COST: PERMANENT DELETION OF 10% OF PERSONAL HISTORY.**

Dale stared at the words. *Permanent deletion.*

He looked at his hands. They were translucent.

He could see the floor through his palms, and he was fading. The story was eating him, but slowly. The Authority was mediating the transaction.

He was being traded. Part of him for the license.

Part of his past for the future.

Footsteps echoed in the hall outside. Heavy boots. The sound of a clipboard being tapped against a thigh.

"Dale Kowalski," a voice called out. It was calm. Bored.

An Auditor.

Dale looked at the pile of paper. He looked at his fading hands. He looked at the exit.

If he walked out, the Auditor would see him. The Auditor would see a man who had violated protocol. Who had contaminated his field guide. Who had tried to self-exterminate.

But if he stayed, he would be redacted. Erased.

There was a third option.

Dale picked up a sheet of paper from the pile. It was a receipt from his first job. A simple one. *One can of spray. $12.50.*

He folded it. He put it in his pocket.

Then he picked up the rest of the pile, and he gathered them into his arms. He couldn't leave them.

If he left them, they would become the new story. The new infestation.

He had to carry them.

The door to the library burst open.

Standing in the doorway was a man in a crisp, gray suit. He held a clipboard identical to Dale's. His face was smooth, featureless, like a mannequin that had never known the sun. His eyes were the color of the Authority's gold.

"Mr. Kowalski," the Auditor said. "You have failed to submit your final report for Job ID 378-C. Furthermore, you are in possession of unauthorized biological matter."

The Auditor pointed a gloved finger at the pile of paper in Dale's arms.

"And you are contaminated."

Dale adjusted his grip on the papers. His hands were barely visible now. He was a ghost holding a stack of invoices.

"The matter is documented," Dale said. His voice was thin, like wind through a crack in the window. "I am preparing the disposal manifest."

The Auditor stepped into the room. The air around him distorted. Reality bent to accommodate his presence.

"Disposal requires authorization," the Auditor said. "You are not authorized for Level VI redaction."

"I am submitting a waiver," Dale said.

He pulled a pen from his pocket. It was his old pen.

The one that hadn't worked, and he clicked it. It clicked.

On the Auditor's clipboard, the Auditor's own entry for *Dale Kowalski* glowed.

The Auditor frowned. For the first time, emotion flickered across the blank face. Confusion.

"What are you doing?"

"Cross-contamination," Dale said.

He pressed the pen tip to the air between them. He didn't write on the paper. He wrote on the Auditor's perception.

**SUBJECT: AUDITOR.** **STATUS: INFESTED.** **PROTOCOL: SELF-EXTERMINATION.**

The Auditor's eyes widened. The gold light in them turned black.

The Auditor's clipboard snapped shut.

And then the Auditor bleeded ink.
