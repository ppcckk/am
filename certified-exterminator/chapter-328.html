Chapter 328: The Auditor Writes

# Chapter 328

The clipboard was the only thing holding reality together.

Dale watched the stamp descend through the eyes of a terrified teenager huddled behind an overturned sedan. To the boy, Dale looked like a suicide waiting to happen. A man in stained coveralls standing in the middle of a cratered asphalt street, facing a god made of parchment and ink with nothing but a ballpoint pen and a refusal to blink.

The paper face didn't have eyes. It had text.

Lines of legalese scrolling rapidly across the cheekbones, dense and unreadable, shifting faster than the human eye could track.

The jaw was a serrated fold of vellum. The hands, large enough to crush a city block, gripped a rubber stamp that glowed with a dull, administrative red light.

Miller was screaming. Not words, just a raw, animal shriek of impending doom. He scrambled backward, his boots slipping on broken glass, trying to get back into the car that had already died a thousand mechanical deaths.

Dale didn't move, and he adjusted his grip on the clipboard. His knuckles were white.

The blood from his nose had dried into a crust on his upper lip, tight and uncomfortable.

He ignored it. Protocol stated that visual obstruction compromised documentation accuracy. The blood was peripheral.

The stamp was central.

The giant's brow furrowed.

The text lines on its face slowed.

Confusion? No.

Processing delay.

"Appeal," Dale said. His voice was flat.

Monotone.

The same tone he used when explaining to a homeowner why they couldn't keep their pet ferret in the ventilation shaft.

"Section 4, Paragraph B of the Licensing Authority Bylaws grants the licensee the right to contest a summary judgment if the agent fails to provide prior notice of infestation severity."

The stamp hovered. It didn't slam down. It hesitated.

Dale's heart hammered against his ribs, a frantic drumbeat against the bone.

He kept his breathing steady. In.

Out.

In. Out. If he panicked, the Authority would read his stress levels as non-compliance.

If he panicked, he lost his license. If he lost his license, he died.

The paper giant tilted its head. A sound like tearing cardboard ripped through the air.

Dale clicked his pen, and he wrote on the clipboard.

Not the form.

The back of the form. A scratch note. *Subject: Authority Proxy.

Weakness: Bureaucratic Rigidity. Note: Proxy operates on precedent. No precedent for 'Appeal' in Class-9 Rift events.

Precedent exists in Class-3 Suburban Infestations.*

He held the clipboard up.

The giant read it.

The text on its face scrolled back to the top. It looked at the paper. It looked at Dale.

It looked at the sky.

Then, with a sound like a thunderclap inside a library, the giant vanished.

The red stamp dissolved into mist. The crushing pressure on Dale's chest lifted so abruptly he staggered.

Miller slumped against the car door, gasping for air.

"What... what did you do?"

"I cited a precedent," Dale said. He uncapped his pen and capped it again.

A nervous habit.

"It wasn't an infestation. It was a filing error. The Authority doesn't like errors.

They hate them more than monsters.

"

"They hate *you*," Miller whispered. He pointed a shaking finger at the empty sky. "You just pissed off the management."

"I corrected the record." Dale checked his Field Guide. The screen flickered. The entry for *Authority Proxy* remained, but a new line of text had populated the bottom of the page.

**STATUS: UNDER REVIEW. APPEAL LOGGED. TIME UNTIL RE-JUDICATION: 24 HOURS.**

Dale stared at the timer. Twenty-four hours. That was all he had.

"We need to move," Dale said.

He turned away from the car, scanning the street.

"The proxy didn't attack. It stalled.

That means it's waiting for a supervisor. A supervisor won't hesitate. It will just delete us.

"

Miller struggled to his feet.

"We can't outrun a giant piece of paper, Dale."

"We don't need to outrun it. We need to change the venue." Dale pointed north, toward the industrial district where the old textile mills rotted into the riverbank.

"Procedural law dictates that appeals must be heard in the jurisdiction of the original violation.

We need to file a new violation. A big one. Something that triggers an automatic audit.

"

Miller laughed. It was a broken, jagged sound.

"You want to find a bigger monster to save us from the paperwork?"

"I want to find a nest," Dale said. "And I want to burn it down so thoroughly that the Authority has no choice but to send a human inspector."

Miller grabbed his jacket.

"You're insane. You're actually insane."

"I'm certified," Dale said. He started walking. "Keep up. If you lag behind, I'm marking you as a contaminant."

***

The industrial district smelled of wet rust and dead fish. The streetlights here didn't work. The darkness was thick, viscous, pooling in the alleys between the crumbling brick factories.

Dale walked with his flashlight beam cutting a sharp cone through the gloom.

He scanned the walls. Every surface.

Every crack.

His eyes moved with the trained efficiency of a man who had spent fourteen years looking for the one gap in a baseboard that let the world's filth in.

Miller walked ten feet behind him. He was clutching a tire iron like a holy symbol. He kept looking up, expecting the paper face to reappear.

"Why here?" Miller asked. His voice echoed too loudly.

"Because the plumbing is broken," Dale said. He stopped in front of a storm drain grate that had been pried open. "And because the Authority has a blind spot."

He crouched. His knees popped. He shone the light into the dark pipe.

"Blind spot?" Miller asked.

"Class-9 Rifts are supposed to be contained by high-level protocols. Class-1 vermin are handled by local technicians like me. But what happens when a Class-9 entity spawns Class-1 offspring before the containment field stabilizes?"

Dale reached into the pipe. He didn't touch anything. He just watched.

"The Authority's system assumes the parent is handled first. It logs the parent as 'Active Threat.' It ignores the spawn until the parent is neutralized. But if we kill the spawn without the parent being dead..."

Dale pulled his hand back. He held up a single, black chit. A piece of shell.

"...it's an unsanctioned kill. A procedural violation by the technician.

The Authority has to come down and investigate the discrepancy, and they can't send a proxy.

They have to send an Auditor.

"

Miller stared at him.

"You're going to commit a crime to get help."

"I'm going to create a loophole," Dale corrected. "Auditors have physical forms. They have bodies.

Bodies can be negotiated with. Proxies can only be filed.

"

A chittering sound came from the drain. Not one. Hundreds.

The darkness inside the pipe shifted. Eyes reflected the flashlight beam. Dozens of them. Then hundreds.

"Back up," Dale said. His voice dropped an octave. "Miller. Back up to the car."

"What are they?" Miller asked, backing away.

"Carpenter Ants," Dale said. "But they're chewing through concrete. Dimensional variation."

He opened his Field Guide. The entry for *Dimensional Vermin (Rattus profundus)* was still there. But below it, a new line appeared.

**SUBJECT: ARCHITECTURAL PARASITE. TYPE: FORMICA INFERNUM. STATUS: UNDOCUMENTED.**

Dale's eyes widened. This wasn't in the guide. He had never seen them.

*New species.*

The chittering grew louder. The ground beneath the grate buckled.

Dale didn't panic, and he reached into his belt and pulled out his spray can. It was empty. He pulled out another.

Empty.

He checked his backup.

*Protocol: Chemical Eradication.*

He looked at the chittering mass. It was pouring out of the drain now. Small, black ants the size of house cats, with mandibles that gleamed like obsidian. They were swarming up the sides of the building, eating the mortar, leaving holes in the brickwork that looked like bite marks.

Miller was shaking.

"Dale, there are too many. We can't fight this."

"We don't fight," Dale said. He stepped closer to the swarm.

One of the ants lunged at him. Its jaws snapped inches from his thigh. Dale sidestepped. The ant crashed into the wall, leaving a dent in the metal siding.

Dale watched it. He didn't swing. He didn't kick. He watched the ant's movement pattern.

*Identify.*

The ant turned. It was blind. It navigated by vibration.

*Document.*

Dale pulled out his camera phone, and he snapped a picture of the ant's thorax. The image zoomed. He zoomed further.

On the abdomen, there was a pulsing, translucent sac. Fluid.

*Isolate.*

Dale backed away, and he grabbed a heavy iron chain hanging from a nearby crane hook.

He swung it. Not at the ants.

At the water main valve on the side of the building.

*Clang.*

The valve didn't break. But the vibration did.

The entire swarm froze. The ground shook. The ants turned their heads in unison toward the source of the noise.

Dale pulled his phone from his pocket. He dialed 911.

"Police dispatch," the operator said.

"This is Dale Kowalski, Technician Class II," Dale said. "I am reporting a Class-3 Structural Infestation at the old Miller Textile Mill. The infestation is compromising load-bearing walls. I am initiating emergency containment protocol."

"Sir, this is an emergency line for life-threatening situations," the operator said.

"This is a life-threatening situation," Dale said. "I am also reporting that the infestation is interacting with municipal infrastructure. I am invoking Clause 12: Inter-Agency Cooperation. I require immediate fire department support for chemical dispersal."

The operator paused.

"There is no fire department currently responding to your area. There is a Rift event in progress. All units are engaged."

Dale closed his eyes.

*Loophole.*

The system didn't want to respond to monsters. It wanted to respond to *code violations*.

Fire departments didn't fight monsters, and they fought fires. And structural failures.

Dale looked at the swarm. The ants were beginning to vibrate their legs, a subsonic hum that made Dale's teeth ache. They were preparing to collapse the wall.

He needed to trigger a fire, and he looked at Miller.

"Do you have a lighter?"

Miller fumbled in his pocket. He produced a cheap plastic lighter.

"What are you going to do?"

"Burn it," Dale said. "But not the ants."

He pointed to the leaking gas pipe that had been severed when the valve broke. The smell of sulfur was thick in the air.

"That's natural gas," Dale said.

"It's not a monster. It's a hazard.

The fire department *must* respond to gas leaks.

It's mandatory. It's in the charter.

"

"If you light that," Miller said, his face pale, "we're all going to die."

"If we don't, the Authority proxies will delete us in less than an hour," Dale said. "At least this way, we have witnesses."

Dale took the lighter. He flicked it. The flame sputtered, alive and orange.

He held it out.

"Miller. Run.

Get to the roof of the mill.

The fire escape.

Go."

"And you?"

"I'm going to write the report," Dale said.

He struck the lighter against the metal railing.

The flame caught the gas jet.

The world turned white.

The explosion wasn't a roar. It was a vacuum. The air was sucked into the center of the blast.

The ants were thrown outward, their bodies incinerated before they hit the ground. The brick wall of the mill collapsed inward, a cascade of dust and debris.

Dale was thrown backward. He hit the asphalt hard. His clipboard flew from his hands.

He lay there, ringing in his ears. The heat washed over him, blistering his face. He tasted ash.

Through the smoke, he saw it.

The System interface.

**ALERT: UNAUTHORIZED USE OF OPEN FLAME IN CLASS-3 ZONE.** **ALERT: STRUCTURAL COLLAPSE REPORTED.** **ALERT: INTER-AGENCY COOPERATION TRIGGERED.**

Red text flashed in his peripheral vision.

**AUDITOR INBOUND. ETA: 3 MINUTES.**

Dale coughed, and he sat up. His left arm hung limp. Broken.

Probably.

He ignored it.

He crawled toward his clipboard.

The smoke was clearing. The fire was burning the remaining gas, a controlled burn now that the main had been cut off by the collapse.

And then, he heard the footsteps.

Not the grinding of stones. The click of dress shoes on concrete.

A man walked out of the smoke. He was short. He wore a grey suit. He held a briefcase.

He looked exactly like a middle-aged accountant, and he stopped five feet from Dale. He looked at the burning wall. He looked at the dead ants. He looked at Dale.

He didn't look angry. He looked annoyed. Like a manager who had just found out the break room fridge had been left open.

"Mr.

Kowalski," the Auditor said. His voice was smooth. Professional.

"You have caused significant property damage."

"I followed protocol," Dale said. He spat blood onto the pavement. "You're late."

The Auditor opened his briefcase.

Inside, there were no weapons.

No spells. Just stacks of paper.

Forms.

"We have a problem," the Auditor said. "Your Field Guide is contaminated."

Dale looked down at his screen.

The entry for *Architectural Parasite* was there. But next to it, the entry for *Authority Proxy* was flashing red.

**CONTAMINATION DETECTED. CROSS-CONTAMINATION PENALTY APPLIED.**

**ALL PROTOCOLS FOR VERMINOUS CREATURES DEBUFFED BY 40% FOR 24 HOURS.**

Dale's blood ran cold.

"I didn't use a protocol," Dale said. "I used fire."

"You used a *tool* designated for Class-1 biological containment against a Class-3 dimensional entity," the Auditor said. He pulled a form from his briefcase. "That is a cross-contamination violation. It is a serious breach of licensing standards."

He handed the form to Dale.

"Your license is suspended pending review."

Dale stared at the paper. The signature at the bottom was his own, pre-printed. He hadn't signed it.

"I appeal," Dale said.

The Auditor smiled. It was a thin, cruel smile.

"No," he said. "Appeals are denied when the violation is self-inflicted.

You are no longer certified.

You have no protocols. You have no protection.

"

He pointed to the sky.

The clouds parted again.

The giant face returned. But this time, it wasn't holding a stamp.

It was holding a pen.

And it was beginning to write.

Dale looked at the Auditor.

"If I'm not certified, who protects me from that?"

The Auditor closed his briefcase.

"No one. That is the point."

The pen touched the cloud.

The first word appeared.

**TERMINATE.**
