Chapter 23

The house was three blocks back. Dale ran the way he always ran, which was not fast but efficient, arms pumping at a 90-degree angle, breathing through his nose in four-count cycles. His clipboard was in his left hand. His phone was in his right. The Field Guide entry burned in his peripheral vision like a stuck key on a keyboard.

*Substratum chthonis.* He'd seen the name before. Not in the Authority's system. In a 2003 extension bulletin from Ohio State University's agricultural entomology department. A footnote. A one-paragraph entry about a subterranean brood parasite found in limestone substrate. The bulletin had called it "a curiosity of no economic significance." The bulletin had been wrong.

The Rift hadn't brought this species. The Rift had turned on the lights in a room that had been dark for millions of years.

He hit the porch steps at a jog, caught the railing, and went through the front door without knocking. The house was the same as when he'd left. Same kitchen, same hallway, same open wall cavity where the eggs pulsed their slow, wet rhythm. The neighbor's tenant had been evacuated two chapters ago. Nobody was home. The silence had a texture, like standing in a grain silo.

Dale set the clipboard on the kitchen counter. Opened it. Pulled a fresh form from the clip. Wrote the header.

*PRELIMINARY ORIGIN REPORT. Species: Substratum chthonis. Claimed classification: TERRESTRIAL. Evidence required: physical substrate analysis, frass dating, structural damage chronology.*

He needed three things. Frass that predated the Rift. Damage patterns consistent with long-term subterranean activity. And a substrate sample from the cavity wall that matched the species' known habitat profile.

He had twenty-six hours left. Webb's dimensional classification was already in the Authority's queue. Once the audit locked, the species would be tagged as Rift-adjacent, and the certification requirements would stay at Class IV. Dale would be locked out. The eggs would hatch. The colony would expand. And nobody in the county would be qualified to treat it.

He went to the wall.

The cavity was about four feet wide, two feet deep, running from the baseboard to the header. The eggs occupied the lower third. The upper section was drywall and insulation, intact. But behind the insulation, the original plaster showed a pattern Dale had seen a thousand times in residential inspections. Tunneling. Not the clean, linear channels of dimensional burrowing. The irregular, branching galleries of a colony that had been expanding slowly, seasonally, for years.

He photographed each section. Tagged each photo with a timestamp and a location code. Standard protocol. He'd done this ten thousand times on termite inspections. The Authority's interface recognized the format automatically. His Field Guide pulsed once. A sub-entry appeared.

*Structural evidence: consistent with colony age 8-15 years. Origin classification: PROVISIONAL TERRESTRIAL.*

Provisional. Not confirmed. He needed the frass.

He found it in the lower cavity, behind the eggs. A thin layer of granular material, dark brown, packed into the gap between the plaster and the lath. He scraped a sample into a collection vial with his utility knife. Held it up to the light. The granules were uniform in size, about 0.5mm, with a slight sheen. He'd seen this frass pattern before. In a 2011 inspection report. A house on Elm Street. He'd flagged it as "possible subterranean activity, recommend follow-up." The homeowner had declined. The house had been sold three years later. The new owner had never called.

He photographed the frass. Logged the sample. Filed a second entry.

*Frass sample: consistent with Substratum chthonis. Age estimate: 6-12 years based on desiccation profile. Origin classification: PROVISIONAL TERRESTRIAL.*

Two out of three. He needed the substrate.

The wall cavity ran down to the foundation. He crouched, shone his flashlight into the gap between the baseboard and the concrete. The concrete was old. Pre-1970. There were cracks in it, hairline fractures that had been there for decades. And in those cracks, a fine powder. Limestone dust. The species' known substrate. The bulletin from 2003 had described it exactly. "Calcium carbonate micro-particulate, deposited by mandibular grinding during gallery excavation."

He scraped the powder into a second vial. Photographed the cracks. Logged the entry.

*Substrate sample: calcium carbonate micro-particulate consistent with Substratum chthonis gallery activity. Location: foundation interface. Age: consistent with structure age (1968). Origin classification: CONFIRMED TERRESTRIAL.*

The Authority's interface flared. A new notification.

*ORIGIN CLASSIFICATION: TERRESTRIAL. CERTIFICATION REQUIREMENT: CLASS II (TERRESTRIAL PROTOCOL 7). AUTHORIZED TREATMENT: YES. JOB ASSIGNMENT: PENDING CONFIRMATION.*

Dale let out a breath. He hadn't realized he was holding it.

Then his phone buzzed.

Not the Authority. A text. From a number he didn't recognize. He almost ignored it. Then he saw the name attached to the contact in his phone. Webb had saved himself in Dale's contacts. A courtesy. A professional courtesy. The kind that meant *I'm watching you.*

The text said: *You filed an origin report. I'm filing a counter-objection. See you at the audit.*

Dale stared at the screen. Webb wasn't just disagreeing. He was invoking the adversarial clause. The one that froze both Field Guide entries pending resolution. Which meant Dale's targeted damage bonus was gone. Which meant his identification advantage was gone. Which meant he was fighting a Class IV terrestrial infestation with a Class II toolkit and zero bonuses.

He looked at the eggs. They pulsed. The rhythm had changed. Faster now. Closer together. Something was pressing against the inside of the casings.

He pulled out his toolkit. Class II. Standard terrestrial protocol kit. A bait applicator. A dust applicator. A containment barrier roll. A growth regulator canister. No dimensional suppression tools. No rift-seal compounds. Nothing designed for anything that came through a crack in the sky.

He opened the growth regulator. Read the label. Checked the active ingredient against the Field Guide entry. The species was an obligate brood parasite. It needed a host substrate to develop. The eggs were already attached to the plaster. The larvae, when they hatched, would burrow into the foundation and feed on the limestone. The colony would expand downward. Into the ground. Into whatever was below.

Protocol 7. Terrestrial. The Authority had authorized it. But Protocol 7 required a host substrate removal step. You had to remove the substrate the colony was feeding on. In a standard terrestrial infestation, that meant excavating the affected material. In this case, the substrate was the foundation. The whole foundation. The whole house.

He did the math. The house was a 1968 ranch. Concrete slab. No crawlspace. The colony was in the slab. To follow Protocol 7, he would need to remove the slab. All of it. And the eggs would hatch in six hours. Maybe less.

He checked the Field Guide. The larvae's development window. He'd populated that entry three days ago. Fourteen hours from egg to first instar. First instar meant mobile. Mobile meant dispersal. Dispersal meant the colony was no longer contained.

He looked at his toolkit. Growth regulator. Bait applicator. Dust applicator. Containment barrier.

None of it would stop a dispersal event.

His phone buzzed again. Authority notification this time.

*ADVERSARIAL AUDIT INITIATED. FIELD GUIDE ENTRIES FROZEN. TARGETED DAMAGE BONUS: SUSPENDED. RESOLUTION WINDOW: 6 HOURS. SUBMIT FINAL EVIDENCE PACKAGE OR ACCEPT OPPONENT CLASSIFICATION.*

Six hours. The eggs would hatch in four. Maybe three.

Dale set the growth regulator canister on the counter. Opened the containment barrier roll. Started measuring the wall cavity. His hands were steady. They were always steady. Contamination anxiety meant his hands shook when the workspace was wrong, but the workspace was wrong. The workspace was always wrong. He'd learned to work with shaking hands years ago.

He laid the barrier along the base of the cavity. Pressed it into the plaster with his thumb. Checked the seal. It wasn't airtight. It wasn't meant to be. It was meant to slow dispersal, not stop it. Against a Class IV terrestrial colony, it would last maybe twenty minutes. Maybe less.

He needed to change the protocol. Protocol 7 said remove the substrate. He couldn't remove the substrate. But Protocol 7 also had a sub-clause. He'd read it once, in the manual, on a Tuesday afternoon when the office was empty and the coffee machine was broken. Sub-clause 7.3. "If substrate removal is structurally infeasible, apply growth regulator directly to brood mass and seal cavity. Monitor for secondary dispersal. Report within 48 hours."

It wasn't a treatment. It was a containment. A holding action. It bought time. It didn't solve the problem. The Authority would dock his Thoroughness Rating for it. Probably a 4 or a 5. Maybe lower.

But it would stop the eggs from hatching into an open cavity.

He opened the growth regulator. The nozzle was designed for bait stations. Not for direct application to a brood mass. He'd need to improvise. He pulled the nozzle off. Checked the thread size. Pulled a length of flexible tubing from his kit. Attached it. The fit was tight but not sealed. He'd lose maybe fifteen percent of the active compound to leakage. The Field Guide said the species was sensitive to chitin inhibitors. Fifteen percent loss might still be enough.

Might.

He started applying the regulator to the egg casings. Slow, even passes. The Authority's interface tracked his movements. A progress bar appeared. Not for the treatment. For the audit. A countdown. Five hours, forty-seven minutes.

He was halfway through the lower row when the first egg cracked.

A sound like a knuckle popping. Wet. Small. He froze. The crack spread. A larva pushed through the casing. Pale. Segmented. About four centimeters long. It writhed on the plaster, blind, searching for substrate. Its mandibles opened and closed. Tiny. Needle-thin.

Dale finished the pass on that egg. Moved to the next. His hands didn't shake. His breathing didn't change. He'd done this ten thousand times. Identify. Document. Isolate. Eliminate. Verify.

The larva found the plaster. Burrowed in. The plaster was not the substrate. The larvae would not develop in plaster. They needed limestone. They needed the foundation. They would dig down.

He finished the lower row. Started the upper row. The progress bar ticked. Four hours, twelve minutes.

The second egg cracked. Then the third. Then the fourth. The sound was like popcorn in a microwave. Small pops. Wet. Getting faster.

He applied the regulator to each new egg the moment it appeared. The larvae that emerged from treated eggs went still. Curled. Died within seconds. The ones from untreated eggs kept moving. He counted them as he worked. Seven. Nine. Twelve.

Four hours, three minutes.

He reached the last egg. Applied the regulator. Stepped back. Watched.

The egg pulsed. Once. Twice. Then went still.

The progress bar hit zero.

*AUDIT RESOLUTION: PENDING. EVIDENCE PACKAGE ACCEPTED FOR REVIEW. FIELD GUIDE ENTRIES REMAIN FROZEN. TREATMENT AUTHORIZATION: CONTINGENT. CONDITION: VERIFICATION OF COLONY NEUTRALIZATION WITHIN 24 HOURS.*

Contingent. The Authority was letting him keep the job only if he could prove the colony was dead. The larvae that had already burrowed into the plaster. There were twelve of them. They were not dead. They were digging. And they would reach the foundation in about ninety minutes.

Dale looked at the floor. The concrete slab. Fourteen feet by twenty-two feet. He couldn't remove it. He couldn't drill through it in time. He couldn't inject the regulator into the substrate because he didn't know where the larvae were.

He looked at the Field Guide. Frozen. No targeted damage bonus. No species weaknesses. No protocol guidance beyond the base level.

He looked at his toolkit. Growth regulator. Bait applicator. Dust applicator. Containment barrier.

He looked at the floor.

And then he heard something. Not from the wall. From below. A sound like grinding. Like something chewing. Coming up through the concrete.

He dropped to his knees. Pressed his ear to the floor. The grinding was louder now. Closer. Not one larva. Many. The colony in the wall hadn't been the whole colony. It had been the brood. The workers were already below. And they were coming up.

His phone buzzed. Authority notification.

*NEW INFESTATION DETECTED. LOCATION: SAME. SPECIES: SAME. CLASSIFICATION: CLASS IV TERRESTRIAL. CERTIFICATION REQUIREMENT: CLASS III. AUTHORIZED TREATMENT: NO.*

He was not authorized to treat this. He was not authorized to touch this. The job had just changed. The problem had just gotten bigger. And Webb's counter-objection was still pending. And his Field Guide was still frozen.

The grinding got louder. The concrete at his knee began to crack.

Dale stood up. Walked to the front door. Opened it. Looked at the street.

Every house on the block was the same age. 1968. Same foundation. Same limestone substrate.

He pulled out his phone. Dialed the Authority's dispatch line. It rang twice.

"Kowalski. What's the situation."

"I need a Class III technician on my location. Now."

"Kowalski, you know the queue."

"I know the queue. I also know that there are fourteen houses on this block. Same substrate. Same species. Same colony. And the larvae are in the ground."

Silence. Then: "Define the scope."

Dale looked at the street. Fourteen houses. Fourteen foundations. One colony. He'd been treating the brood. The workers were the problem. And the workers were underground. And they were moving.

"County-wide," he said. "This isn't one job. It's the whole county."

The line went quiet. Not dead. Thinking.

Then the notification came.

*CASE EXPANSION REQUEST: PENDING REVIEW. SUBMIT SCOPE DOCUMENTATION WITHIN 2 HOURS. FAILURE TO SUBMIT: CASE REMAINS LOCAL. LOCAL CASE STATUS: UNAUTHORIZED.*

Two hours to document a county-wide infestation with a frozen Field Guide and a Class II toolkit. Or watch the colony eat the foundations of every house in his service area while the Authority decided whether he was allowed to stop it.

The concrete at his knee cracked wider. Something pale and segmented pushed through. Then another. Then three more.

Dale started running.
