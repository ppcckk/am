Chapter 5: Twenty-Nine Meters

# Chapter 5

The ground in front of Unit A was doing something Dale had never seen in fourteen years of field work. It wasn't settling, which was common after a foundation crack, and wasn't heaving, which was common after a water main break. It was undulating in a slow, rhythmic pattern that made the asphalt flex like a drumhead, and with each cycle, a thin line of pale fluid seeped up through the cracks and pooled in the depressions.

Dale crouched at the edge of the service lane, forty feet from the front door, and wrote: "Surface exudate, color: off-white, viscosity: high, odor: sulfurous with sweet undertone. Suspected biological origin. Recommend collection sample for analysis."

He wrote it because he needed to write it. Because if he didn't write it, he couldn't proceed. Because the protocol required documentation before intervention, and if he intervened without documentation, he'd be no better than the man with the baseball bat who'd already been outside this morning.

The ground pulsed again. A crack widened near the front steps. Something inside the duplex shifted, and the sound that came out was wet and low, like a boot pulled from deep mud.

Dale's hands were steady. His clipboard was steady. His breathing was steady.

He was not ready.

The protocol for a Class 3 unknown required a containment perimeter, a decontamination corridor, and a minimum of two certified operators. He had none of these. He had one empty canister, one page left on his clipboard, and a phone that kept buzzing with texts from a man named Rick who wanted to learn his methods.

He typed: "Bring gloves. Nitrile, double-layer. And a second clipboard."

He hit send. Then he stood up, walked to his truck, and opened the supply box.

He pulled out a roll of 4-mil poly sheeting, a box of nitrile gloves, and a canister of residual spray that was rated for crawling insects only.

He knew it was the wrong tool. He knew it would trigger the cross-contamination penalty if he used it on something that wasn't a crawling insect. But it was the only thing in his truck that came in a pressurized canister, and the protocol required a delivery mechanism.

He laid the poly sheeting on the ground between himself and the front door, taping the edges down with duct tape. This was his decontamination corridor. It was stupid. It was also protocol.

The ground pulsed. The sweet smell got stronger.

His phone buzzed. A new Authority notification:

CONTAMINATION ZONE EXPANDING. CURRENT RADIUS: 12 METERS AND INCREASING. IF RADIUS EXCEEDS 30 METERS, ALL CONTIGUOUS PROPERTIES WILL BE CLASSIFIED AS A SITE. OPERATOR HAS 14 MINUTES TO CONTAIN.

Dale read it twice.

Twelve meters. The front door of Unit A was about eight meters from where he stood.

The duplex across the street was fifteen.

The apartment complex two lots down was twenty-eight.

Fourteen minutes, and he pulled on his gloves. Double-layered, just like he'd asked Rick to bring, though Rick hadn't arrived yet.

He checked the canister.

Half-full.

Enough for a targeted application if he could get close.

He walked the poly corridor to the front door. The undulation was stronger here. The asphalt was visibly soft, like walking on a waterbed. He put his ear against the door.

Something was moving inside. Not one thing. Many things.

A sound like a thousand legs on drywall, layered over a deeper wet shifting that he recognized from experience. It was the sound of a colony relocating.

He wrote: "Acoustic signature consistent with mass relocation behavior. Subject is not stationary. Subject is preparing to disperse. Containment priority: elevated."

The door was locked, and he tried the handle. Locked. He tried the window.

Locked.

He went around the side of the house. The back door was glass.

The glass was intact.

The glass was also, he noticed, covered in a thin film of the same off-white fluid that was seeping from the ground.

He raised his fist to break it and stopped, and the fluid was on the glass. If he broke it, the fluid would aerosolize. He didn't know what it was. Protocol required identification before breach.

He looked at the notification again. Fourteen minutes had become eleven.

He wrote: "Unknown biological exudate present on all accessible surfaces. Breach method: mechanical entry risks aerosolization. Alternative: structural entry via foundation gap. Inspecting perimeter."

He went to the side of the house where the foundation met the ground, and the mortar had failed, leaving a gap roughly two inches wide.

Old house.

Common defect.

He'd written up forty of these this year alone, and he crouched and shone his flashlight into the gap.

The interior was dark. But the dark was moving. Not with the frantic scuttle of rats.

With a slow, deliberate flow. Like something thick and segmented was pouring itself through the space between the foundation and the wall.

He pulled back.

Wrote: "Entry via foundation gap. Subject density: high. Subject morphology: indeterminate from exterior observation.

Recommend visual confirmation before treatment."

He couldn't get visual confirmation without breaching. He couldn't breach without identifying the exudate; was stuck in a loop, and the loop was costing him time.

Nine minutes.

His phone buzzed. Rick's truck pulled into the service lane, white with the Apex logo on the door.

A man got out.

Late twenties, athletic, wearing work boots and a polo shirt with a company patch, and he had a canister in one hand and a clipboard in the other.

"You're Dale?" Rick said. He didn't wait for confirmation. "I've got the canister. What do you need me to do?"

"Stay behind the poly line."

Rick looked at the poly sheeting. Looked at the ground, which was now visibly rippling. Looked at Dale.

"That's not going to stop anything," Rick said.

"It's not designed to stop anything. It's designed to keep my workspace clean."

Rick's face did something complicated. He was trying to figure out if Dale was insane or brilliant, and he couldn't commit to either answer.

"Nine minutes," Dale said. "I need a sample of that fluid without breaking the glass. Do you have a pipette?"

Rick reached into his truck. Came back with a turkey baster.

Dale took it. "Close enough."

He crept to the back door. The fluid on the glass was about a millimeter thick, and he pressed the tip of the turkey baster against the glass and squeezed. Drew a small amount of the off-white liquid into the bulb.

He held it up to the light. It was viscous. It had a faint luminescence.

He looked at his Field Guide. The Authority's interface was still in his peripheral vision, that faint overlay that had become as natural as a heads-up display over the past forty-eight hours.

He opened his Field Guide.

One entry: Rattus profundus.

Dimensional Vermin, and he looked at the sample. Looked at the Field Guide entry. Compared.

The exudate from the rats had been clear and acidic. This was opaque and sweet-smelling. Different chemical signature. Different organism.

But, and he pulled up the Authority's taxonomy interface; the system categorized creatures by kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species. His Field Guide entry for Rattus profundus was classified as: Animalia, Chordata, Mammalia, Rodentia, Muridae, Rattus, R. Profundus.

The thing in Unit A was not a mammal. He was fairly certain of that. The movement pattern, the density, the sound of many legs.

It was arthropod. Maybe.

Probably.

But here was the thing. The Authority had classified this as a "contiguous infestation event." Same event. Same Rift. Same dimensional origin.

He opened the taxonomy interface and tried to file a Field Guide entry for the unknown species. The system prompted him for a classification. He didn't have one. He couldn't identify the phylum without a specimen.

So he did something that felt like cheating, and he filed an addendum to his existing Rattus profundus entry. He wrote: "Addendum: Secondary organism present in same infestation event. Morphology: arthropod, colonial, exudate-producing.

Relationship to primary host: commensal or parasitic.

Taxonomic classification: pending. Provisional designation: Rattus profundus (variant association)."

The Authority processed it.

FIELD GUIDE UPDATE: Rattus profundus entry modified. Secondary species data added as variant association. Cross-reference bonus: +15% targeted damage against organisms sharing infestation vector with documented species.

Dale exhaled.

It wasn't a new species entry.

It wasn't a full identification. But the Authority had accepted the addendum because the system's own rules said that a contiguous infestation event was a site, and a site's Field Guide could contain variant associations. He hadn't identified the creature.

He'd identified its relationship to something he already knew.

The damage bonus wasn't as good as a full entry. It was something. And more importantly, it meant that if he used his Rattus profundus protocol, the Authority would read it as a same-vector treatment than a cross-contamination deployment.

He pulled out the residual spray.

Crawling insect formula.

Not a rodent protocol. But the Authority's interface now showed his Rattus profundus entry with the variant association attached.

The system read the spray as a tool associated with the rodent protocol because he'd been using it on the rats.

He wasn't sure this would hold. But he had four minutes.

He broke the back window with the butt of the canister. The glass shattered outward. The fluid aerosolized. He stepped back, breathing through his collar, watching the mist settle.

The interior was dark. Then it wasn't. The darkness moved.

They came through the window. Not rats.

Something else.

Pale, segmented, maybe eight inches long, with too many legs and a flat body that moved like a cockroach but wrong. Wrong in the proportions. Wrong, because the segments defied any order he recognized.

He sprayed.

The residual formula hit the first wave and they slowed, twitched, curled. Not dead.

Not even close.

But slowed, and he sprayed again. And again. Each burst took a canister's worth and he only had half a canister.

"Rick!" he said. "The front door. I need you to open the front door."

Rick was already moving. He'd grabbed a second canister from his truck and was running toward the front of the house.

Dale sprayed through the window until the canister hissed empty. The creatures inside were still moving. Still coming. But the residual formula was coating the window frame, and the ones that tried to cross it slowed and died on the sill.

The front door opened. Rick stood there with his canister, face pale, staring into the dark interior of Unit A.

"There are hundreds of them," Rick said.

"I know."

"They're all over the walls. The ceiling. The floor."

"I know."

"They're going to come out."

"I know. Spray the doorway. Create a barrier."

Rick sprayed. The formula coated the doorframe.

The creatures that tried to cross it slowed.

Some died.

Most didn't, and the Authority notification came:

TREATMENT IN PROGRESS. CONTAINMENT STATUS: PARTIAL. CONTAMINATION ZONE RADIUS: 27 METERS. TIME TO FULL CONTAINMENT: 3 MINUTES.

Dale looked at his empty canister. Looked at Rick's canister, which was also running low. Looked at the house, which was still full of things that were still alive.

He opened his Field Guide. Looked at the Rattus profundus entry. Looked at the variant association.

He wrote: "Secondary organism behavior: colonial dispersal triggered by host elimination. Primary host (R. profundus) must be located and eliminated to halt secondary organism reproduction."

The Authority processed it.

FIELD GUIDE UPDATE: Behavioral relationship confirmed. Treatment priority updated. Primary target identification: Rattus profundus (broodmother). Secondary organisms will cease reproduction upon primary elimination.

Dale looked at the house. The broodmother. The one from the east side.

The one he'd contained in a trash bag and duct tape, and the one he'd left in the crawlspace because he hadn't had a proper disposal protocol.

He'd left it. He'd written the report and left it. Because the Authority had given him points for containment, not elimination, and he'd been satisfied.

The broodmother was still alive. Still producing. And now it was producing something worse.

He ran toward the crawlspace entrance. Toward the trash bag. Toward the thing he'd left behind because he'd been too thorough to kill it and too sloppy to dispose of it properly.

Rick grabbed his arm. "Dale. The notification says the zone is at twenty-eight meters. The apartment complex is at twenty-nine."

Dale looked at the crawlspace door. Looked at his phone.

Twenty-nine meters, and he kicked the crawlspace door open and dropped into the dark.

The dark smelled like something that had been waiting.
