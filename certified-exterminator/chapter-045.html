Chapter 45: Both Exterminators

# Chapter 45

The foundation was already gone.

Dale crouched at the corner of the split-level on Maple Crescent and ran his gloved hand along the gap where the concrete block should have been. His fingers found only a webbing of gray filaments, dry and papery, like the inside of a dead wasp nest stretched over rebar.

The rebar was pitted.

Not rusted. Pitted. Tiny circular holes, uniform as a drill press.

He pulled his phone out. Opened the Field Guide. Scrolled past his existing entries. Nothing matched.

"Stop poking it." Marisol Vega stood behind him with her arms crossed. Her Conditional License notification floated in the corner of his vision, amber and persistent. "The Authority already logged the structural failure. We're here for the organism, not the damage assessment."

"The damage assessment is the identification." Dale didn't look up. "You can't treat what you can't characterize. And this isn't a feeding pattern I've seen."

"It's a boreworm variant. I ran the ID at the county office before I got your transfer notice."

"You ran it on a Conditional License. That means the Authority logged it as provisional. Provisional IDs don't populate the Field Guide."

Marisol's jaw tightened. "It's a boreworm variant."

"Then show me the frass."

She didn't. She looked at the house. The house looked back, as houses do when something inside is eating the bones, and they hold because the eating isn't finished.

Dale stood. Wiped his glove on his pant leg. Pulled out his clipboard.

"Where's the entry point?"

"Basement. North wall. I showed you the—"

"I need to see it."

They walked the perimeter.

Dale counted three additional exit holes along the rear foundation, each one the size of a fist, each one lined with the same gray filament. He marked them.

He noted the spacing.

Twelve inches between centers. Uniform. Not random.

Not the irregular pattern of a colony expanding outward.

"Twelve inches," he said.

"So?"

"So this isn't a colony.

Colonies don't space their exit holes evenly unless they're following a substrate boundary.

And there's no substrate boundary here. The soil composition is uniform.

I pulled a core sample from the neighbor's yard."

"You pulled a core sample."

"Before I got here. Forty minutes ago. I was already on the way."

Marisol said nothing. She looked at the house with an expression Dale couldn't read. Not guilt. Something more complicated.

He opened his clipboard. Wrote: ENTRY POINTS: 4 CONFIRMED.

SPACING: UNIFORM. 12 INCHES ON CENTER.

NOT CONSISTENT WITH COLONIAL EXPANSION. HYPOTHESIS: STRUCTURAL PARASITE, NOT SOCIAL INSECT. SINGLE ORGANISM.

EXTERNAL FEEDING PATTERN.

He photographed each hole. Logged the time.

Checked the wind direction.

Noted the temperature differential at each opening, using the infrared thermometer clipped to his belt, and the readings were consistent: 11 degrees above ambient at every exit point.

"Thermals are uniform," he said. "That's not a nest.

A nest has a thermal gradient.

Heat at the center, cooling toward the edges, which is a single heat source with equal radiation at four points. That means the organism is at the center of the house, not distributed."

"Then we go in the center."

"We go in the center after I document the thermal profile from all four points. Protocol requires it."

"Protocol requires—" Marisol stopped. Swallowed. "Fine. Do your protocol."

Dale did his protocol.

The Authority's notification appeared as he was logging the fourth thermal reading. Not the usual blue-white text. Something new. Amber, like Marisol's Conditional License.

JOINT PROTOCOL NOTICE: CO-OPERATION WITH CONDITIONALLY LICENSED TECHNICIAN ACTIVATES CONTAMINATION FLAG. ALL FIELD GUIDE ENTRIES FOR CREATURES ENCOUNTERED DURING JOINT OPERATION WILL BE FLAGGED AS CONTAMINATED. CONTAMINATED ENTRIES DO NOT GRANT IDENTIFICATION BONUS. CONTAMINATION DURATION: 24 HOURS POST-OPERATION.

Dale read it. Read it again. Looked at Marisol.

"They're penalizing me for working with you."

"They're penalizing me for existing," she said. "The Conditional License is the penalty. This is just... administrative bleed."

"It's not bleed. It's a design choice. They want me to refuse the assignment. They want a protocol violation on my record."

Marisol didn't argue. She knew he was right.

Dale looked at his clipboard. Looked at the house. Ran the math in his head.

If he identified this as a known species, his Field Guide entry would be contaminated. No bonus.

He'd be fighting blind.

But identifying it as a new species meant there was no existing entry to contaminate. You can't flag something that doesn't exist yet.

The loophole was in the system's own logic, and the Authority could only contaminate an entry that already populated the Field Guide. A new species identification created a fresh, unflagged entry because there was nothing to contaminate.

But a new species identification meant no existing weakness data. No targeted damage bonus. He'd be writing the entry from scratch, in the middle of a live operation, with a creature that was eating the foundation of a house and possibly going to eat the people inside it.

He wrote: SPECIES IDENTIFICATION: PENDING. NEW SPECIES CANDIDATE.

The Authority logged it. No contamination flag. The amber text vanished.

"You're going in blind," Marisol said.

"I'm going in with a clipboard. That's not blind."

He walked to the front door. It was unlocked. The Authority had already flagged the structure as abandoned, which meant no entry permit was required. Dale noted the absence of a permit requirement as a procedural convenience and logged it.

The foyer was normal.

Linoleum floor. A coat rack.

A bowl of decorative stones that had gone gray and brittle.

Dale stepped on one. It crumbled into powder.

He knelt. Touched the powder with his glove. Rubbed it between his fingers.

"Calcium carbonate. Same as the foundation filaments."

He stood. Walked to the hallway.

The walls were bulging.

Not with moisture.

With something solid pushing outward. The drywall was stretched thin over a shape that had the proportions of a ribcage but the surface texture of bark.

Dale pulled out his flashlight. Shone it along the baseboard. Found the trail.

A thin line of gray powder running from the front door to the basement stairwell. A frass trail.

He'd seen frass trails a thousand times.

Different species, same principle. The organism leaves a path. The path tells you where it lives.

He followed it down, and the basement was warm.

Not ambient warm. The kind of warm that means something large is metabolizing.

The gray filaments covered every surface.

They pulsed. Slowly. Like a heartbeat you can only see if you're watching the right surface.

Dale stopped at the bottom of the stairs. Raised his clipboard. Started writing.

"Marisol. I need you to hold the perimeter at the top of the stairs. If this thing breaches the main floor, I need you to collapse the stairwell."

"Collapsing the—"

"Protocol. Isolation. I can't treat a nest if the nest can leave. Hold the stairwell."

Marisol moved to the top of the stairs. She had a containment foam canister. Dale could see the nozzle from below. She knew what to do.

Dale walked forward. The filaments parted around his boots.

Not because they were avoiding him.

Because they were growing around them.

Slowly. He felt a pull at his ankles, like a carpet tugging at socks when walking against the nap.

He reached the center of the basement. The organism was there. Not a creature in any conventional sense.

A mass. Gray and fibrous, the size of a water heater, with four tendril-like extensions reaching into the four walls of the basement.

Each tendril passed through the concrete.

Not through it. Through it. The concrete was intact around the tendril.

The tendril was inside the concrete, and the concrete had grown around it.

Dale stared.

"That's not possible," he said.

Marisol's voice came from the stairs. "What's not possible?"

"The concrete grew around it. The organism didn't bore into the foundation. The foundation was built around the organism."

Silence from the stairs.

"Dale."

"The concrete grew around it.

Which means the organism was here before the foundation was poured. Which means this house was built on top of it. Which means—" He stopped.

Pulled out his phone. Opened the county records app.

He'd downloaded it for work.

"When was this house built?"

"1987."

"And the organism?"

He looked at the mass. The gray filaments.

The calcium carbonate composition. He thought about the uniform spacing of the exit holes. Twelve inches on center.

A pattern. A designed pattern.

He thought about Marisol's story.

That she'd created this infestation to test the Authority. He thought about the Conditional License. He thought about the Authority's response time.

The speed of the transfer.

They had paired them before either finished their previous jobs.

He looked at the mass again.

At how the tendrils entered the concrete. Not from the outside. From the inside.

The concrete was the shell. The organism was the core.

And the shell was new, and the shell was 1987.

The core was older.

"Marisol." His voice was flat. "When you said you created this infestation. What exactly did you do?"

She didn't answer.

"Marisol."

"I filed a report. I flagged a potential infestation site. That's all I did. I flagged a site."

"Where did you get the site coordinates?"

Another silence. Longer this time.

"From the Authority's backlog," she said. "They had an unassigned job. No technician attached. I filed a claim."

"An unassigned job."

"Yes."

"For an infestation that predates the house."

"Yes."

Dale looked at the mass. The mass pulsed. The house was built around it.

The Authority had known about it. The Authority had a job for it.

The Authority had never assigned anyone to it.

And then Marisol filed a claim, and the Authority punished her, and now they were pairing her with Dale, and the Authority was flagging everything Dale did with an amber contamination mark, and the whole system was designed to make Dale refuse the job.

Because if Dale refused, the infestation stayed unassigned. And if the infestation stayed unassigned, it stayed here. Growing.

Eating the bones of the house.

Eating the bones of the block. And the Authority would have a reason to send someone else.

Someone without a clipboard.

Someone who would fight it instead of exterminate it.

Someone who would fail.

And the failure would justify the next tier of response.

Dale wrote on his clipboard: HYPOTHESIS: INFESTATION IS AUTHORITY-MAINTAINED. PURPOSE: CERTIFICATION BOTTLENECK CREATION.

The Authority did not flag this entry, which was worse than if it had.

He looked up. The mass pulsed.

The tendrils pulsed with it.

And in the center of the mass, where the concrete shell was thinnest, Dale saw something.

A shape. Flat and rectangular.

Gray-white. The same color as the Authority's notification text.

He reached toward it.

Marisol's voice cracked like a whip. "Don't touch it."

Dale stopped. His hand hovered six inches from the object.

"That's not part of the organism," she said.

"What is it?"

"It's a license."

Dale stared at the shape. A license. Embedded in the core of an Authority-created infestation. In a house that had been built around it thirty-six years ago.

"Whose license?" he asked.

Marisol came down the stairs. Slowly. Her foam canister in one hand. Her face the color of the walls.

"Mine," she said. "It's my original license. The one they took when they made it Conditional."

Dale looked at the mass. Looked at Marisol. Looked at the license embedded in the core of a creature that had been growing under a suburban house for thirty-six years, maintained by an entity that predates human civilization, and which had decided that Dale's thoroughness was the only thing standing between it and a specific outcome.

He pulled out his spray can. The one he always carried. The one that was useless against dimensional vermin.

He sprayed the license.

The Authority's notification appeared. Blue-white. Normal.

FIELD GUIDE UPDATED. NEW ENTRY: AUTHORITY SEED (LICENSING ARTIFACT). WEAKNESS IDENTIFIED: CHEMICAL DISSOLUTION. TREATMENT PROTOCOL AVAILABLE.

Marisol stared at him. "You just identified the Authority's own tool as a target."

"I identified a contaminant," Dale said. "Protocol requires removal."

The mass pulsed. Faster now. The tendrils tightened. The house groaned.

He flipped to a clean page and dated it.

"Marisol. I need you to make a decision."

"What decision?"

"Are we exterminating this thing, or are we exterminating the thing that put it here?"

The house groaned again. The ceiling cracked. A chunk of drywall fell and hit the floor and broke into gray powder.

Marisol looked at the license in the mass. Looked at Dale. Looked at her own Conditional License notification.

She uncapped the foam canister.

"Both," she said.

Dale wrote it down.
