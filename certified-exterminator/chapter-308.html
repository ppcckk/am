Chapter 308: Filed Away

# Chapter 308

The clipboard was wrong.

Marcus stared at the laminated sheet in his hands, the plastic edges sharp against his palms. He adjusted his hard hat, wiping sweat from his brow with a sleeve that smelled of diesel and stale coffee. The job site was a mess. Not the usual sort of mess found on a municipal reclamation project—broken concrete, rusted rebar, the occasional rat nest—but a mess that defied OSHA regulations and basic geometry.

A hole had opened in the center of the parking lot. Not a sinkhole. A breach.

And standing at the edge of it was a man who looked like he belonged in a hardware store, not a combat zone.

Dale Kowalski.

Marcus watched from the treeline, his heart hammering against his ribs. He was a Foreman, Level 4, with a clean safety record and a mortgage that required him to keep his head down. He wasn't supposed to see this. He wasn't supposed to know about the Rifts, the Licensing Authority, or the things that crawled out when the sky bruised purple.

But he saw it.

The thing rising from the mud was Dale.

Not the Dale who checked smoke detectors. This Dale wore a Class II badge pinned to a shirt that was already torn at the collar. The mud on its boots was moving, swirling like oil. It raised a hand, holding a marker that glowed with a faint, violet hue.

Marcus dropped his clipboard. The sound was too loud in the silence.

The mud-Dale turned. Its face was a perfect copy, down to the receding hairline and the permanent frown of a man who had seen too many unsealed entry points. But the eyes were voids. Black pits where depth should have been.

"Unlicensed entity detected," the mud-Dale said. Its voice sounded like grinding gravel. "Protocol violation: Identity theft."

Marcus tried to run. His boots slipped on the wet asphalt.

He scrambled back, crashing through a chain-link fence that bent like tinfoil.

He didn't look back. He knew what happened to people who interfered with the Authority. They got audited.

They got erased, and he ran toward the portable office, where the foreman's radio sat silent and dead. He grabbed the handset, dialing the emergency frequency for the District Office. No signal. Just static.

In the distance, he heard a sound. Not a scream. A wet tearing noise.

He turned, just for a second.

The mud-Dale was gone.

And Dale Kowalski, the real one, was on his knees in the crater, trying to get his footing in the slurry.

***

Mud sucked at Dale's boots, and he pulled one free. The other stayed stuck. The vibration in his ankles was wrong. It wasn't the tremor of a shifting foundation; it was the pulse of something alive.

He looked at the marker in his hand. The cap was lost. The tip was dry.

The system prompt hovered in the corner of his vision, a translucent blue box that refused to blink.

**HYPOTHESIS CONFIRMED.** **HYPOTHESIS: MIMICRY FLORA USES INK AS A CONDUCTIVE AGENT FOR IDENTIFICATION PROTOCOLS.** **RESULT: TARGET NEUTRALIZED.**

But the target hadn't neutralized. It had duplicated.

Dale stared at the crater. The mud was still churning.

The face he had seen—the one with his own face—was gone, dissolved back into the slurry.

But the threat remained. The chittering in the trees had stopped, but the air felt heavy, pressurized.

"Okay," Dale said aloud. His voice sounded thin. "Okay."

He checked his inventory.

**ITEM: MARKER (BLACK).** **STATUS: DEPLETED.** **NOTES: EFFECTIVE AGAINST CHITINOUS AND BARK-BASED SPECIES. INEFFECTIVE AGAINST LIQUID-BASED PHANTOMS.**

That was the loophole. That was the catch.

The Authority rewarded thoroughness, but thoroughness required materials.

If you ran out of ink, you couldn't document.

If you couldn't document, you couldn't isolate. If you couldn't isolate, you couldn't eliminate.

And he had no ink.

Dale wiped his hands on his trousers. The fabric was damp. He pulled out his Field Guide.

The pages were fluttering in the wind that had picked up. He flipped to the new entry.

**SPECIES: MUD-MIMIC (VARIATION: SELF).** **CLASS: UNCLASSIFIED.** **WEAKNESS: UNKNOWN.**

The guide was blank. The ink from the tree had been consumed by the creature's mimicry, leaving the entry as a void.

"Self-reference," Dale muttered. "It used my face to confuse the identification protocol."

It was a clever trick.

The Authority relied on clear categorization. A monster was a monster. A technician was a technician.

By wearing Dale's face, the creature had become a grey area.

A paradox. And the System hated paradoxes.

It didn't know whether to treat it as a threat or a user.

So it paused, and the pause was the danger.

While the System processed the error, the creature could expand.

Dale scanned the perimeter. The trees were still, but the shadows stretched longer than they should have. The parking lot lights flickered, buzzing with electrical interference.

He needed to document the entity. He needed to define it so the System could classify it. But he couldn't use ink.

He looked at his hands, and they were clean. He always kept them clean. Contamination anxiety.

It was a flaw, but it was also a survival mechanism. You couldn't treat what you couldn't touch, and you couldn't touch what you didn't trust.

He reached into his utility belt.

His fingers brushed the cold metal of his spray canister. Empty.

His fingers brushed the roll of duct tape. Useless against liquid.

His fingers brushed the clipboard, and he pulled it out. The paper was wet. The violation report he had been writing before the Rift opened was ruined, the ink running into illegible smears.

But the back of the clipboard was plastic.

Dale bit his thumb. Hard.

He didn't flinch. He watched the bead of blood well up, dark and red. He smeared it across the back of the clipboard.

**ACTION: BLOOD-INK SUBSTITUTION.**

The System didn't have a rule against it. It only required a marking agent.

He wrote, in shaking, red letters:

**ENTRY: PARADOX ENTITY.** **WEAKNESS: CONSISTENCY.**

It wasn't a weapon. It was a definition.

The mud beneath him shifted. A tendril rose, shaped like a hand. It reached for his leg.

Dale didn't move. He held the clipboard out, the red letters facing the creature.

"I am Dale Kowalski," he said.

"Certified Technician.

Class II. My workspace is standard.

My tools are standard. My identification is standard.

"

The tendril froze.

The System prompt flashed, bright and urgent.

**SYSTEM ALERT:** **IDENTITY CONFLICT DETECTED.** **USER: DALE KOWALSKI (VERIFIED).** **ENTITY: DALE KOWALSKI (UNVERIFIED).** **ACTION REQUIRED: DISAMBIGUATION.**

The creature's face formed again in the mud. It tried to smile.

It looked like Dale trying to smile, which was unsettlingly accurate, but the eyes were still wrong. Too wide. Too focused.

"You're a copy," Dale said. He pointed the marker, still dry, at the mud. "And copies don't have certification numbers."

The creature lunged.

It didn't attack his body. It attacked the clipboard.

The mud wrapped around his wrists, cold and slick. It tried to erase the red letters.

Dale kicked.

His boot connected with the mud-tendril, but it didn't break. It absorbed the impact.

He was sinking, and the mud was rising to his waist.

He couldn't fight it physically. He had to fight it procedurally.

He remembered the rule. *Cross-contamination penalty.*

If he used a protocol designed for one species against another, he took a debuff. But what if he used a protocol designed for *himself*?

He closed his eyes, and he didn't reach for a weapon. He reached for his mental checklist.

**STEP 1: IDENTIFY.** Done. It was a mimic. **STEP 2: DOCUMENT.** Partially. He had written on the clipboard. **STEP 3: ISOLATE.**

He opened his eyes and shouted, "Isolate!"

The System responded.

A blue barrier snapped into existence around the crater. It was faint, flickering, powered by the residual energy of the previous protocol.

The mud-creature slammed into the barrier. The blue light rippled.

Dale gasped. The debuff hit him instantly.

**CROSS-CONTAMINATION WARNING:** **PROTOCOL MISMATCH.** **DEBUFF: 40% EFFICIENCY LOSS.** **FIELD GUIDE CONTAMINATED.**

His movements slowed. His vision blurred. The barrier was holding, but it was eating into his stamina. He could feel the drain, a cold hollowness in his chest.

The creature inside the barrier changed. It lost its shape. It became a swirling vortex of brown liquid, searching for a way out.

Dale watched it. He held the blood-stained clipboard.

The System was confused. It couldn't delete the creature because the creature was wearing his face. It couldn't save it because it was an infestation.

It needed a tiebreaker.

Dale looked at the trees. He looked at the distant treeline where he had seen Marcus the Foreman running away.

He couldn't see Marcus anymore, but he knew someone was watching. Someone with a clipboard. Someone with a job.

He shouted into the empty air.

"I am reporting a secondary infestation!"

The System paused.

**USER COMMAND?**

Dale pointed at the mud-creature.

"This is not a single entity.

This is a brood, and the parent is not here.

The offspring is using the parent's template for camouflage.

"

It was a lie. A procedural lie.

The Authority loved lies if they were structured correctly. If they followed the format of a report.

The System processed the new data.

**RE-EVALUATION:** **TARGET RE-CLASSIFIED: CLASS 2 VERMIN (LARVAL STAGE).** **PARENT NOT PRESENT.** **ACTION: STERILIZATION PROTOCOL.**

The blue barrier tightened. The mud inside dried out rapidly. The creature shrieked, a sound like tearing paper, as the moisture was sucked out of it.

Dale felt the pressure on his chest ease. The debuff faded as the classification shifted.

He sank to his knees in the mud, which was now just mud.

He checked his Field Guide.

**ENTRY: MUD-MIMIC (VARIATION: SELF).** **STATUS: ELIMINATED.** **THOROUGHNESS RATING: 9.5.** **PENALTY: FALSE REPORTING (-0.3).**

He had lied to the Authority. He had called a Class 1 threat a Class 2 larva. He had avoided a fight he couldn't win by changing the paperwork.

It worked.

But the penalty stung.

Dale wiped the blood from his thumb. He stood up, his legs shaking. He retrieved his clipboard. The red letters were smeared, but still legible.

He turned to leave, and the parking lot was silent. The Rift had closed. The sky was clear, a pale, innocent blue.

But on the ground, where the crater had been, there was a single object.

A badge.

A Class III badge.

It lay in the dried mud, gleaming in the sunlight.

Dale stared at it. He hadn't earned a Class III badge. He had taken the exam for Class II three weeks ago. He hadn't taken the Class III practical.

He picked it up, and the metal was warm.

On the back, scratched into the steel, were words.

**NEXT EXAM: TOMORROW. LOCATION: THE SUBURBS.**

Dale dropped the badge. It clattered against the asphalt.

He looked at his hands. They were trembling.

He looked at the trees. The Mimicry Flora was gone, but the shadows were still deep.

And then, a new notification appeared. Not in his peripheral vision. Directly in front of his eyes, large and bold.

**NOTICE OF COMPLIANCE AUDIT.** **SUBJECT: DALE KOWALSKI.** **ISSUE: UNAUTHORIZED PROMOTION.** **STATUS: SUSPENDED.**

Dale froze.

Suspension.

It wasn't a fight. It was a bureaucratic execution.

If he was suspended, he lost his license. If he lost his license, he couldn't use protocols. He couldn't use the spray. He couldn't use the barrier.

He was just a guy in a shirt with a hole in it.

And the things in the ground were still hungry, and he heard a sound behind him.

Not a scream. Not a chitter.

The sound of a car engine starting.

Dale turned slowly, and a white truck was pulling into the parking lot. It wasn't a municipal vehicle. It was a sedan, black, with tinted windows. The license plate was obscured by a smear of ink.

The door opened.

A man stepped out. He wore a suit. He held a briefcase. He looked at Dale with eyes that were entirely white, no iris, no pupil.

"Dale Kowalski," the man said. His voice was smooth, like polished stone. "You have a lot of explaining to do."

Dale looked at the Class III badge on the ground. He looked at the man. He looked at his empty spray canister.

"I didn't take the exam," Dale said. His voice was steady. "But I did the work."

The man smiled. It was a thin, predatory expression.

"The Authority doesn't care about the work, Dale. It cares about the paperwork."

The man reached into his briefcase, and he didn't pull out a weapon.

He pulled out a pen.

And a red stamp.

Dale backed away.

"You can't stamp me," Dale said. "I'm a Certified Technician."

"Not anymore," the man said.

The stamp came down.

**REJECTED.**

The world went dark.

Not black. Dark.

A void.

Dale felt himself being pulled, not by gravity, but by bureaucracy.

He was being filed away.

And somewhere in the dark, he heard the chittering start again.

But this time, it was coming from inside his own head.

The voice in his head whispered a new file number.
