Chapter 172: Tomorrow's Death Certificate

# Chapter 172

Dale’s cheap ballpoint trembled in his grip, ink drying on the line. Not the Agent's pen. The Agent had lowered his hand, confused by the request for credentials. But Dale's own pen—the cheap ballpoint he used for field notes—was trembling in his grip. The ink hadn't dried on the line that declared him an infestation.

Around them, the air rippled. Static electricity snapped against the wet asphalt. The other agents in their white suits shifted, their clipboards glowing with red error messages.

"Credentials," Dale repeated. His voice sounded flat, scraped raw.

He didn't look at the Agent, and he looked at the pen in his own hand. It was vibrating.

The Agent tilted his head. His parchment skin stretched too tight over cheekbones that shouldn't exist in three dimensions.

"You are requesting verification of a Class IV Provisional license?"

"I'm requesting verification of the authority to issue a removal order," Dale said.

He didn't wait for an answer. Dale dropped to one knee. The movement was sudden, violent. He slammed his clipboard against the wet pavement, angling it toward the nearest crack in the earth where green, viscous sludge was oozing upward.

*Protocol 4-Alpha: Containment of Dimensional Fluids.*

The sludge hissed.

It tried to rise, forming tendrils that reached for the agents' ankles. Dale didn't attack them.

He didn't strike, and he opened his Field Guide app on his phone, the screen cracking under the strain of the sudden drop.

"Log entry," Dale muttered into his recorder. "Substrate instability detected. High-viscosity dimensional runoff. Immediate isolation required to prevent cross-contamination of Audit Team."

The red glow on the agents' clipboards flickered.

*System Alert: Environmental Hazard Detected.*

The Agent stepped back. The other agents froze. They were bureaucratic entities, bound by rigid code. They could not proceed with an execution while an active, uncontained biohazard posed a threat to the audit team's integrity.

"Seal the perimeter," Dale commanded. It wasn't a request. It was an order, delivered with the tone of a foreman who had seen a hundred foremen fail to clean up a spill.

One of the junior agents, a man with eyes like polished obsidian, hesitated. He looked at the Agent. The Agent looked at Dale.

"If the containment fails," Dale said, pointing his pen at the oozing crack, "the paradox propagates. You want a clean audit, Agent?

You want to eliminate me?

Then you need the workspace sterile.

You know the rules. Cross-contamination voids the certification.

"

The Agent's face remained impassive, but the air around him grew colder. Frost formed on his clipboards.

"Fine," the Agent said. "Contain it. You have three minutes before the audit resumes."

Three minutes.

Dale stood up. His knees popped. The pain was sharp, a reminder that he was flesh and bone, not just a data point.

He looked at the line of men. They were waiting.

They were expecting him to run, to fight, to beg.

Instead, Dale pulled a roll of duct tape from his belt, which was standard issue. Silver. Industrial grade.

Useless against monsters, theoretically.

But Dale Kowalski had been sealing entry points for fourteen years. He knew that a monster was just a variable, and a variable needed a constant environment to be measured.

He moved toward the crack. The sludge bubbled, trying to climb the concrete lip. It smelled of sulfur and rotting lilies.

Dale ignored the smell, and he focused on the geometry of the breach. The crack was jagged, widening by fractions of an inch every second. If he sprayed it, the acid would eat the tape.

If he hit it with force, it would split further.

He needed a physical barrier.

He reached into his utility bag.

No weapons.

No swords. He grabbed a sheet of heavy-duty polyethylene, the kind used for painting rooms.

He unfolded it with practiced speed.

"Watch the edges," Dale said to no one. He spoke to his hands. They were steady.

The Agent watched, his pen still raised but not striking.

"You are delaying the inevitable, Kowalski. The system recognizes your self-identification. You are the infestation."

"Infestations are local," Dale said. He slapped the plastic down over the crack.

It adhered with a wet *smack*.

"Paradoxes are global. You can't audit a global variable in a local frame without causing a stack overflow."

He pulled out a can of industrial adhesive. Not poison. Glue. Thick, yellow, viscous stuff.

He squirted it along the rim of the plastic sheet.

*System Prompt: Non-Lethal Intervention Detected. Points: 0. Thoroughness Bonus: +5%.*

The Agent frowned.

"Zero points?"

"Efficiency," Dale said. He pressed the plastic down, smoothing it out with his palms. The sludge beneath it writhed, trying to dissolve the adhesive. The plastic bulged.

Dale grabbed the duct tape, and he tore long strips. He applied them across the plastic, crisscrossing the edges.

Over.

Under. Over. Under.

The sound of tearing tape was loud in the silent street. *Riiip. Riiip. Riiip.*

It was the sound of mundane labor. It was the sound of a man who did not care about fate, but cared about leaving a job site clean.

The crack stopped widening. The plastic held. The sludge churned beneath it, trapped.

Dale stepped back. He wiped his hands on his pants.

"Perimeter secured. Workspace is sterile."

The Agent lowered his pen. The red glow on his clipboard turned to a neutral amber.

"Auditing protocols are suspended," the Agent said. "Pending environmental clearance."

"Good," Dale said. He turned to the junior agents.

"You.

The one with the clipboard.

Take the measurement. Log the containment status.

Timestamp it."

The junior agent blinked.

"I... I am an Auditor. Not a technician."

"You're holding a clipboard," Dale said. "You're taking notes. Do the work."

The agent obeyed. It was instinct. Bureaucracy was a reflex. He wrote.

Dale walked toward the center of the street. The heat was still oppressive.

His shirt was soaked, and the fear was there, a cold knot in his stomach, but he buried it under the procedure. If he stopped working, he would think. If he thought, he would realize that he had just named himself a monster.

He had written *Dale Kowalski* on the line for *Species*.

That was the loophole.

The System required identification. *Identify.

Document. Isolate.

Eliminate.

Verify.*

By identifying himself, Dale had entered the loop. He had become both the exterminator and the pest.

The System couldn't process the contradiction, which had thrown an error. *Status: Paradox Class.

Action: Eliminate.*

But Dale had forced the System to prioritize *Isolation*. He had isolated the paradox (himself) from the environment (the street). Now, the System had to decide: did it eliminate the containment, or the contained?

If it eliminated the containment, the street was compromised. The audit failed.

If it eliminated the contained, Dale died, which was a stalemate. For now.

Dale pulled out his Field Guide. The entry for *Paradox Vermin* was blank, except for the name. He tapped the screen.

"Update," he whispered.

*Field Guide Entry: Dale Kowalski.* *Weakness: None.* *Resistance: High.* *Behavior: Procedural. Predictable.*

He closed the app.

"Agent," Dale said. "The audit can resume. But I require a witness for the final verification."

The Agent stepped forward. The frost on his clipboard melted.

"You think this changes anything?

You are an anomaly. Anomalies are purged.

"

"I'm not an anomaly," Dale said. "I'm a compliance failure. And compliance failures don't get purged. They get retrained."

The Agent paused. The word *retrained* hung in the air.

The other agents whispered among themselves. Their voices were like dry leaves scraping together.

"Retraining," the Agent said slowly. "Yes. There is a protocol for that. Class I Remediation."

"Then do it," Dale said. "Write the citation. Give me the training. Let me prove I'm not the infestation."

The Agent raised his pen. He didn't write. He pointed it at Dale's chest.

"The Authority does not retrain those who have named themselves," the Agent said.

"It erases them. It is a mercy. You are a mirror, Kowalski.

And mirrors shatter.

"

Dale looked at the pen. He looked at the street. The plastic over the crack was starting to tear. A single tendril of green sludge pushed through, seeking air.

Dale's eye twitched, and he didn't look at the Agent. He looked at the sludge.

*Protocol 9-Delta: Escalation of Minor Breach.*

He didn't have the protocol. He was Level IV. He didn't have the clearance.

But he had the clipboard.

Dale grabbed the clipboard from the junior agent. He tore the top sheet off. He held it up.

"I am invoking the Right of Observation," Dale said.

The Agent froze. The other agents stopped whispering.

"That is a Class VII right," the Agent said. "You do not possess it."

"I am observing the infestation," Dale said. "The infestation is the street.

The street is the container for the anomaly, and the anomaly is me. If you kill me, you fail to observe the source. If you observe the source, you must document it.

If you document it, you must verify it.

"

He pointed the clipboard at the Agent's face.

"You want to verify me?

Fine.

But you have to fill out the form. All of it.

Species, behavior, habitat, vectors of transmission.

"

The Agent's eyes narrowed.

"And if I do?"

"Then you have to sign it," Dale said. "And if you sign it, you acknowledge that I am a valid entry in the Field Guide. And valid entries aren't erased. They're managed."

The Agent looked at the clipboard. He looked at the torn paper.

It was a stupid trick. A clerical error. A loop-hole born from pedantry.

But the Agent was a creature of rules. And rules were literal.

The Agent's pen hovered. He looked at Dale. For the first time, there was something other than pity in his expression. There was calculation.

"You are playing a dangerous game, Kowalski."

"I'm playing the job," Dale said. "Write the citation."

The Agent did not move.

Instead, the ground beneath Dale's feet gave way.

Not the crack. The solid pavement.

The street sagged. A sinkhole opened up, not green, but black. Pure, void black.

Dale stumbled. He grabbed the junior agent for balance. The agent didn't help. He just watched, his clipboard clattering to the ground.

Dale fell, and he hit the edge of the sinkhole. His feet dangled over the void.

The cold rising from below was absolute.

It wasn't cold like ice. It was cold like absence.

Dale gripped the lip of the pavement. His fingernails cracked.

"Agent!" Dale shouted.

The Agent looked down. He didn't reach out.

"Anomaly detected," the Agent said. His voice was calm. "Source: Unstable Substrate. Recommendation: Immediate Purge."

The other agents raised their pens.

Dale looked up. He saw the blackness below. And in the blackness, he saw something.

Not a monster. Not a rat.

He saw a clipboard.

Huge. Towering. Made of bone.

And on the clipboard, a single sheet of paper.

Dale squinted. The light from the streetlamps reflected off the paper.

He could read it.

*Subject: Dale Kowalski.* *Status: Terminated.* *Date: Tomorrow.*

Dale's breath hitched.

It wasn't a prediction, which was a receipt.

"Agent," Dale said, his voice small. "Why is my death certificate dated tomorrow?"

The Agent smiled. It was the same smile. Infinite pity.

"Because we haven't finished the paperwork yet," the Agent said. "And you, Technician Kowalski, are very bad at paperwork."

The ground beneath Dale's hands crumbled, and he fell.

He landed on the paper.
