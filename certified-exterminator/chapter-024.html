Chapter 24

Dale ran the block in ninety seconds. He counted emergence points as he went. Fourteen houses, same 1968 foundation, same limestone substrate, same crack pattern in the driveway apron where the concrete had heaved upward by roughly two inches. He'd seen this before. Not the larvae. The pattern. He'd seen this pattern in the driveway of a 1972 ranch in Westerville where a grub infestation had pushed a four-inch slab off its footing.

He stopped at the corner. Pulled out his phone. Opened the Authority's submission portal. The scope documentation form loaded with its usual wall of required fields: species identification, colony extent, substrate type, structural risk assessment, treatment window. Every field that required a Field Guide entry was greyed out. Frozen. The cross-contamination penalty still had twenty-three hours on the clock.

He stared at the form. The larvae behind him were pulling themselves out of the ground with a wet tearing sound, each one about six inches long, pale as a maggot, segmented like a centipede but thicker. They moved in a direction. All of them, the ones emerging from the cracks in the street and the ones pushing up through the front lawns, moved in the same direction. East. Toward the creek.

Dale watched them for ten seconds. Then he pulled out his clipboard. The one he'd borrowed from the neighbor three chapters ago, still in his back pocket, still dog-eared at the corner where he'd folded it to fit.

He started writing. Not in the Authority's form. In his own notes. The way he'd been doing it for fourteen years. Species: unknown. Morphology: coleopteran larval form, scaraboid. Size: 150 to 180 millimeters. Coloration: unpigmented, suggesting subterranean lifestyle with no surface exposure. Movement pattern: directional, unidirectional, suggests foraging behavior rather than random emergence. Substrate preference: limestone, high calcium content, consistent with calcium-dependent exoskeletal development.

He looked at the larvae again. Counted them. Twenty-three visible. He extrapolated. The emergence points were distributed evenly along the block, roughly one per six feet of street frontage. If the pattern held, and it would, it always did, the colony extended the full length of the block and probably beyond. He checked the far end of the street. Two more houses past the fourteen. Same foundation. Same limestone.

Sixteen houses. Maybe more.

He wrote it down. Then he looked at the Authority's form again. The species identification field was still greyed out. He couldn't enter a species name because the Field Guide wouldn't let him. But the form had a second option, one he'd never clicked because he'd never needed it. A small link at the bottom of the field that read: PROVISIONAL ENTRY (UNVERIFIED).

He clicked it.

A new form appeared. Simpler. Shorter. It asked for: observed morphology, behavioral notes, substrate correlation, and confidence level. No species name required. No Field Guide entry required. Just observations. Just what a technician could see with their own eyes and write down with their own hands.

Dale filled it in. Morphology: scaraboid larval form, six-legged, segmented, unpigmented. Behavior: directional foraging, unidirectional movement toward calcium-rich substrate. Substrate correlation: 100% association with 1968 limestone foundations. Confidence level: he set it at 70%. Not high enough for a treatment protocol. High enough for scope documentation.

He hit submit.

The response came in four seconds.

*PROVISIONAL ENTRY ACCEPTED. SCOPE DOCUMENTATION: IN PROGRESS. REMAINING TIME: 1 HOUR 47 MINUTES.*

He went back to the street. The larvae were still emerging. More of them now. He counted forty-one. They were moving faster than before. The first ones had reached the curb and were turning east, toward the creek bed that ran behind the subdivision. He watched one reach the edge of the street and drop into the storm drain. Gone. He watched three more follow.

He wrote that down too. Drain access. The colony had a route to the water table. That changed the scope. If the larvae reached the groundwater, they'd spread beyond the county. The limestone substrate extended for miles along the creek bed. He could see it in the retaining walls along the drainage channel. Same rock. Same age.

He added it to his notes. Then he sat down on the curb, opened the Authority's form again, and filled in the structural risk assessment. This was the part that mattered. This was the part that would determine whether the Authority sent a technician or a bulldozer.

He wrote: Sixteen residential structures. Foundation type: continuous limestone footing, 1968 construction, approximately 18 inches below grade. Larval emergence concentrated at footing junctions, indicating direct consumption of substrate material. Structural compromise estimated at 15 to 20 percent across affected structures. Progression rate: accelerating. Estimated time to critical failure: 48 to 72 hours.

He knew the numbers. Not from the System. From experience. He'd written structural risk assessments for foundations that termites had hollowed out, for basements that water damage had softened, for crawl spaces where the joists had rotted past the point of no return. The math was the same. Damage percentage, progression rate, time to failure. The only variable was the organism.

He submitted the structural assessment.

*STRUCTURAL RISK ASSESSMENT: ACCEPTED. CASE UPGRADED. NEW VIOLATION CLASS: STRUCTURAL COMPROMISE. RESPONSE PROTOCOL: PENDING.*

Then a second notification.

*CO-LEAD ASSIGNED: CLASS IV TECHNICIAN. ETA: 12 MINUTES.*

Dale looked at the street. Looked at the larvae. Looked at his clipboard.

Twelve minutes. He had twelve minutes to figure out what was coming and whether it would help or hurt.

He stood up. Walked to the nearest house. Knelt down at the foundation. Pressed his palm flat against the limestone. Felt the vibration. Faint, rhythmic, like a pulse. He moved to the next house. Same vibration. The next. Same. The pattern was consistent across the entire block. The colony was active. Moving. Building something under the foundations.

He pulled out his phone. Called dispatch.

"Kowalski. You submitted scope documentation."

"I did."

"The Authority has reviewed it. Case status is now ACTIVE. You are authorized to treat."

"I know. Who's the co-lead?"

"Class IV. Webb."

Dale's hand tightened on the phone. "Webb is the one who filed the counter-objection."

"Correct."

"On what grounds?"

"Scope overreach. He filed a competing case file for the same block. He's been running a Class IV treatment protocol on this species for three weeks. He says your colony estimate is wrong."

Dale looked at the ground. The larvae were still emerging. Still moving east. Still dropping into the storm drains. "My estimate is right."

"He disagrees."

"Webb's protocol is wrong."

"Kowalski, Webb is Class IV. You're Class II. The Authority has assigned him as co-lead. That means he has override authority on treatment decisions."

Dale closed his eyes. Opened them. Looked at the storm drain where the larvae were disappearing. "He's treating the workers."

"He's treating the colony."

"He's treating the workers as the colony. There's a difference."

The line went quiet. Then: "You have twelve minutes. Submit your treatment plan or defer to Webb."

Dale hung up. He looked at his clipboard. At the provisional entry. At the structural assessment. At the notes he'd written in his own handwriting, in his own shorthand, with his own fourteen years of pattern recognition.

He started writing a treatment plan. Not the Authority's form. His own. The way he'd done it for every job, every inspection, every code violation. Identify. Document. Isolate. Eliminate. Verify. The five steps. The protocol. The thing that made the difference between a job done and a job done right.

He was halfway through the isolation step when he heard the truck. Not a regular truck. The Authority's response vehicle. White, unmarked, with the Authority's seal on the door. It pulled up to the curb two houses down. The door opened. Webb stepped out.

Webb was younger than Dale. Mid-thirties. Wore the Authority's field kit, the full Class IV loadout. Dale could see the protocol holsters on his belt. Could see the Field Guide interface glowing on his forearm. Could see the treatment tools racked across his back. Everything Dale didn't have. Everything Dale couldn't use right now.

Webb looked at the street. Looked at the larvae. Looked at Dale sitting on the curb with a clipboard.

"You're the one who filed the scope?"

"I am."

Webb crouched down. Picked up one of the larvae. It twisted in his hand. He held it up to his Field Guide interface. The interface flickered. A readout appeared. Webb read it. His expression changed. Not much. A tightening around the eyes.

"This isn't the species I've been treating," he said.

"No."

"My Field Guide says this is a different entry. A different species entirely."

"I know."

Webb stood up. Looked at the ground. Looked at the storm drains. "How long have you known?"

"Since the cross-contamination penalty hit. The protocol I used was wrong. That means the species was wrong. I figured it out from the substrate correlation."

Webb was quiet for a moment. Then he pulled out his own Field Guide. Scrolled through it. His finger stopped on an entry. He turned the interface toward Dale.

The entry was greyed out. Not frozen. Greyed. Like it had been there a long time and nobody had updated it. The species name was there. The morphology was there. The substrate correlation was there. And below it, in the treatment history section, was a single line.

PREVIOUS TREATMENT ATTEMPT: 1983. TECHNICIAN: M. HARGROVE. STATUS: UNRESOLVED.

Dale stared at the entry. "Who's Hargrove?"

Webb didn't answer. He was looking at the ground. At the vibration he could feel through the soles of his boots. At the larvae that were still moving east. Still dropping into the drains.

"He was Class IV," Webb said. "Best technician the county had. Treated this exact block. Same species. Same substrate. Same year the houses were built."

"What happened?"

Webb looked at him. "The Authority's file says he completed the protocol. All five steps. Identified, documented, isolated, eliminated, verified. Thoroughness rating: 9.8."

"That's near perfect."

"It is."

"So what's unresolved about it?"

Webb pointed at the ground. At the larvae. At the vibration. "Because he eliminated them. And they came back. Same species. Same block. Same substrate. Forty years later. And the Authority's file has no explanation for why."

Dale looked at the ground. At the storm drain. At the larvae disappearing into the water table. At the limestone that stretched for miles along the creek bed.

He thought about the five steps. Identify. Document. Isolate. Eliminate. Verify.

He thought about what verify meant. Not just checking that the pests were dead. Checking that the conditions that allowed them to return were also addressed. In pest control, you didn't just kill the colony. You fixed the entry point. You removed the food source. You changed the environment so the species couldn't re-establish.

Hargrove had done all five steps. Had gotten a 9.8. And the colony was back.

Which meant Hargrove had missed something. Something the protocol didn't cover. Something that let the species return despite a near-perfect treatment.

Dale looked at the entry again. At the treatment history. At the date. 1983. He looked at the houses. 1968. Fifteen years between construction and the first infestation.

He looked at the larvae. At the direction they were moving. East. Toward the creek. Toward the water table. Toward the limestone that ran for miles.

And he realized something that made his stomach drop.

The colony wasn't coming back. It had never left. Hargrove had treated the surface. The workers. The visible population. But the colony itself was deeper. In the limestone. In the water table. In a place no surface protocol could reach.

And now it was growing. And it was moving. And in forty-eight to seventy-two hours, it would reach the water table and spread beyond the county.

Webb was already setting up his treatment tools. He was going to fumigate. Dale could see it in the way he was laying out the canisters. A Class IV fumigation protocol. Would kill everything in the soil within a fifty-foot radius. Would kill the larvae. Would kill the workers. Would kill every organism in the top three feet of soil.

It wouldn't kill the colony. It would just drive it deeper.

Dale opened his mouth to say something. Webb looked up.

"Stand back, Kowalski. This is a Class IV protocol. You're not certified for it."

"Webb. The colony is below the fumigation depth. You're going to drive it deeper."

Webb's hands stopped on the canister rack. He looked at Dale. Looked at the ground. Looked at the storm drains.

He didn't say anything. But he didn't start the fumigation either.

And then the ground under the middle house cracked. Not a hairline. Not a shift. A full crack. Six inches wide. Running from the front porch to the back door. And through the crack, something was pushing up. Not a larva. Something larger. Something with a carapace. Something that was not the species in either of their Field Guides.

Webb's interface flashed red. His Field Guide populated with a new entry. And the entry had a classification that Dale had never seen before.

BREACH ORGANISM. NOT VERMIN. NOT INSECT. CLASSIFICATION: PENDING.

The thing in the crack turned toward them. It had too many legs. And it was looking at them like they were the infestation.
