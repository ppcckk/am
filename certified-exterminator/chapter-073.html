Chapter 73: Node Status

# Chapter 73

Dale was writing the structural integrity assessment when the cold stopped mattering.

Not because the temperature changed. Because his hands had gone numb enough that the pencil felt like it belonged to someone else, and the writing came out steady anyway.

That was the thing about protocol work.

You didn't need to feel good to do it right. You just needed the form in front of you and the knowledge of which boxes to check.

He'd been through the form three times.

Field Guide entry. Species identification.

Host integration markers.

Entry point documentation. He'd filled every box he could fill and left the rest blank with the notation "pending adjudicator observation" in the margins.

The birdhouse sat on the concrete beside his knee. He hadn't touched it. He kept it in his peripheral vision, like a specimen jar on the bench while he wrote the rest of the report.

At 6:44, his clipboard pulsed.

ADJUDICATION COMMENCING.

The air in front of him folded. Not dramatically.

Not like the Rift events he'd been treating for three months, where the sky split and things poured out like drain water.

This was smaller.

A seam. A seam in the air that opened like a zipper on a jacket and showed something gray behind it.

A woman stepped through. She was maybe fifty. Wore a gray coat that fit too well, like it had been tailored to her body by something that understood anatomy at a level deeper than fashion.

Her clipboard was black. Not dark brown.

Black.

Like the material had been dyed with something that absorbed light.

She looked at Dale. Looked at Linda. Looked at the birdhouse.

"Technician Kowalski," she said. "Certification Level IV. Thoroughness Rating 9.2. You filed a Contested Verification against a Level IV technician's field determination."

"Yes."

"You understand the implications."

"I understand the form."

She looked at him for a moment.

Then she pulled a chair from somewhere. There hadn't been a chair.

A chair appeared. She sat, opened her black clipboard, and started writing.

"Voss filed for immediate elimination," she said. "He documented the subject's behavior patterns, identified non-human motor function, and classified the subject as a complete infestation event. His Thoroughness Rating on that job was 7.1."

"7.1," Dale said.

"He's fast. He's not thorough. You know the difference."

"I do."

"Your opposing verification claims the subject is not an infestation. You filed under Host Integration Assessment. You're claiming the subject is a structure containing an infestation, not an infestation itself."

"Yes."

"Explain."

Dale set his pencil down. He looked at Linda.

She was still sitting.

Her eyes were open.

Her breathing was shallow but rhythmic. Her hands rested on her knees, fingers slightly curled, and if you didn't know what you were looking at, you'd think she was just a woman sitting in a basement.

But Dale knew what he was looking at.

"Host Integration Assessment is a structural protocol," he said. "It's the same framework we use for building inspections. You don't classify a house as a termite infestation.

You classify it as a structure with a termite infestation. The house isn't the problem.

The termites are.

You don't demolish the house. You treat the termites and you repair the structure."

The adjudicator wrote something.

"Voss's classification treats Linda Marchetti as the pest. My classification treats her as the structure. The pest is inside her."

"Define the pest."

Dale opened his Field Guide.

The entry he'd been building for six hours appeared in the margin. He'd been writing it in the Field Guide's internal buffer, not committing it to the permanent record until the adjudication resolved.

If he committed it now, the entry would be locked to his interpretation.

He needed the adjudicator to validate it first.

"I don't have a species ID yet," he said. "I have behavioral markers.

I have entry point documentation.

I have a structural assessment of the host.

What I don't have is a confirmed species name, because the specimen is still integrated. Pulling it out for identification would violate the host."

"Voss pulled tissue samples."

"Voss pulled tissue from the host's forearm. He didn't sample the infestation. He sampled the structure."

The adjudicator stopped writing. She looked up.

"Show me."

Dale stood. His knees cracked. He walked to Linda, crouched, and held his flashlight at an angle across her face.

The light caught the corner of her left eye. A film coated the surface.

Not cataract.

Not dryness. A membrane. Thin, translucent, with a faint iridescence at the edges.

"Ocular membrane.

Non-human. Invasive.

Located at the posterior lacrimal duct.

It's not covering her eye. It's growing from behind it."

He moved the light to her hands. The fingers were slightly curled, but the nails were wrong.

Not long.

Not dirty, and the cuticles had receded past the point where a human cuticle could exist. The nail beds were exposed.

Not painful-looking. Just wrong.

"Cuticular regression. Consistent with chitinous substrate growth beneath the dermal layer.

She's not sick.

She's being replaced. Slowly. From the inside."

The adjudicator wrote faster now.

"Timeframe?"

"Based on the cuticular regression rate and the ocular membrane development, I'd estimate three to four weeks to full integration.

Maybe less if she's stressed. Stress accelerates the substrate growth.

I found her because she was having a panic attack in her own basement.

Her body was trying to reject the infestation and the infestation was fighting back."

"Voss arrived after you?"

"Twenty minutes after. He saw the ocular membrane, classified it as non-human tissue, and filed for elimination.

He didn't check the entry point, and he didn't check the integration rate.

He didn't check whether the host was still viable."

"Was it?"

"She was alive. She was breathing. She was sitting in her own house in her own basement with a birdhouse she'd been trying to fix.

She was a host with an infestation. Not an infestation."

The adjudicator closed her clipboard.

"Where is Voss?"

"Outside. He filed his Contested Verification remotely. He's not here."

"Good." The adjudicator stood. "That saves time."

She walked to Linda.

Knelt. Held her own light. The light was different from Dale's flashlight.

It was a flat, white glow with no beam angle.

It illuminated everything equally. She held it over Linda's face, then moved it down her body.

She paused at the sternum.

"Rib cage shows minor expansion. Two millimeters bilateral. Consistent with thoracic cavity colonization."

Dale nodded. He'd seen that too.

"The adjudication is resolved," she said. "Subject classified as Structure with Active Infestation. Host status: viable.

Treatment protocol: host-preserving extraction. You are the assigned technician."

Dale felt something shift in his chest. Not relief. Not pride. The feeling of a job being handed to you before you'd finished the paperwork.

"I need the species ID," he said. "I can't extract without knowing what I'm extracting."

"You'll get it during extraction. The Field Guide will populate in real time. You'll have a window."

"How long?"

"Twelve seconds."

Dale looked at Linda, and she blinked.

Once. Slowly.

Her eyes tracked to him. Something stood behind them. Not recognition.

Not fear. Something that was still her but was also something else, layered over her like a second skin that hadn't fully adhered yet.

"I'll need a workspace," he said.

"You'll get one."

"I need it to meet protocol standards.

Containment perimeter. Ventilation. Decontamination station.

I can't work in a basement with a concrete floor and no drainage."

The adjudicator looked at him.

"You're asking me to build a treatment room for a Class VI extraction in a residential basement."

"I'm asking you to give me the conditions I need to not kill the host."

She wrote something. Her clipboard pulsed. Somewhere outside, Dale heard a sound like a truck backing up, except it wasn't a truck. It was larger and more precise.

"Your workspace will be ready in four minutes. The extraction begins immediately after.

You have one attempt.

If you fail, the infestation completes integration and the host becomes a full infestation event. At that point, elimination is the only remaining protocol."

"One attempt."

"One attempt."

Dale looked at Linda. She was looking at him.

Her mouth moved.

No sound came out.

But he could read the shape of it.

Thank you, and he turned back to his clipboard. He started writing the extraction protocol. Step one: isolation.

Step two: containment. Step three: substrate separation.

Step four: host stabilization.

Step five: verify, and he was on step four when the room changed.

The basement walls shifted.

Not dramatically. Not like the Rift.

The concrete floor developed a drain in the center.

The walls gained a coating that looked like epoxy. A table appeared. A light rig appeared.

A decontamination station appeared, complete with a spray tank that was the right size and the right nozzle configuration for a thoracic cavity treatment.

It was his workspace. Built in four minutes by something that had no business being able to build anything.

He set his clipboard on the table, and he picked up the spray tank.

He checked the nozzle. He checked the pressure.

He checked the tank contents. The Field Guide identified the solution as a Class VI chitin inhibitor. He'd never used one.

He'd read about them in the protocol manual. They were designed to dissolve chitinous substrate without damaging organic tissue.

They were designed for insect infestations in wooden structures.

Linda was not a wooden structure, and he looked at the Field Guide entry for the chitin inhibitor. The notes said: "Not tested on mammalian hosts. Application to host tissue may result in secondary integration."

Secondary integration.

That meant if the inhibitor touched Linda's tissue and the substrate was already bonded to her cells, the inhibitor wouldn't dissolve the bond. It would dissolve the organic tissue around the bond and leave the substrate free-floating inside her. Which would be worse.

He set the spray tank down.

"Adjudicator."

She looked up from her black clipboard.

"The standard Class VI extraction protocol is designed for structural infestations. Wooden structures. Drywall.

Foundation.

It won't work on a mammalian host. The chitin inhibitor will strip the organic tissue and leave the substrate bonded to the host's cellular structure."

"Then what do you propose?"

Dale looked at Linda, and he looked at the birdhouse. Four pieces. Contact cement.

A person who had been trying to fix something small and broken.

He looked at his Field Guide.

The entry he'd been building for six hours.

The behavioral markers. The entry point documentation.

The structural assessment.

And then he saw it.

The entry point. He'd documented it six hours ago.

He'd noted it in his structural assessment.

He'd noted it in his Field Guide buffer, and he hadn't connected it to the species ID because the species ID wasn't confirmed.

The entry point was the birdhouse.

Not Linda.

Not her body. The birdhouse.

She'd been fixing it.

She'd been holding it. The substrate had entered through her hands while she was working on it. The contact cement.

The adhesive. The substrate had used the adhesive as a vector.

It had bonded to the cement and then bonded to her through the contact.

The birdhouse wasn't a birdhouse. It was a vector.

He opened his Field Guide. He committed the entry.

SPECIES: UNNAMED.

SUBSTRATE-BASED. ENTRY VECTOR: ADHESIVE CONTACT.

INTEGRATION RATE: 3-4 WEEKS. HOST VIABILITY: CONFIRMED.

The Field Guide pulsed.

NEW MECHANIC UNLOCKED: VECTOR SOURCE IDENTIFICATION.

Dale stared at the text. He'd never seen this mechanic before.

It wasn't in the protocol manual.

It wasn't in any of the certification materials, and appeared in his Field Guide in a font slightly different from the others. Slightly smaller.

The Authority seemed to have added it for this moment.

VECTOR SOURCE IDENTIFICATION: Upon committing a species entry with a confirmed entry vector, the Field Guide will retroactively scan all prior Field Guide entries for shared vector signatures. If a match is found, the prior entry is reclassified.

Dale's hands stopped shaking, and he looked at his Field Guide. He looked at the entries. He looked at the one entry that had a shared vector signature.

Dimensional Vermin. Rattus profundus.

The rat he'd killed in the crawlspace.

Chapter one. The first thing he'd ever exterminated.

The rat's entry pulsed.

RECLASSIFIED: VECTOR FRAGMENT.

NOT A SPECIES. A FRAGMENT OF THE SAME SUBSTRATE.

The rat wasn't a rat. The rat was a piece of the same thing that was inside Linda.

And Dale had killed it.

And the Field Guide had recorded it as exterminated.

And it wasn't exterminated. It was a fragment.

And fragments regrow.

The adjudicator's voice came from far away. "Technician Kowalski. The extraction window is open. Begin protocol."

Dale didn't move, and he looked at his Field Guide. The reclassified entry was still pulsing. And below it, a new line appeared.

VECTOR SOURCE IDENTIFICATION: ACTIVE. SCANNING ALL FIELD GUIDE ENTRIES FOR SHARED VECTOR SIGNATURES.

The scan bar moved across his entries. One by one.

The beetles.

The broodmother. The Class II vermin from the water treatment plant. The thing in the storm drain.

The thing in the school basement.

Each one pulsed.

RECLASSIFIED: VECTOR FRAGMENT.

Every thing Dale had ever killed was a piece of the same organism; that organism was in Linda Marchetti. And it had been in everything. And Dale had been treating fragments for three months and calling them infestations.

The scan bar reached the bottom of his Field Guide.

TOTAL FRAGMENTS DOCUMENTED: 347.

TOTAL FRAGMENTS EXTERMINATED: 347.

TOTAL CONFIRMED DEAD: 0.

The adjudicator said his name again. Closer this time.

Dale looked at Linda. She was looking at him. Her mouth moved again.

Run.

The Field Guide pulsed one final time.

NEW ENTRY DETECTED.

SUBJECT: MARCHETTI, L.

STATUS: NOT HOST.

STATUS: NODE.

Dale's pencil broke.
