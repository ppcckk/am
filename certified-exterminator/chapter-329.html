Chapter 329: Bait of Submission

# Chapter 329

The pen tip pressed against the cloud layer, dark and heavy. Ink bled across the sky, spreading like oil on water.

**TERMINATE.**

Dale didn't run. Running wasted energy he didn't have.

He stood in the cratered lot, his clipboard clutched to his chest, and watched the word form.

The letters were massive, each one the size of a school bus, dripping void-black substance onto the asphalt below. Where the ink touched the ground, the concrete sizzled and dissolved into gray sludge.

The Auditor stood ten feet away, adjusting his tie. He looked bored.

"Write it down," Dale said. His voice was steady, though his left hand trembled against the clipboard.

"Write what?" The Auditor asked.

"The citation. You're citing a violation of the Licensing Authority's Code of Conduct, Section 4, Subsection B: Unauthorized Termination of Licensed Personnel."

The Auditor paused, and he looked at the sky, then back at Dale. A flicker of annoyance crossed his face.

"You are unlicensed.

The protection clause does not apply.

"

"It applies to the *person*," Dale said. He tapped the clipboard.

"Look at the form you handed me.

It's a Suspension Notice, Form 10-C.

Not a Revocation. Not a Termination.

There's a legal difference.

"

Dale stepped forward. The ground beneath his boots was slick with residual slime from the earlier fight. He ignored it. He focused on the paper.

Form 10-C was standard bureaucratic fare. Gray text, red header, small print at the bottom.

But the small print was where the leverage lived.

Dale had memorized the fine print of every form he'd ever signed. Fourteen years of inspections meant fourteen years of learning how to read between the lines.

"Subsection B states that during a suspension pending review, the licensee retains 'administrative immunity' from external dimensional entities that have not filed a formal grievance against the Authority," Dale recited. "The cloud-face didn't file a grievance. It's acting on its own."

The Auditor's eyes narrowed. He pulled a pen from his pocket.

"Clever. But the grievance is implied by the threat to public safety."

"No," Dale said.

"Implied grievances require a Form 4-F, *Notice of Intent to Intervene*. I haven't seen one.

I've checked my peripheral vision.

My System Interface is blank except for the contamination warning. No notices. No warnings.

Therefore, no legal standing for the cloud-face to act.

"

Dale pointed at the sky.

"Stop the ink. Or I'll file a complaint with the Oversight Committee for procedural negligence."

The Auditor stiffened, and he looked up.

The pen hovered, a drop of black ink hanging suspended. The giant face seemed to hesitate.

The air grew heavy, charged with static.

Then, the Auditor sighed, and he raised a hand. The pen stopped. The ink retracted, sucking back into the tip with a wet slurp. The word **TERMINATE** faded, leaving only a faint stain on the atmosphere.

"Fine," the Auditor said. "We proceed with the review."

Dale exhaled. He hadn't moved his feet. He hadn't drawn a weapon. He'd used the one thing monsters couldn't eat and gods couldn't burn: bureaucracy.

"Follow me," the Auditor said. "The review will take place in the Sub-Basement. You will need to bring your Field Guide."

Dale looked at his screen. The Field Guide was still there, locked behind a gray overlay. **STATUS: SUSPENDED. CONTENTS LOCKED.**

"I can't access it," Dale said.

"Then you are blind," the Auditor said. He turned and walked toward a manhole cover that had appeared in the middle of the cracked asphalt. The cover lifted with a hiss of steam. "Step down."

Dale looked at the hole. It was dark. It smelled of ozone and old paper.

He jumped.

***

The Sub-Basement was not underground. It was outside.

The transition felt like falling through a cold sheet of glass. The light changed from gray daylight to the flat, humming white of fluorescent tubes.

The floor was polished linoleum, and the walls were beige. It looked like a DMV office from 1995, except the ceiling was too high, and the air conditioning hummed in a minor key that made Dale's teeth ache.

Rows of desks stretched into infinity. Behind each desk sat a clerk. They were all identical: pale skin, black suits, eyes hidden behind thick spectacles. They typed on keyboards that clicked like insect legs.

Dale walked between the rows. The Auditor led the way, his briefcase swinging by his side.

"Where are we?" Dale asked.

"Processing," the Auditor said. "Your case has been flagged for Expedited Review due to the severity of the cross-contamination. You will undergo a Standard Thoroughness Audit."

"I know what that is," Dale said. "It's a test of my documentation."

"It is a test of your *worth*," the Auditor corrected. "If you fail, your license is revoked permanently. You will be reclassified as 'Unlicensed Hazard.' The cloud-face will finish writing the word."

They reached a desk. It was isolated from the others. On it sat a single sheet of paper and a stamp.

"Sit," the Auditor said.

Dale sat. The chair was hard.

"The task is simple," the Auditor said.

He placed a folder on the desk.

"Identify the error in this file.

Correct it.

Stamp it. You have ten minutes.

"

Dale opened the folder.

Inside was a file from three weeks ago. Job ID: 88-492.

A residential infestation in a split-level home on Maple Street.

Dale remembered it, and he had treated a nest of *Acidic Weevils* behind the drywall. Standard job.

Easy.

He flipped through the pages.

Inspection Report. Signed.

Date: Oct 12.

Treatment Log. Signed. Date: Oct 12.

Verification Report.

Signed. Date: Oct 12.

Post-Service Follow-Up.

Signed. Date: Oct 19.

Everything looked perfect. Clean. Professional.

But something was wrong.

Dale stared at the Verification Report. The signature was his. The date was correct. The stamp was clear.

He looked at the Treatment Log.

"Where is the Frass Sample Analysis?" Dale asked.

The Auditor shrugged.

"Frass analysis is optional for Class-1 vermin if the species is pre-identified."

"It's not optional," Dale said. "Not when the infestation involved structural damage.

Section 2, Paragraph C requires a physical sample to rule out secondary infestations.

I always take a sample.

I always send it to the lab.

"

Dale reached into his pocket. He pulled out a small plastic bag. He had kept it.

He was a packrat. He kept everything.

"I still have the sample," Dale said. "It's in my car. In the glovebox."

"Retrieve it," the Auditor said. "Prove the error."

Dale stood up. He turned to walk away.

The Auditor blocked his path.

"You cannot leave the review room," the Auditor said. "The error is in the file. Find it."

Dale looked at the paper. He looked at the Auditor.

"The error," Dale said slowly. "Is that I didn't find the error."

The Auditor blinked.

Dale sat back down. He picked up the Treatment Log. He read it line by line.

*Applied aerosol spray to wall void. Concentration: 5% Pyrethrin. Wait time: 4 hours. Re-application: None.*

Wait time: 4 hours.

Dale frowned, and he never waited four hours. He waited two. Four hours was the protocol for *Carpenter Ants*.

Four hours allowed the bait to work its way back to the queen.

But this was for *Acidic Weevils*. Weevils were slow.

They didn't need bait cycles, and they needed immediate knockdown. Four hours was a waste of time. It was a waste of money.

And it was a violation of Dale's personal standard.

Dale had never made a mistake like that. He checked his logs on his phone. He pulled up his personal records from that day.

*Oct 12: 2:00 PM - Arrival.* *2:30 PM - Treatment.* *4:00 PM - Departure.*

The official log said 4:00 PM to 8:00 PM.

"I left at 4:00 PM," Dale said. "The log says I stayed until 8:00 PM."

The Auditor smiled.

"Then you falsified the report."

"No," Dale said. "Someone else did."

He pointed to the signature.

"Look at the loop on the 'K' in Kowalski."

The Auditor leaned in.

"The 'K' in my signature has a sharp angle. The 'K' in this log has a rounded curve. It's a forgery."

The Auditor's smile vanished.

"Explain," the Auditor said.

"I didn't write this log," Dale said. "I wrote the Inspection Report.

I wrote the Treatment Log.

I signed the Verification.

But this specific page, the Post-Service Follow-Up, has a different ink density. It's ballpoint.

I use gel. My gel pen is blue. This is black.

"

Dale pulled out his pen. He wrote his name on the back of the folder. He held it up next to the forged signature.

"Blue," Dale said. "And look at the date.

Oct 19.

I was at the Regional Licensing Exam that day. I couldn't have been at the house on Maple Street. I have the exam ticket in my jacket pocket.

"

Dale pulled out the crumpled ticket.

The Auditor stared at the ticket. Then at the pen. Then at Dale.

"Who forged it?" the Auditor asked.

"That's not my problem," Dale said.

"My problem is that my file is contaminated. If a third party altered my records, then the contamination wasn't self-inflicted.

It was external.

Which means my license isn't suspended. It's compromised.

"

Dale stood up.

"Restore my protocols. And give me the name of the person who tampered with my file."

The Auditor sat down slowly. He opened his briefcase. He pulled out a new form.

**FORM 99: EXTERNAL TAMPERING INQUIRY.**

He placed it on the desk.

"You are correct," the Auditor said. "The system flagged the ink discrepancy but failed to escalate it. This is an oversight on our part."

Dale felt a surge of relief, but he kept his face blank.

"So, what happens now?"

"Now," the Auditor said, "you enter the Investigation Phase. You are reinstated as a Level II Technician, but with a 'Red Flag' status. All your jobs will be monitored. And you will be assigned a handler."

"I don't need a handler," Dale said.

"You do," the Auditor said. "Because the forger wasn't just changing dates. They were changing species classifications."

Dale froze. "What?"

"Look at the header of the forged page."

Dale looked. The header read: **SPECIES: ARCHITECTURAL PARASITE.**

Dale's blood turned to ice.

"Architectural Parasites are Class-4," Dale said. "Weevils are Class-1."

"If you had treated the home as a Class-4 threat," the Auditor said, "you would have used Class-4 protocols. You would have used the incinerator spray. You would have burned the house."

"I would have killed everyone inside," Dale whispered.

"But you didn't," the Auditor said. "You used Class-1 protocols.

Which is why the house is still standing.

But the System saw you treating a Class-4 threat with Class-1 tools, which is the cross-contamination. You didn't contaminate yourself by using the wrong tool.

You were set up to contaminate yourself.

"

Dale looked at the sky, even though they were underground. He imagined the giant face writing again.

"Who did it?" Dale asked.

The Auditor handed him a photo. It was a grainy security shot from the Maple Street house. A figure stood in the kitchen, wearing a hazmat suit. The helmet was reflective.

"Unknown," the Auditor said. "But they used a counterfeit Field Guide. Someone who has access to the Authority's database but isn't licensed to use it."

Dale stared at the photo. The figure was holding a clipboard. Their posture was rigid. Professional.

"They're a pest control technician," Dale said.

"Yes," the Auditor said. "And they are still active.

They are currently operating in Sector 4, and the Rift is expanding there. They are feeding it.

"

"Feeding it?"

"By introducing false infestations.

By mislabeling threats. They are causing confusion in the System.

Every error they make creates a ripple.

The Authority is spending all its processing power cleaning up their messes instead of sealing the Rift.

"

Dale looked at the photo. He recognized the stance.

The way the figure held the clipboard.

Tight grip.

Elbows tucked. Weight on the back foot.

It was a stance he had taught to his apprentice.

"Marcus," Dale said.

The Auditor raised an eyebrow.

"You know this individual?"

"Marcus Thorne," Dale said. "He failed his Level II exam. He cheated.

I reported him. He was banned from the Authority.

"

"He is back," the Auditor said.

"And he is using your old protocols. That is why the System flagged the contamination. It thinks it is you.

But the data trail... it smells like him.

"

Dale picked up the photo. He put it in his pocket.

"Where is Sector 4?"

"Industrial District," the Auditor said. "The old textile mill.

He has set up a nest there, and he is creating a fake *Architectural Parasite* infestation to lure the Authority's attention. Meanwhile, he is draining the Rift's energy.

"

"Give me my license back," Dale said.

"I cannot," the Auditor said. "Not until you prove you can distinguish between a real infestation and a forged one. And you have no Field Guide."

Dale closed his eyes. He thought about the map of the city in his head. He thought about the patterns.

"I don't need the Field Guide," Dale said. "I need a map."

The Auditor hesitated. Then he opened his briefcase. He pulled out a map. It was transparent, glowing with blue lines.

"Here," the Auditor said. "This shows the active Rift sites.

The red dots are confirmed.

The blue dots are suspected.

Marcus Thorne is moving between them.

"

Dale took the map. He looked at the blue dots. They formed a pattern.

A spiral. Centered on the textile mill.

"He's not just draining energy," Dale said. "He's building a trap."

"What kind of trap?"

Dale looked at the Auditor.

"A bureaucracy trap," Dale said.

"He's filing false reports in real-time. Every time the Authority sends a team to check a blue dot, they find nothing. They waste points.

They get debuffed, and they leave. And while they're gone, Marcus expands the Rift.

"

Dale folded the map.

"I can stop him," Dale said.

"How?" the Auditor asked.

Dale looked at the stamp on the desk.

"By filing the correct report," Dale said. "If I can identify the forgery in real-time, I can cancel the false alarm. I can redirect the Authority's resources. I can trap *him*."

The Auditor looked at Dale for a long moment.

"You are not authorized to engage in field operations," the Auditor said.

"I am authorized to inspect," Dale said.

"Inspection is a core competency. If I inspect the site and file a report that proves it's a forgery, the System will update. The debuffs will lift.

And Marcus Thorne will be exposed.

"

The Auditor picked up the stamp. He hovered it over the **FORM 99**.

"If you fail," the Auditor said, "you will be terminated.

Not just fired.

Terminated. The System will erase your existence from the database.

You will become a null entity. The cloud-face will not hesitate.

"

"I know the risks," Dale said.

The Auditor stamped the form. The ink glowed red.

**INVESTIGATION GRANTED. SCOPE: SECTOR 4. TIME LIMIT: 24 HOURS.**

Dale stood up. He walked to the manhole cover. He jumped.

***

The surface air hit him like a wall. The sky was still gray, but the giant face was gone. The ink stain remained, a dark bruise on the horizon.

Dale pulled out his phone. He dialed a number he hadn't used in three years.

"Marcus?" Dale said.

Static. Then a click.

"Dale?" Marcus's voice was smooth. Unnerved.

"Why are you calling me?

You're suspended. You're nobody.

"

"I'm inspecting," Dale said.

"I see your blue dots. I see your spiral.

It's sloppy.

The entry points are too uniform. Real parasites don't move in perfect geometric shapes. You're leaving a signature.

"

"Go away, Dale. You're out of your depth."

"You're not draining the Rift," Dale said. "You're trying to hack the Certification Algorithm. If you can make the Authority reject your fake reports long enough, the System will crash. And when it crashes, the Rift opens wide."

Marcus laughed. It was a dry, raspy sound.

"You think you're the only one who reads the fine print?" Marcus said. "I've been reading it for longer than you. And I found a loophole."

"What loophole?"

"Section 7," Marcus said. "The 'Unwitnessed Infestation' clause.

If I can kill you before you file a report... the System doesn't know you were here.

The contamination stays permanent.

And the license... stays suspended.

"

Dale's hand went to his belt. He had no spray. No spray can. No flashlight.

Just his clipboard. And his pen.

"Meet me at the mill," Dale said.

"Why?"

"Because I'm filing a complaint," Dale said. "And you have to be present for the interview."

Marcus hung up.

Dale looked at the map. The textile mill was two miles east.

He started walking.

The air smelled of rain and rot. The ground vibrated beneath his feet. Somewhere, deep in the earth, something was chewing through the foundations of the world.

Dale checked his clipboard, and he had no protocols. He had no protection. He had no Field Guide.

But he had a pen.

And he knew how to fill out a form.

He reached the perimeter of the mill. The chain-link fence was torn. He climbed through.

Inside, the air was thick with dust and the smell of wet wool. Machines still stood in rows, silent and rusted.

In the center of the main floor, a nest pulsed, which was not made of dirt or leaves. It was made of paper.

Thousands of pages. Reports. Forms.

Invoices. They were fused together into a throbbing, fleshy mass.

And in the center, wrapped in the paper cocoon, was Marcus Thorne.

He was alive. His skin was translucent, fused with the paper. His eyes were open. They were blank.

And above him, the giant face of the Auditor appeared. But it was smaller now. Closer.

It was holding a red pen.

And it was circling something in the air.

Dale looked up. The Auditor wasn't looking at Marcus.

It was looking at Dale.

And the word forming in the sky wasn't **TERMINATE**, which was **SUBMIT**.

Dale realized the trap.

Marcus hadn't built a nest. He had built a bait.

And the System was hungry.

Dale raised his clipboard. He uncapped his pen.

He had to write fast.
