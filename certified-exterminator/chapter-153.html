Chapter 153: The Loophole Gambit

# Chapter 153

The clipboard was missing a corner. Not the physical corner, but the metaphysical one that held the world together.

Marcus Thorne, a Class IV specialist for the Authority's Enforcement Division, watched the man in the stained jumpsuit crawl out of the drainage grate. The mud on Dale Kowalski's knees was fresh, smelling of ozone and rot. Thorne adjusted his cufflinks, checking the reflection of the neon sign in the wet pavement. The sign buzzed with a dying flicker, casting long, jagged shadows across the alleyway where Kowalski had just been demoted.

Thorne had seen many failures. He had seen heroes break under the weight of the licensing system.

He had seen power-hungry individuals try to cheat the Thoroughness Scale and end up as biomass in the containment vats.

But Kowalski was different. The man did not scream. He did not rage.

He simply calculated.

Thorne tapped his stylus against his tablet, recording the incident. The file was labeled **INCIDENT 894-B: UNAUTHORIZED PROTOCOL DEPLOYMENT**.

"Look at him," Thorne muttered to his junior associate, a young woman named Elara who was trying hard not to look directly at the weeping creature still slumped against the dumpster. "He's writing in the dirt."

"Should we intervene?" Elara asked, her voice tight. She kept her hand near her stun-baton. "The exam is in twelve hours. If he fails, the cleanup crews have to incinerate the block."

"No," Thorne said. He watched Kowalski stand up, dusting off his hands with a meticulousness that bordered on obsessive.

"He is not failing yet.

He is reorganizing. That is the first sign of competence. Or madness.

It is hard to tell the difference."

Kowalski walked past them. He did not acknowledge the Authority agents. He did not acknowledge the weeping monster, which was rapidly losing coherence, its form dissolving into a puddle of black viscous fluid. Kowalski walked with the stiff, precise gait of a man who believed that if he slouched even an inch, the structural integrity of the universe might collapse.

He stopped at the edge of the alley. He pulled a crumpled receipt from his pocket. He unfolded it. He stared at the blank space on the back.

Thorne leaned forward. "What is he doing?"

Kowalski wrote. Not with a pen. He used the tip of a broken shard of glass from the dumpster, scratching into the wax-coated paper with a pressure that made his knuckles white.

Thorne zoomed in on his tablet feed. The scratch marks were illegible from this distance, but the System interface overlay on Thorne's vision picked up the semantic intent.

**INPUT DETECTED: LOCAL ANOMALY LOGGING.** **STATUS: INVALID FORMAT.**

"Let him," Thorne said, his voice dropping an octave.

"He is trying to bypass the database entry.

If he logs it manually before the exam, the Authority's auto-correct might accept it as a provisional field note. It's a loophole.

A stupid, fragile, bureaucratic loophole."

Elara frowned. "Why would he care about the logs? He lost his license."

"Because the Authority does not recognize 'lost,'" Thorne said. "It recognizes 'pending.' If he can prove he was performing due diligence during the demotion window, he retains his Thoroughness Rating.

If he loses the Rating, he doesn't just fail the exam.

He ceases to exist in the system. And without the system, he is just a man in a dirty suit, standing next to a monster that is currently eating its own tail."

Kowalski finished writing. He folded the receipt into a tight square. He placed it in his breast pocket, over his heart.

Then he looked up.

Not at Thorne. Not at Elara.

He looked at the sky.

The Rift was still open, a purple bruise spreading over the Ohio suburbs. Debris rained down, small chunks of concrete and twisted metal.

Kowalski took a step forward.

He didn't run. He didn't flee. He walked toward the center of the infestation zone, the place where the ground had split open and the things from below had poured out.

"Track him," Thorne ordered. "Do not engage. If he dies, the paperwork is simple. If he survives, the paperwork becomes a nightmare."

***

The air in the service tunnel tasted of copper and wet dog.

Dale Kowalski adjusted his grip on the flashlight. The beam was weak, flickering in sympathy with the unstable mana density of the area.

He checked his watch.

Six hours until the exam.

Six hours to regain enough points to prove he wasn't a liability.

He had 55 points. He needed 500.

The math was impossible. Unless he changed the denominator.

He stepped over a pile of rubble. To his left, a swarm of Chitin-Horned Scarabs was excavating a support pillar. They were Class II pests, usually dealt with by localized fumigation.

But Dale had no fumigation tank. He had no acid spray.

He had a pen, a clipboard with a broken corner, and a deep, abiding hatred for unsealed entry points.

He stopped. He crouched.

The scarabs paused. Their antennae twitched. They sensed the predator in their midst, the man who smelled of bleach and old coffee.

Dale didn't attack. He opened his Field Guide.

It was empty. The Authority had wiped it clean after his demotion. A blank page stared back at him, white and accusing.

He closed the book. He pulled out the receipt from his pocket.

He looked at the scarabs. He counted them. Thirty-two.

He looked at the pillar. It was compromised. The structural integrity was down to 40%.

He looked at the ceiling above the pillar. There was a crack. A leak. Water was dripping through, mixing with the dust and forming a slurry that made the floor slick.

Dale smiled. It was a thin, painful expression.

He reached into his tool belt and pulled out a can of industrial adhesive. Not the special kind for monster goo. The kind for fixing drywall cracks. The kind that cost three dollars at the hardware store.

He didn't spray the scarabs. He sprayed the crack in the ceiling.

The scarabs hissed, confused. Their protocol required them to consume organic matter or attack threats. They did not know how to process a man repairing infrastructure.

Dale worked quickly. He applied the adhesive to the crack, sealing the leak. The dripping stopped. The floor dried.

Then he moved to the pillar. He applied the adhesive to the fractures in the concrete, reinforcing the structure. He used the excess adhesive to coat the edges of the tunnel entrance, creating a sticky barrier.

The scarabs lunged.

They hit the adhesive. Their legs stuck. They thrashed, their chitinous bodies slapping against the sticky wall.

Dale stepped back. He watched them struggle.

He pulled out his pen. He wrote on his clipboard.

**OBSERVATION: ADHESIVE INTERACTION WITH CHITINOUS EXOSKELETONS.** **RESULT: MOBILITY DEGRADATION. 100%.** **ACTION: MANUAL REMOVAL.**

He didn't kill them. That would cost energy. Instead, he used a crowbar to pry the stuck scarabs off the wall, one by one, and tossed them into a pile.

He did this for twenty minutes.

**+2 POINTS.** **+2 POINTS.** **+2 POINTS.**

The notifications appeared in his peripheral vision, faint and translucent. The System was confused. It hadn't registered an extermination protocol. It had registered a maintenance repair.

But the Authority's backend recognized the outcome. The pests were contained. The area was safe.

Dale looked at his score. **69 POINTS.**

It wasn't enough. But it was a start.

He moved deeper into the tunnel. The air grew colder. The light died.

He heard a sound. Not a skitter. A thud.

Heavy.

Wet.

He clicked his flashlight off.

In the darkness, something breathed.

Dale didn't panic.

Panic was unprofessional. Panic led to cross-contamination errors. He activated his night-vision mode, a basic utility granted to all Class I technicians.

The world turned green.

The creature was massive.

It was a **Grief-Weaver**.

A Class IV horror. It had no eyes, only mouths.

Dozens of them, lined up along its torso like pearls on a string. It was weeping. Black tears poured from its face, pooling on the floor.

The creature was trapped. It had gotten its massive, bulbous body stuck in a narrow ventilation shaft. It was struggling to pull itself free, its limbs flailing.

Dale stared at it.

A Class IV. He was a demoted Class I.

Attacking it would be suicide. Running away would be cowardice. Both resulted in a zero on the Thoroughness Scale.

He looked at the creature's struggle. He looked at the ventilation shaft. It was a standard HVAC duct, designed for air flow, not for eldritch horrors.

Dale walked closer. The Grief-Weaver sensed him. Its mouths snapped.

"Stop," Dale said.

The creature froze. The black tears slowed.

Dale raised his hands. He showed them the empty palms.

"You are in violation of Building Code 44-B," Dale said, his voice flat, echoing in the damp tunnel. "Occupancy of a confined space without proper egress. You are obstructing the HVAC system. This is a fire hazard."

The Grief-Weaver tilted its head. One of its mouths opened. A low, mournful wail emerged.

Dale pulled out his clipboard. He wrote.

**VIOLATION: OBSTRUCTION OF UTILITIES.** **RECOMMENDATION: EXTRACTION VIA LUBRICATION AND LEVERAGE.**

He didn't attack. He assessed.

The creature was stuck because its skin was drying out in the cold air. The friction was too high.

Dale walked back to his tool belt. He pulled out a bottle of industrial lubricant. It was meant for squeaky hinges. It was not meant for monster skin.

But the System didn't care about intent. It cared about results.

He walked up to the Grief-Weaver. The creature snapped at him, missing his hand by inches.

Dale didn't flinch. He sprayed the lubricant onto the creature's back, right where it was jammed against the metal duct.

The creature shrieked. The liquid sizzled.

Dale stepped back. He grabbed a winch cable from a nearby construction site. He looped it around the creature's ankle.

"Pull," Dale said to the empty air.

He pulled. The cable groaned. The creature slid. Inch by inch.

The friction dropped. The lubricant worked. The creature's bulk shifted. It was moving.

Dale pulled harder. His muscles burned. His breath came in short, sharp gasps.

The creature popped free. It spilled out of the duct, landing on the floor with a wet thud.

It stood there, trembling. It looked at Dale. Its mouths formed a circle.

Dale held out his hand.

"Exit," he said.

The Grief-Weaver turned. It moved away, slithering into the shadows, leaving a trail of slime and lubricant.

Dale stood there, panting. He checked his score.

**+150 POINTS.**

The notification flashed in gold.

**THOROUGHNESS RATING: 7.6.** **STATUS: REINSTATEMENT PENDING.**

He had done it. He had resolved a Class IV encounter with zero combat damage and full documentation.

But as he looked at the notification, a new message appeared. It wasn't green. It was red.

**VIOLATION DETECTED.** **CROSS-CONTAMINATION WARNING.** **PROTOCOL MISMATCH: LUBRICANT APPLIED TO ENTITY NOT LISTED IN FIELD GUIDE.** **PENALTY: FIELD GUIDE CORRUPTION. 24 HOURS.**

Dale stared at the red text.

He had gained points, but he had broken a rule. His Field Guide was now blank again. He couldn't identify weaknesses. He couldn't use targeted bonuses.

And worse, the Authority wasn't done with him.

A voice echoed through the tunnel. It wasn't the Authority's monotone. It was human. Angry.

"Kowalski!"

A figure stepped out of the shadows. It was Marcus Thorne. But he wasn't alone. Behind him stood a man in a pristine white suit, holding a golden rod.

Thorne's face was grim.

"You didn't exterminate it, Dale," Thorne said. "You released it. That is a Class I felony. Unauthorized relocation of a high-level entity."

The man in the white suit stepped forward. He didn't speak. He raised his rod.

**SYSTEM ALERT: EXAMINATION STARTED.** **CONDITION: HUNT OR BE HUNTED.** **TARGET: DALE KOWALSKI.** **RIVAL: ELITE INSPECTOR VANCE.**

Dale looked at the rod. It glowed with a light that hurt his eyes.

He looked at Thorne. Thorne shook his head slightly. *Run*, the gesture said.

Dale looked at his clipboard. He looked at the dark tunnel behind him.

He didn't run. He opened his blank Field Guide. He picked up his pen.

He wrote one word.

**LOOPHOLE.**

Then he threw the clipboard at Vance's face.
