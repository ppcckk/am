Chapter 14: Class Three Intrusion

# Chapter 14

Dale was on the fourth property when the other technician showed up.

He had his clipboard in his left hand and his flashlight in his right, and the fourth house was a split-level ranch with a brick foundation and a slab porch.

He could smell it before he saw it.

That particular smell. Wet chalk and old copper. The substrate was already degrading under the porch steps.

He could hear it, a faint crackling like a styrofoam cup being crushed.

He was writing his initial assessment. Structural degradation at grade level.

Probable harborage beneath the slab.

Estimated broodmother depth: two feet. When a truck pulled up on the street. White truck.

No markings. The driver's side door opened and a woman got out, tall, dark hair pulled back tight, wearing a grey field jacket with a patch on the left shoulder.

Class III, and she walked up the driveway toward Dale without slowing down.

"You're in my service area," she said.

Dale looked up from his clipboard. "This is a Class II domestic infestation. It's under my jurisdiction."

"It's a Rift event."

"It's not."

She stopped. Looked at him. Looked at the clipboard. Looked at the house.

"The Authority flagged it as Rift-adjacent," she said.

"The Authority flagged it as unlicensed.

That's not the same as Rift-adjacent. The species is domestic.

It was here before the Rift. The Rift didn't bring it."

She stared at him for a long moment. Then she reached into her jacket pocket and pulled out a glass vial, amber liquid, about two ounces. She uncapped it.

"Standard Rift-adjacent neutralizer," she said. "Class III protocol. I'm treating this property."

Dale watched her approach the porch steps. He could see the substrate degradation.

The brick was already soft, crumbling at the mortar joints.

If she poured that neutralizer on a domestic species, the cross-contamination penalty would hit her Field Guide immediately. Twenty-four hours of contamination. The entry would be locked.

She'd lose her damage bonus on every future encounter with this species.

"Stop," he said.

She didn't stop.

"Ma'am. If you apply a Rift protocol to a domestic species, you trigger a cross-contamination penalty.

Forty percent debuff.

Twenty-four hour Field Guide lockout. You lose your damage bonus on this species for a full day."

She paused. The vial was still uncapped.

She looked at the porch steps.

Looked at the notification that was probably floating in her peripheral vision.

Looked at Dale.

"You're sure it's domestic."

"I read the Authority annotation. It's explicit. This species is native.

It predates the Rift. The Rift revealed it, didn't introduce it.

Your Class III protocols are calibrated for dimensional vermin.

This isn't dimensional vermin."

She capped the vial. Put it back in her pocket.

"Who are you?"

"Dale Kowalski. Class II. This is my route."

She looked at the house again.

The crackling under the porch steps was getting louder. A fine powder was emerging from the mortar joints.

White.

Chalky.

"Okay," she said. "What's your protocol?"

Dale looked at his clipboard. He had already written the assessment.

He had already identified the harborage, and he had already noted the depth.

All he needed now was the treatment method.

"I need a permit to excavate under the slab," he said. "That takes forty-eight hours to process. I don't have forty-eight hours."

"So what do you do?"

Dale looked at the porch steps. Looked at the powder. Looked at the other properties on the street.

He could hear it now. Faintly.

Under the next house.

And the one after that. A low, continuous crackling. Like the whole street was turning to chalk.

He opened his Field Guide.

The entry for the broodmother was clean. Verified.

Substrate deprivation was the weakness, and he had his damage bonus; substrate deprivation meant removing the material the creature was embedded in. You couldn't just pull it out.

You had to remove the substrate it was feeding on. The foundation.

The slab.

The concrete itself, which was not a forty-eight-hour permit situation. That was a structural engineering situation.

Unless.

Dale looked at his clipboard. Looked at the other houses.

Fourteen properties. Eleven broodmothers. All connected.

He had been treating them as eleven separate infestations. That was the mistake.

That was what the notification had framed it as.

But pest control had a term for this. He had used it a hundred times on termite jobs.

Systemic infestation.

When multiple infestations share a common causal vector, a common harborage network, a common entry point, they are not multiple infestations. They are one infestation with multiple nodes.

He started writing. Fast. His handwriting was ugly and small and he didn't care.

Property 1 through 14.

Common substrate network. Single causal vector: degraded foundation substrate across contiguous residential properties.

Classification: systemic infestation.

Single treatment plan. Single inspection report.

He held the clipboard up.

The Authority notification flickered. Changed.

SYSTEMIC INFESTATION RECOGNIZED. SINGLE JOB CLASSIFICATION APPLIED. TREATMENT POINTS: 11x MULTIPLIER PENDING VERIFICATION. THOROUGHNESS RATING: PENDING.

The woman read it over his shoulder.

"You just turned eleven jobs into one job and got a multiplier for it?"

"I turned eleven jobs into one job because that's what it is. They share a substrate network.

They're feeding off the same degraded foundation material.

If you treat one without treating the others, the untreated nodes stress-response and accelerate maturation.

You've seen it."

"I've seen it."

"Then you know the window isn't seventy-two hours. It's less. Every hour you leave one untreated, the others speed up. By the time you get to the ninth one, the first one is already past maturation."

She looked at the street. The crackling was louder now. The powder was thicker. On the porch steps of the house in front of them, a section of brick had already collapsed inward, leaving a dark gap.

"How long?" she asked.

"Based on the degradation rate I'm seeing? Forty-eight hours. Maybe less.

The maturation window is compressing because they're networked. Treating one without treating all of them makes the rest worse."

"So we need to treat all eleven simultaneously."

"We need to treat all eleven in the same treatment cycle. Under the same report. With the same protocol."

She looked at him. "What protocol?"

Dale looked at the gap in the porch steps, and he looked at his clipboard. He looked at his Field Guide. Substrate deprivation.

The weakness was removing the material the creature was embedded in.

Concrete. Foundation material.

Substrate.

"I need a jackhammer," he said.

"You need to jackhammer eleven foundations in forty-eight hours."

"I need to jackhammer eleven foundations in forty-eight hours."

"That's not pest control. That's demolition."

"That's structural pest control. It's in my Level II license. Substrate removal for embedded domestic species. I just never had to do it on this scale."

She looked at the street. At the houses. At the powder that was now visible on every porch step, every foundation wall, every brick joint. The whole street was turning to chalk.

"I'll call my supervisor," she said.

"Don't."

"Why not?"

"Because your supervisor is going to look at this and classify it as a Rift event. And then they're going to send a Class IV team with Rift protocols. And they're going to cross-contaminate their own Field Guides.

And then they're going to fail the job. And then the broodmothers will mature and every house on this street will collapse."

She looked at him.

"You're saying I should help you."

"I'm saying you should help me because you're the only other person on this street who isn't going to pour Rift neutralizer on a domestic species and lose their Field Guide entry for twenty-four hours."

She stood there.

The crackling under the houses was a continuous sound now.

Not intermittent. Continuous.

Like the whole block was being digested, and she reached into her jacket. Pulled out a clipboard.

Her own.

Different format. Authority-standard Class III.

"Where's the first one?" she asked.

Dale pointed at the house behind them.

"Already assessed. Harborage beneath the slab.

Two feet deep.

I need to remove the slab."

"Then we start there."

They started there, and the jackhammer came from a construction site two streets over. Dale had called in a favor from a contractor who owed him for a wasp nest removal. The contractor had looked at Dale's face and asked no questions; the jackhammer was loud and ugly and it broke the slab in eleven minutes.

Under the slab, in the dark, the broodmother was exactly where Dale said it would be. Two feet down.

Embedded in the concrete.

The concrete was soft around it, chalky, crumbling, and the creature was pale and segmented and roughly the size of a large dog. It was not moving.

It was not yet active. It was in the pre-maturation state, and the state where it was still feeding on the substrate but had not yet emerged.

Dale documented it. Photographed it.

Wrote the depth, the dimensions, the substrate composition, and he wrote the treatment method: full substrate removal, four-foot radius around the organism. He wrote the verification method: visual confirmation of complete extraction, followed by structural assessment of remaining foundation integrity.

Then he killed it.

Not with a protocol.

Not with a spell. With a shovel and a crowbar and the specific knowledge that a pre-maturation broodmother has a soft ventral surface because it hasn't yet hardened its chitin.

He knew this because he had documented it, and he had written it down. He had read it back.

The Authority awarded the points. Verification confirmed.

One down. Ten to go.

They moved to the next house. Then the next.

The woman, whose name was Reyes, was fast, and she documented as well as Dale did.

She didn't ask questions about the protocol. She didn't try to use Rift tools.

She followed the Class II domestic protocol exactly as Dale had written it.

By the fourth house, they had a rhythm. Dale assessed.

Reyes documented, and they removed the substrate together. They killed the broodmother. They verified.

They moved on.

By the seventh house, the crackling had stopped. The powder had stopped forming. The untreated properties were no longer accelerating because the networked response had been disrupted, and the systemic infestation was being dismantled node by node.

The Authority updated the notification.

SYSTEMIC INFESTATION: 7/11 NODES ELIMINATED. MATURATION WINDOW: SUSPENDED. THOROUGHNESS RATING: PENDING FINAL VERIFICATION.

They were on the ninth house when Dale's phone buzzed, and he looked at it.

AUTHORITY NOTIFICATION: FINAL NODE IDENTIFIED. PROPERTY ADDRESS: 1147 ELMCREST DRIVE. SERVICE AREA: YOUR CURRENT LOCATION.

Dale looked at the house.

His house, and he had lived at 1147 Elmcrest Drive for fourteen years; he had inspected the foundation himself three times.

He had written inspection reports on the crawl space.

He had noted minor settling. He had noted moisture intrusion at the slab joint. He had recommended a moisture barrier.

He had not excavated, and the broodmother was under his house.

It had been under his house for years.

Maybe always. He had slept above it.

He had written his inspection reports above it, and had walked across the kitchen floor above it every morning for fourteen years.

Reyes looked at him. She had read the notification. She was standing on his porch.

"Your house," she said.

"Yes."

"Do you want me to take it?"

Dale looked at the front door. His front door.

The door he had opened this morning, and the door he had walked through to get to his truck. The door above a broodmother that had been feeding on his foundation for longer than he had owned the house.

He opened his Field Guide.

The entry was there. Clean.

Verified.

Substrate deprivation. He had his damage bonus. He had his protocol.

He had his clipboard, and he walked through his own front door.

The infestation was already inside.
