Chapter 80: Three Seconds to Break

# Chapter 80

Dale's hand did not move. The thing's hand did not stop.

The gap between them shrank by a centimeter. Then another.

The thing's arm pushed through the Rift like a pipe through a wall, moving with the certainty of a pre-determined destination.

The joints in its forearm clicked in sequence, each one locking into place before the next one released. Mechanical. Deliberate.

Like a ratchet.

Dale counted the clicks. Four. Five. Six.

He'd seen this pattern before. Not in a creature.

In a telescoping inspection probe.

The kind he used to check for termite damage inside wall cavities. The mechanism was the same: sequential locking, incremental extension, no retraction until the operator released the trigger.

The thing was not reaching. It was extending.

That distinction mattered. Reaching implied intent.

Extending implied mechanism.

Mechanisms could be interrupted.

Intent could not.

Dale shifted his weight to his back foot. Not to run. To create distance.

The protocol for maintaining a safe working distance from an unidentified organism was minimum six feet. The thing's arm was currently at four.

He looked at the clipboard, which was a Transfer Notice.

He could tell from the layout. Top header, bold. Species Designation field, left-aligned.

Liability Assignment block, right-aligned.

Signature line at the bottom, pre-printed with a certification number. He'd received eleven Transfer Notices in the last six months.

They all looked the same.

The Authority standardized its forms.

The thing's clipboard had the header. It had the Liability Assignment block. It had the signature line.

The Species Designation field was blank.

Dale blinked. He looked again.

The field was there, marked, a rectangular box the size of a business card.

Inside it: nothing.

No species name. No classification code.

No dimensional origin notation.

A Transfer Notice without a Species Designation field was invalid, and he recognized this with the same absolute certainty he applied to invalid violation notices. The Authority's own regulations, Section 14.3(b), stated that a Transfer Notice lacking species identification could not be accepted by a licensed technician. The receiving technician had the right to file a Formal Objection.

Dale had filed three Formal Objections in his career. All for missing entry-point documentation. All successful.

He could object to this.

The thing's arm clicked again. Seven.

Eight, and the clipboard was now three feet from Dale's face. The paper on it was not paper. It was something thin and pale and slightly translucent, like the inner membrane of an eggshell.

The text on it was printed in a font that Dale recognized as the Authority's standard typeface, but the letters were too close together.

The spacing was wrong. The kerning was off.

A counterfeit form.

But the header was real. The Authority watermark was real. Dale could see it if he squinted, the tiny geometric pattern embedded in the paper's surface that indicated Authority-issued documentation.

So it was a real form with a missing field. Which meant it was a real form that the Authority had issued. Which meant the Authority had generated this notice. Which meant the Authority considered the thing in the Rift to be a transferable liability.

But it hadn't identified the species. Because it couldn't. Because the thing had no Field Guide entry. Because the Authority had no data on it.

Dale's mind ran the protocol.

Identify, document, isolate, eliminate, verify. Step one was always identify. You couldn't document what you couldn't name.

You couldn't treat what you couldn't document, and the thing was an unidentified organism presenting itself as a compliance document. It was an infestation disguised as a form. And the form was invalid because the Authority couldn't identify it.

But the invalidity didn't protect Dale.

It made him more vulnerable.

Because Section 14.3(b) had a clause he remembered from the written exam at Chapter 30. He'd studied it.

He'd passed it. He'd written the answer in the margin of his answer sheet because the phrasing was ambiguous.

An uncatalogued entity generating a Transfer Notice was, by definition, an unlicensed infestation, and unlicensed infestations fell under the jurisdiction of the nearest licensed technician. The nearest licensed technician was Dale.

The catch-22 was not a loophole. It was the design.

The thing was not trying to hand him the clipboard; it was trying to force a transfer that would trigger his liability.

And it was using the Authority's own assignment logic to do it.

The Authority saw an uncatalogued entity. The Authority assigned it to the nearest qualified technician. The nearest qualified technician was Dale, because his Thoroughness Rating was 9.2 and he was the only Class II in a forty-mile radius with a Field Guide that had populated entries for dimensional organisms.

His compliance record was the reason he was here.

The thing's arm clicked again.

Nine. The clipboard was two feet away.

Dale could smell it now.

Not a smell exactly. An absence of smell. Air displaced by an entity with non-biological metabolic processes.

The atmosphere of a container broken open for the first time.

Dale's clipboard hand was still raised.

His own clipboard was still blank.

The amendment form he'd been filling out was gone. Replaced by nothing.

The Authority had removed it. Or the thing had.

He thought about his Field Guide. Forty-three entries.

Every one of them populated through direct observation.

Every one of them earned by getting close enough to see, to measure, to identify, and the guide didn't populate from a distance. It populated from proximity.

From risk, and the thing was two feet away. If Dale looked at it long enough, the guide might populate.

If the guide populated, he'd have a Species Designation.

If he had a Species Designation, he could file a Formal Objection. If he filed a Formal Objection, the Transfer Notice would be void.

But if the guide populated, the Authority would register the species, and if the Authority registered the species, the Transfer Notice would no longer be invalid. It would be complete. And a complete Transfer Notice could be accepted.

The thing knew this. It was waiting for him to identify it.

The moment his Field Guide populated, the form would fill itself.

The Species Designation field would complete.

And the handoff would become legal.

Dale lowered his clipboard.

The thing's head tilted. The joints in its neck moved. One.

Two. Three.

Four.

Five. Six. Seven.

Eight.

Nine. Ten.

Eleven.

More than a human had. More than anything that evolved on Earth had. The movement was not fluid.

It was a sequence. Each vertebra testing its range independently, like a quality control check.

Dale stepped back.

One foot.

Two. The gap between them widened to three feet.

The thing did not follow. Its arm remained extended. The clipboard hung in the air like a sign.

He thought about the protocol. Identify, document, isolate, eliminate, verify.

He thought about what he could do without identifying.

Isolation. He could isolate without identifying.

You could contain an unknown organism without knowing what it was.

Seal the entry point. Deny access. Cut off the pathway.

The Rift was two feet wide.

Three, maybe. It was a gap in the air above the driveway.

The edges were frayed, and the thing was standing in it, occupying the gap. If Dale sealed the gap, the thing would be cut off. Not eliminated.

Not treated. But isolated.

And isolation was Step Three.

You were allowed to isolate before you identified.

The protocol didn't require identification before containment. It required identification before elimination.

You could trap a rat without knowing its species. You could seal a wall cavity without knowing what was inside it.

Dale reached into his belt pouch. His fingers found the sealant.

Authority-issue, Class II, designed for dimensional entry points.

He'd used it eleven times. It came in a pressurized canister, like expanding foam, but the compound was keyed to dimensional frequency. You sprayed it across a Rift seam and it crystallized on contact, forming a barrier that the Authority classified as a temporary seal.

Temporary meant it would hold for seventy-two hours.

After that, the infestation would resume unless treated.

Seventy-two hours. Enough time to identify. Enough time to document. Enough time to figure out what he was dealing with.

He raised the canister.

Aimed it at the gap. The thing's head tilted again, all eleven vertebrae moving in sequence, and its clipboard hand did not lower. It stayed extended.

Offering. Waiting.

Dale pressed the nozzle.

The sealant hit the edge of the Rift and crystallized instantly, a white lattice spreading across the frayed boundary like frost on glass.

The thing's arm was inside the sealed area.

The sealant reached the arm and stopped. It could not crystallize over the thing's body.

The compound recognized it as part of the infestation, not as part of the entry point.

The thing's arm was trapped between the sealant and the gap. Not contained. Not sealed. Just pinned.

The thing looked at its arm. The joints stopped moving. The clipboard tilted.

Dale sprayed again. The second burst hit the lower edge of the gap.

Crystallized.

The gap narrowed. The thing's arm was now enclosed in a cage of white lattice, its body still on the other side, its clipboard still extended through the remaining opening.

One foot, and the gap was one foot wide now. The thing's arm could not retract.

The sealant had crystallized around it.

The thing's body was on the far side of the Rift, and its arm was on Dale's side, and neither could move.

Dale stepped forward. Eighteen inches from the thing's clipboard.

He could see the paper now, and the text was printed in the Authority's standard font.

The header read: TRANSFER NOTICE, AUTHORITY CERTIFICATION DIVISION. The Liability Assignment block read: RECEIVING TECHNICIAN, KOWALSKI, DALE R.

Certification Level II. The signature line was pre-printed with a certification number Dale recognized. It was his own.

The Species Designation field was still blank.

Dale looked at the thing's face. There was no face.

The area where a face should be was smooth and pale, like the underside of a mushroom cap.

No eyes. No mouth. No nostrils.

Just surface.

And the surface was slightly warm. He could see heat rising from it in a faint shimmer, like exhaust from a vent.

He looked at the clipboard. He looked at the blank field.

If he identified the creature, the field would fill. The form would complete. The transfer would become legal. And he would be responsible for treating an entity he had no data on, with no protocol, with no weakness data, with nothing but his Field Guide and whatever the guide told him in the moment of identification.

If he did not identify the creature, the form remained invalid.

The transfer could not be accepted. The thing was an unlicensed infestation, and he was the nearest licensed technician, and the Authority would assign him to treat it regardless. But at least he would have time.

Seventy-two hours of sealant. Enough time to research.

Enough time to find a weakness.

Enough time to prepare.

Dale uncapped the sealant. Aimed at the remaining gap.

One foot wide. The thing's clipboard was still extended. Its arm was pinned.

Its body was on the other side, and he sprayed.

The crystallization spread.

The gap closed. The thing's clipboard was sealed inside the lattice, trapped in the barrier like an insect in amber. The thing's arm was still pinned.

The thing's body was still on the far side, and the Rift was gone.

Silence.

Dale stood in his driveway.

The sealant was a white rectangle in the air, two feet wide, one foot tall, floating where the Rift had been. Inside it, the thing's arm was visible. The clipboard was visible.

The fingers holding it were long and jointed and pale, and they were still holding the clipboard in the same grip Dale used. Thumb at the top edge.

Fingers at the bottom.

He held his Field Guide with the professional detachment of a technician during treatment. No entry. No new data. The guide remained at forty-three entries.

He looked at his clipboard. The amendment form was still gone. The page was still blank.

He looked at the sealant. It was holding.

The lattice was stable. No cracks. No stress points.

It would last seventy-two hours, and he walked to his truck.

Opened the driver's door. Sat down. Put the sealant canister on the passenger seat.

Started the engine, and he drove three blocks to the nearest Authority kiosk. Parked.

Walked inside.

The kiosk was a phone booth-sized structure with a glass front and a notification screen. Dale pressed his palm to the scanner. His certification number appeared on the screen.

He filed a Formal Objection.

Section 14.3(b). Transfer Notice, species designation field blank.

Invalid.

Objection filed.

Reference number generated. The screen flashed: OBJECTION ACCEPTED.

TRANSFER NOTICE VOID.

Dale exhaled.

Then the screen flashed again.

NEW ASSIGNMENT. PRIORITY: IMMEDIATE.

SOURCE: UNLICENSED INFESTATION, UNCATEGORY.

LOCATION: 4417 MAPLE DRIVE. RECEIVING TECHNICIAN: KOWALSKI, DALE R.

He stared at the screen, and the assignment was not a Transfer Notice. It was a standard infestation assignment.

Generated by the Authority's own logic.

Uncatalogued entity. Nearest licensed technician. No species designation required.

No form to reject. No field to object to.

The Authority did not need a Transfer Notice to assign him.

It just needed to know that something unlicensed was on his property.

And he had just told it that something unlicensed was on his property. By filing the objection.

By identifying the gap. By confirming the presence of an uncatalogued entity in his service area.

His own objection had generated the assignment.

Dale read the screen again. The assignment had a timer.

Seventy-two hours.

Same as the sealant. The Authority expected him to treat the infestation within the sealant's effective window.

He walked back to his truck. Sat down. Looked at the canister on the passenger seat.

Seventy-two hours.

No Field Guide entry. No protocol.

No weakness data.

Just a white rectangle in the air and an arm that wouldn't stop holding a clipboard.

He started driving home.

The thing's arm was still in the sealant when he arrived. He could see it from the driveway. The fingers were still curled around the clipboard. The paper was still blank in the Species Designation field.

Dale parked. Got out. Walked to the sealant. Stood in front of it.

The arm moved.

Unlike its previous movements. Not the ratchet sequence.

This was different.

The fingers uncurled.

Slowly. One at a time.

The pinky first. Then the ring finger. Then the middle.

The index finger extended toward him.

The clipboard tilted. The paper slid off the board. It fell through the lattice and hit the driveway.

Dale looked down, and the paper had text on it now.

Not the Authority font. Handwritten. In his handwriting.

His own handwriting, on his own paper, in his own pen.

It read: FIELD GUIDE ENTRY 44. KOWALSKI, DALE R.

SPECIES DESIGNATION: PEST.

THREAT LEVEL: CLASS IV. TREATMENT PROTOCOL: NOT YET DETERMINED.

Dale looked up. The thing's faceless head was pressed against the sealant from the other side. The surface of it was warm. The shimmer was stronger now.

The clipboard in the thing's hand was empty. The paper was gone.

It was on the ground at Dale's feet.

And it was his name.

And it was his handwriting. And it said he was a pest.

His Field Guide populated. Entry forty-four. His own face stared back at him from the guide.

His own certification number. His own Thoroughness Rating.

And a weakness field that was already filled in.

The weakness field read: CONTAMINATION ANXIETY. TRIGGER: UNIDENTIFIED ORGANISMS. RESPONSE: PARALYSIS. VULNERABILITY WINDOW: 3.2 SECONDS AFTER INITIAL CONTACT WITH UNKNOWN SPECIES.

Dale looked at the thing. The thing looked at him with its face that wasn't a face.

His hand was shaking. He could feel it. The tremor started in his fingers and moved up his wrist; he looked at his own hand like it belonged to someone else.

The thing's index finger pressed against the sealant. The lattice cracked. A fracture line, thin as a hair, spreading from the point of contact.

Dale had three seconds.
