Chapter 77: Factory Set Screws

# Chapter 77

Dale was on his stomach in the crawlspace with a flashlight in his teeth and a clipboard balanced on his forearms when the first notification hit.

FIELD GUIDE ENTRY: KOWALSKI, D. WEAKNESS: PENDING IDENTIFICATION.

He didn't look at it. He was already three crawlspace panels deep into the smell, and the smell was the data.

Fourteen years of crawlspace work had taught him that organic decomposition had a grammar.

Fungal bloom meant moisture intrusion. Acrid ammonia meant rodent urine concentrated over months, and the sweet rot underneath meant something had died in here and nobody had found it.

He wrote the observation down.

Entry condition: unaddressed biological decomposition, estimated duration eighteen to twenty-four months. He noted the panel screws.

Factory set.

No tool marks. No prior access.

He moved forward. The crawlspace was tight.

Shoulders scraping both sides.

His knees found the plastic vapor barrier and the plastic had gone brittle and crumbly under his weight.

He logged that too. Vapor barrier degradation consistent with prolonged moisture exposure.

His phone buzzed in his chest pocket. He ignored it. He was in the middle of a transect.

You don't break a transect. You complete it or you lose the data integrity and the whole inspection is worthless.

The crawlspace was thirty feet deep.

He'd measured it once, years ago, when he'd sealed the entry point that led to the broodmother. The broodmother was gone. Fourteen years gone.

But the conditions that had supported it were still here.

The moisture. The decomposition.

The sealed entry point that had kept the problem contained but never resolved.

He understood now. The Authority hadn't classified him because of what he'd done to the Rift, and it had classified him because of what he'd done to the problem.

He'd sealed the entry point. He'd contained the infestation.

He'd written the report and collected his check and walked away.

And the conditions that had created the broodmother were still here.

Still active. Still producing.

He was the vector. Not because he'd brought something in. Because he'd left the conditions intact.

His phone buzzed again. He read it this time.

TREATMENT ORDER UPDATE: TECHNICIAN VOSS, R. EN ROUTE. PROTOCOL CLASS-VII ERADICATION. AUTHORIZED SUB-PROTOCOLS: NEST DESTRUCTION, ENTRY POINT SEALING, ENVIRONMENTAL REMEDIATION.

Environmental remediation. Voss was going to treat the house. The whole house.

Including the crawlspace. Including the conditions.

Including Dale, and he finished the transect. Thirty feet. Four observation points.

Two entry conditions.

One biological decomposition event. One structural failure (vapor barrier).

He pulled himself backward out of the crawlspace and stood up in the kitchen.

His knees ached. His hands were shaking, which he noted but did not write down.

He went to the front door. Locked it.

Checked the deadbolt.

Checked the window seals.

All intact. All sealed.

The house was tight. That was the problem. A sealed house with unaddressed internal conditions was a pressure vessel.

The Authority's classification made sense in that context. You seal the entry points but don't remediate the interior, and the infestation doesn't leave.

It evolves.

It finds new vectors, and the new vector was him.

He sat at the kitchen table and pulled out his Field Guide.

The entries were still blurred. He couldn't read what the Authority had written about him.

But he could read everything else, and he flipped to the entry for Dimensional Vermin. Rattus profundus. Fourteen years old.

The weakness field read: territorial aggression creates predictable response patterns; isolate nest before engaging adult.

He flipped to the acid beetle entry. Weakness: pheromone trail disruption causes disorientation; treat trail before treating individual.

He flipped to the broodmother entry. Weakness: reproductive cycle requires sustained moisture; eliminate moisture source to render nest non-viable.

Every weakness in his Field Guide was environmental.

Not combat.

Not physical. Environmental.

The Authority didn't classify creatures by what they were. It classified them by what conditions they required; Dale had spent fourteen years sealing entry points without ever addressing the conditions inside.

He pulled out a fresh report form. Standard inspection format.

Client name.

Address. Date. He filled in the client name.

Kowalski, D, and he filled in the address. His address.

He filled in the date.

Today.

His hand stopped moving.

He'd never done this. Not once.

Not in fourteen years.

You don't inspect yourself.

You don't write a violation report on your own work. The whole system is built on external validation.

You do the job, someone else checks it, the Authority certifies it. Self-inspection isn't a thing because the inspector can't be the problem.

Except now the inspector was the problem, and he wrote the violation code. K-7.

Structural entry point.

Then he crossed it out. K-7 was wrong. K-7 was about gaps in the physical structure.

His violation wasn't structural, which was procedural.

He looked at the code list.

He'd memorized it years ago. K-1 through K-12. None of them fit.

He flipped to the back of the form where the Authority had printed the extended codes. K-13 through K-19.

Added after the Rift.

New categories for new problems.

K-13: Cross-dimensional entry point. K-14: Unlicensed dimensional breach.

K-15: Protocol contamination. K-16: Environmental condition persistence. K-17: Vector-class containment failure.

K-18: Self-referential contamination. K-19: [BLANK]

K-18.

Self-referential contamination. He'd never seen that code before. He didn't know what it meant.

He filled out the form regardless, then started the inspection.

The conditions that made him a vector. Not his body.

His behavior.

His procedures. His failures.

Observation one: Sealed entry point without interior remediation. Fourteen years ago.

The broodmother.

He'd sealed the crawlspace entry.

He'd written the report. He'd left the moisture source unaddressed.

The conditions persisted. The infestation didn't resolve. It adapted.

It produced new vectors. He was the new vector.

Observation two: Protocol dependency.

Dale's entire combat methodology relied on Field Guide entries. Identification before engagement. Documentation before elimination.

Without the Field Guide, he had no protocol toolkit.

No weakness data. No targeted damage.

He was, operationally, a technician without tools.

Which meant the Authority's classification was correct. He was a vector for infestation because his effectiveness depended entirely on the system that was now trying to eliminate him.

Observation three: Contamination anxiety. The flaw.

The thing he'd never written in a report because it wasn't a violation, which was just how he worked.

He wouldn't engage a threat until his workspace met protocol standards. Clean surfaces.

Sealed entry points. Controlled environment. The Authority had taken that behavior and reframed it.

The anxiety wasn't a personality quirk. It was a containment protocol.

He was containing himself.

Sealing his own entry points. Keeping his conditions stable. And that stability was what made him a vector.

Because a vector is something that maintains conditions for an infestation to persist.

He wrote it down. The weakness. His weakness. The thing that made him a VECTOR-CLASS.

CONTAMINATION ANXIETY. OPERATIONAL DEPENDENCY ON ENVIRONMENTAL CONTROL. TARGETED TREATMENT: DISRUPT CONTROL ENVIRONMENT TO INDUCE RESPONSE FAILURE.

He stared at the words. He'd identified his own weakness. He'd written it in a format that matched the Field Guide structure, and the moment the pen left the paper, his phone buzzed.

FIELD GUIDE UPDATE: WEAKNESS IDENTIFIED. ENTRY: KOWALSKI, D. WEAKNESS: CONTAMINATION ANXIETY. STATUS: VISIBLE TO ALL LICENSED TECHNICIANS IN SERVICE AREA.

He felt cold. Not the crawlspace cold. A different cold. The cold of understanding what he'd just done.

The Field Guide was shared. It had always been shared.

Every entry he'd ever written was visible to every other licensed technician, which was the system. That was how the Authority built collective knowledge. You identified a weakness, you documented it, and every other technician in range could use it.

He'd just written his own weakness into the shared guide.

And Voss was eleven minutes out. Maybe less now.

Voss had a Field Guide.

Voss could see the entry.

Voss could see the weakness.

Dale looked at the report. K-18. Self-referential contamination.

He'd identified the violation. He'd documented it.

He'd found the weakness.

The Authority would score this. Thoroughness Scale. Maybe a 10.

Maybe a 10.0.

The first perfect inspection of a vector-class organism by the vector itself.

It didn't matter, and the thoroughness was the problem. The thoroughness was what made the weakness visible. If he'd been sloppy, if he'd rushed, if he'd gotten a 4 on the Thoroughness Scale, the weakness field would have been incomplete.

Partial data. Unusable.

Voss would have had to guess.

Instead, Voss had a precise target.

His phone buzzed.

TECHNICIAN VOSS, R. STATUS: ARRIVED. SERVICE AREA: ACTIVE.

The front door handle turned.

Dale didn't move.

He sat at the kitchen table with his clipboard and his pen and his self-inspection report and he watched the handle. It turned again.

Locked. He'd locked it.

The handle stopped. Silence.

Three seconds.

Four. Five.

Then the porch light came on. Then went off. Then came on.

Dale looked down at the report.

The weakness field. CONTAMINATION ANXIETY.

OPERATIONAL DEPENDENCY ON ENVIRONMENTAL CONTROL.

TARGETED TREATMENT: DISRUPT CONTROL ENVIRONMENT TO INDUCE RESPONSE FAILURE.

He understood what Voss would do. Voss wouldn't attack him.

Voss would attack his environment.

Break the seals.

Open the windows. Create chaos.

Make the workspace dirty. Make the conditions uncontrolled. And when Dale froze, when the anxiety locked him down, when he couldn't function because his workspace didn't meet protocol standards, Voss would complete the eradication protocol.

The Authority had given Voss the exact procedure. And Dale had written it himself.

The front door exploded inward. Not kicked.

Not rammed.

The hinges detached from the frame, the wood failing to hold as though ordered to release. The door fell flat on the porch.

Voss stood in the doorway.

Same face. Same clipboard.

Same calm, procedural expression.

Behind him, the porch light flickered in a pattern that wasn't random. It was a protocol. A Class-VII environmental disruption sequence.

The light was part of the treatment.

Voss looked at Dale. Looked at the clipboard on the table. Looked at the report.

"You wrote it," Voss said. Not a question.

"Yeah."

"Self-referential contamination. K-18."

"Yeah."

Voss nodded, and he stepped inside.

The porch light went dark behind him. The windows in the kitchen rattled.

Not from wind. From pressure. The air in the house was changing.

Conditions shifting. The sealed environment Dale had maintained for fourteen years was being systematically unsealed.

Voss raised his clipboard.

"You know the protocol," Voss said.

"I wrote it."

"Then you know what happens when the control environment fails."

Dale looked at his hands, and they were shaking. They'd been shaking since the crawlspace. They were shaking now.

The windows were rattling.

The seals were breaking. The conditions were degrading.

And his hands were shaking because his workspace was no longer protocol-compliant.

He picked up his pen, and he wrote one line on the report.

COUNTER-PROTOCOL: ACCEPT NON-COMPLIANT CONDITIONS. MAINTAIN OPERATIONAL FUNCTION IN UNCONTROLLED ENVIRONMENT.

He underlined it. He signed the report. He held it up.

Voss stopped.

The Authority notification hit Dale's peripheral vision.

VIOLATION REPORT FILED. SUBJECT: KOWALSKI, D. CODE: K-19. [NEW CODE GENERATED].

He hadn't written K-19. He'd written a counter-protocol. And the Authority had assigned it a code.

A code that hadn't existed before this moment. A code for something no technician had ever done.

Voss read the code. His expression changed. Not fear. Recognition.

"That's not in the manual," Voss said.

"No," Dale said. "It's not."

The windows stopped rattling. The porch light stayed off. The house held its breath.

Voss's phone buzzed. He looked at it. His face went still.

"New treatment order," Voss said quietly.

"For who?"

Voss turned the phone around. The notification read:

TREATMENT ORDER ISSUED.

SUBJECT VOSS, R.

ASSIGNED AS PRIMARY TECHNICIAN. TARGET: VOSS, R.

PROTOCOL: CLASS-VII ERADICATION. REASON: CROSS-CONTAMINATION WITH VECTOR-CLASS SUBJECT. SELF-INSPECTION REQUIRED.

Voss stared at his own name. Then at Dale. Then at the clipboard in his own hands.

"You did that," Voss said.

"No," Dale said. "You did that.

You deployed a protocol designed for vector-class organisms against a licensed technician.

That's cross-contamination. Forty percent debuff. And it contaminated your Field Guide entry for twenty-four hours."

Voss looked down at his clipboard. His hands were steady. His face was not.

"You just made me the infestation," Voss said.

"I just made you inspect yourself," Dale said. "Same thing I did. Same code. Same protocol."

The phone in Voss's hand buzzed again, and he didn't look at it. He couldn't.

Because the next notification would be the same one Dale had gotten.

The one that said the thing being inspected was not a technician. It was the problem.

Voss set his clipboard on the kitchen table. Next to Dale's report.

Two forms.

Two inspections.

Two vectors.

"Eleven minutes," Voss said.

"What?"

"The next technician. The Authority doesn't stop. You classified me as a vector.

Now someone else is coming to treat me, and when they get here, they'll see your entry in the shared guide.

They'll see your weakness.

They'll see mine. And they'll treat both of us."

Dale looked at the window. The glass was intact. The seals were holding. The house was quiet.

"Then we have less than eleven minutes," Dale said.

"To do what?"

Dale picked up his pen. He pulled a fresh form from his clipboard. He wrote the client name.

Kowalski, D. And Voss, R.

He wrote the address. His address.

He wrote the date. Today.

He wrote the violation code.

K-19, and he didn't know what K-19 meant yet. The Authority hadn't printed a definition. It was a new code. A code that existed because two technicians had turned their own protocols against each other and the system had generated a category for what they'd become.

He looked at Voss.

"We do the inspection together," Dale said.

"Both of us. Same report.

Same weakness fields.

Same counter-protocols. If we document each other's conditions before the next technician arrives, we can file a joint remediation plan."

Voss stared at him.

"That's not how the system works."

"No," Dale said. "It isn't. That's why the code is blank."

The phone in Dale's hand buzzed.

FIELD GUIDE UPDATE: NEW ENTRY UNLOCKED. SUBJECTS: KOWALSKI, D.

AND VOSS, R.

CLASSIFICATION: MUTUAL VECTOR.

WEAKNESS: PENDING IDENTIFICATION. RECOMMENDED TREATMENT: TOTAL ERADICATION.

NOTES: THIS ENTRY CANNOT BE DELETED.

Dale read it twice. Then he looked at Voss.

"We have to find each other's weakness before the next one gets here."

"And if we can't?"

"Then we're both infestations. And the next technician gets a ten on the Thoroughness Scale."

The porch light came back on.

Footsteps on the walkway.
