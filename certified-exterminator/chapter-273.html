Chapter 273: The Queue Doesn't Shake

# Chapter 273

The woman at the counter didn't look up from her monitor. Her fingers flew across the keyboard, a rhythmic clacking that echoed in the sterile, white-tiled lobby of the Dayton Regional Licensing Bureau.

To her, the line of desperate, bruised, and bleeding applicants was a static annoyance.

A queue of numbers. A backlog to be processed before 5:00 PM.

She saw the man in the third row, and he was large, broad-shouldered, and wearing a stained jumpsuit that had seen better decades, let alone days. His skin had a peculiar, matte texture, like cured leather left out in the sun too long.

He wasn't shaking.

Most applicants shook. They trembled with fear, rage, or the adrenaline crash of near-death experiences. This man sat perfectly still, his hands resting on his knees.

His fingers were thick, the nails thickened and yellowed, and he was staring at the floor. Specifically, he was staring at the grout lines.

The woman, whose name badge read *Brenda*, finally glanced up.

She frowned.

The security sensors had flagged him the moment he crossed the threshold. Not for weapons.

Not for unauthorized magic. They had flagged him for *biological irregularity*. His cellular density was off the charts.

His thermal signature was too low. He looked less like a human man and more like a walking corpse that had been properly preserved.

Brenda tapped her headset.

"Desk Four has a Code Red anomaly. Requesting containment."

The man looked up then.

His eyes were dark, flat, and unblinking. He didn't raise his voice. He didn't beg.

He simply raised a clipboard.

"Dale Kowalski," he said. His voice was a dry rasp, like sandpaper on concrete.

"Certification Level IV.

Job ID 89044-Alpha. I am here for the mandatory re-evaluation and the submission of my post-incident documentation.

"

Brenda paused. She looked at his badge, which was flickering slightly, the holographic projection struggling against the static field radiating from his body.

Then she looked at the clipboard, which was pristine.

The paper was crisp. The pen was capped.

"Sit," Brenda said, her voice losing some of its bureaucratic edge. "You're early. The queue isn't open for another twenty minutes."

"I'm not in the queue," Dale said. He stood up. The chair groaned under his weight, the metal legs bending slightly.

"I'm filing a preemptive correction to a System error. And I have a question about the classification of my current condition."

Brenda stared at him. For a second, she forgot her training. She forgot the script. She saw a man who treated an apocalyptic dimensional breach like a paperwork dispute.

"Go to booth three," she whispered, pointing to a small, soundproofed booth at the back of the lobby. "The inspector is already waiting for you."

Dale nodded, and he didn't thank her. He didn't look relieved. He looked like a man who had just remembered he left the stove on.

He walked toward booth three, his footsteps heavy, each impact leaving a faint, dusty print on the polished floor.

Brenda watched him go. She checked her screen.

The alert was still blinking. *Subject: Kowalski.

Status: Contaminated. Action: Immediate Purge.*

She looked at the line of people behind him. She looked at the booth. She took a deep breath, adjusted her headset, and called the next number.

Dale entered the booth. The door sealed with a hiss of pressurized air.

The lighting shifted to a harsh, clinical white, and a single desk sat in the center.

Behind it sat Inspector Vane.

Vane was small, thin, and smelled of old coffee and ozone. He wore a suit that was perfectly pressed, but his tie was askew. He didn't look at Dale. He looked at a tablet.

"Sit," Vane said.

Dale sat. The chair was reinforced steel. It held.

"You're late for your appointment," Vane said, without looking up. "You had a twenty-four-hour window. You used eighteen of them to drive from the incident site.

That's efficient. But unnecessary.

"

"I took a detour," Dale said. He placed the clipboard on the desk. "I encountered a Class-3 nuisance on Route 9. I handled it."

Vane finally looked up. His eyes were sharp, predatory.

"You handled a Void-spawn on a public highway? Without filing a public safety advisory?"

"I did file one.

It's in the appendix. Page four.

" Dale tapped the clipboard. "I used a localized suppression field generated by my Field Guide's *Static Charge* protocol.

Minimal collateral damage.

One pothole. Two flat tires.

The creature was documented, identified, and eliminated.

Thoroughness rating: 8.5.

"

Vane frowned. He picked up the clipboard. He didn't open it. He just held it, feeling the weight.

"You're glowing, Kowalski."

Dale looked down at his hands. A faint, purple luminescence pulsed beneath his skin.

The tag he had caught was embedded in his left palm.

It wasn't just a tracker; it was a symbiote, and he could feel it rooting, sending tendrils up his wrist, into his forearm.

"The Authority flagged you for deletion," Vane said. "Your biological signature has shifted. You are no longer fully human.

You are a hybrid. A breach vessel.

Under Protocol 77-C, you are to be terminated and your license revoked.

"

Dale didn't flinch. He reached into his pocket and pulled out a small, folded piece of paper. He unfolded it.

It was a hand-drawn diagram.

Complex. Detailed.

It showed the tag, his arm, and the energy flow.

"I am not a breach vessel," Dale said. "I am a vector. And I have a loophole."

Vane raised an eyebrow.

"Loopholes are for lawyers. You're a technician."

"Read the manual, Inspector. Section 4, Paragraph 12. *Contamination Protocol.*" Dale slid the paper across the desk. "When a licensed technician is infected by a dimensional entity during active duty, their body becomes a *containment unit*.

The Authority does not delete containment units. It *harvests* them.

Or it *monitors* them.

Termination is only for *failed* containment.

"

Vane picked up the diagram. He studied it. The lines were precise. The labels were correct.

"You're arguing that by letting the tag enter your skin, you've completed the *Isolation* phase of the extermination protocol," Vane said slowly.

"Correct. I identified the threat (the tag).

I documented its nature (surveillance and potential weaponization). I isolated the host (myself). And now, I am eliminating the threat from within.

" Dale pointed to the tag.

"It's not controlling me. I'm controlling it.

I'm using its energy signature to power my *Static Charge*.

The Authority's own rule states that *thoroughness* is rewarded. If I delete myself, you lose the data on the Void's new integration method. If you keep me, you get a living case study.

"

Vane stared at him. The silence stretched, thick and heavy. The air in the booth hummed with the tension of the containment field.

"That's a dangerous gamble," Vane said. "You could be exorcised. Or lobotomized."

"I'm a pest control technician," Dale said. "I don't exorcise. I exterminate. And I'm very good at my job."

Vane sighed, and he tapped his tablet.

"Fine.

I'll flag you for *Observation* instead of *Deletion*.

But there's a cost. You've been marked. That means you're visible to the Rift.

Everything that comes through now will see you. And the Authority's monitoring doesn't come free.

"

"What's the cost?"

"Your Field Guide is compromised.

The entry for *Rattus profundus*?

It's gone. Contaminated.

You have twenty-four hours to reacquire the data. If you don't, your next certification exam is void.

"

Dale's jaw tightened. *Rattus profundus*. The first monster he'd ever faced. The entry he'd spent weeks perfecting. The weak point he'd memorized so he could one-shot the next one.

"Why?" Dale asked.

"Because you let the Void inside your workspace," Vane said, gesturing to Dale's arm. "That's a code violation.

K-7.

Structural integrity failure. You have to fix it. Or you lose your license.

"

Dale stood up. The chair screeched. He picked up his clipboard.

"I'll fix it," Dale said.

"You have four hours until the Rift surge peaks in Sector 4," Vane said.

"That's where the *Rattus* nests. But it's not just rats anymore.

The Void has changed them.

They're armored. They're fast. And they're hunting you specifically.

"

Dale walked to the door. He paused.

"I need new gear."

Vane smiled, a thin, cruel expression.

"That'll be on your bill. Level IV technicians get a stipend. But you're currently in debt from the cleanup in Chapter 270."

"Charge it," Dale said.

He walked out, and the lobby was different now. The line had grown.

People were arguing.

A man in the corner was trying to cast a spell, but the containment field kept fizzling his magic.

Brenda was typing furiously, her face pale.

Dale moved through the crowd. He didn't push. He didn't apologize.

He just moved with the inevitable momentum of a glacier. He reached the equipment locker at the back.

He swiped his badge.

It flashed red, then amber, then green.

*Access Granted: Level IV Gear. Temporary.*

The locker opened.

Inside hung a new suit. It was heavier than his old one.

Matte black.

Reinforced plating on the shoulders and chest. Integrated respirator. And in the corner, a new canister.

It wasn't spray. It was a gel. *Void-Neutralizing Compound*.

Expensive.

Unstable.

Dale dressed. He pulled the mask over his face. The filters hissed. The air tasted like copper and ozone.

He checked his Field Guide on his wristpad. The entry for *Rattus profundus* was blank. Just a question mark.

He walked back into the lobby.

Brenda looked up. She saw the new suit. She saw the canister. She saw the dead look in his eyes behind the mask.

"You're going back out there?" she asked.

"I have a job to finish," Dale said.

He pushed open the doors.

Outside, the sky was bruised. Clouds swirled in unnatural patterns, purple and black. The air vibrated. In the distance, sirens wailed, but they sounded distant, muffled by the heavy atmosphere.

Dale walked to his truck. It was still there, parked crookedly in the lot. The windshield was spiderwebbed.

He got in. He started the engine. It coughed, then roared.

He looked in the rearview mirror.

His reflection was wrong.

For a second, the man in the mirror wasn't Dale. It was something else. Something with too many teeth. Something with eyes that reflected light that wasn't there.

Then the image snapped back. Dale Kowalski, 38 years old, tired, angry, and alive.

He put the truck in gear.

His phone buzzed. A text message. Unknown number.

*They're not just rats anymore, Dale. Look at the walls.*

Dale looked up.

The building's exterior wall, right outside the licensing bureau, was rippling. The concrete was softening. Turning into chitin.

A head poked through. Not a rat head. A head shaped like a rat, but with human teeth. And it was wearing a tiny, stolen security badge.

It looked at Dale.

It smiled.

Dale slammed the truck into reverse, tires screeching.

The creature pulled itself free, its body unfolding like wet paper. It landed on the asphalt. Then another. Then another.

They weren't attacking. They were waiting.

And they were all looking at the tag in Dale's hand.

The Authority wasn't watching him, and the Authority was watching *them*.

Dale started the engine.
