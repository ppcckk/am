Chapter 83: Wet Driver Seat

# Chapter 83

The truck was already in Rachel's driveway when Dale pulled up. Not his. A county Authority sedan, white with the blue certification stripe along the door, parked at an angle that blocked her garage. Dale registered it with his usual assessment: incorrect angle, incorrect position, unfit for the task.

He killed the engine. Sat for three seconds.

Counted the lights on the house.

Rachel was on the porch with a folder. She had been on the porch with that folder the last two times he came out, and the folder had grown a rubber band.

Kitchen. Hallway. Rachel's bedroom.

Front porch.

Four lights, no motion behind the curtains, and he checked his kit bag. Field Guide tablet, emitter, sealant gun, three canisters of Class-III suppressant, a roll of barrier tape, the clipboard he'd been using since chapter eleven. Everything in its place. Everything accounted for.

The front door was open.

Not broken. Open. The deadbolt was engaged but the chain was off, which meant someone had been invited in or had a key.

Rachel kept her key under the mat. Dale knew because he'd put it there himself when she was nine.

He stepped onto the porch. The Authority sedan's engine ticked as it cooled. No driver. No technician visible through the windows.

Inside, the kitchen light was too bright.

Rachel sat at the table with her laptop open, a mug of something cold pushed to the side, and her hands flat on the counter like she was holding the table down.

Her eyes were red. Not crying.

The kind of red that comes from staring at a screen for too long in a dark room.

"Dad."

"You left the door open."

"I know."

He set his kit bag on the floor by the door. Didn't sit.

Didn't take off his jacket.

Protocol required he assess the space before settling in, and the space was wrong. Two tablets on the table. One was Rachel's county-issued unit, the standard gray slab they handed to junior techs pending certification.

The other was black.

Matte black, no branding, no certification stripe. Dale had seen one like it before.

On the technician's face.

In the crawlspace under the Meridian complex.

"Where did that come from?"

Rachel looked at the black tablet. "It was on the porch when I got home. No one in the car. No one in the house."

"You didn't touch it."

"I touched it."

Dale pulled a pair of nitrile gloves from his jacket pocket. Pulled them on. Walked to the table and looked at the black tablet's screen.

The Field Guide index was open. Entry 46 through 49 were visible, exactly as he'd seen them in the truck.

Margaret Chen.

Victor Osei.

Rachel Kowalski. Dale Kowalski.

The text was stable now. No new entries forming. The mimic had stopped writing.

But the index counter read 50. Entry 50 existed.

It was blank, and the name field was empty. The weakness field was empty. The trigger field was empty.

But the vulnerability window field had a number in it. 0.0 seconds.

"Rachel. Show me what you found in the county database."

She turned her laptop toward him. The screen showed the Authority's central Field Guide repository.

The interface was the same one Dale used every day, the one he'd memorized fourteen years ago when the system first went live in Ohio.

Standard search. Standard filters. But Rachel had run a query he'd never seen anyone run.

She'd searched for entries with a vulnerability window under 5 seconds, and the results were not creatures.

Forty-nine entries. All human.

All formatted identically to the Authority's official species documentation.

Species name, habitat, behavior pattern, vulnerability window, recommended treatment protocol.

The same fields Dale filled out for every job he'd ever done, and the same fields that triggered the damage bonus system.

"They're in the official database," Rachel said. "Not in some shadow file. Not in a corrupted partition.

In the live system. The one every certified technician in the county pulls from."

Dale scrolled through the entries, and the formatting was perfect. The classification codes were valid. The treatment protocols recommended for each entry were specific, actionable, and calibrated to the listed vulnerability.

Margaret Chen's entry recommended a protocol that exploited claustrophobia.

The protocol was labeled as a Class-IV containment sequence, the kind used for burrowing arthropods. It would work.

The system would grant a damage bonus to whoever deployed it.

"Who filed these?"

"That's the problem." Rachel's voice was flat. "They're filed under your certification number."

Dale looked at the filing metadata. Every entry listed his ID. His certification level.

His Thoroughness Rating. The Authority had accepted them as his work.

Which meant the system had validated them.

Which meant the damage bonuses were active.

Which meant anyone with a valid Field Guide pull could target his daughter.

He pulled his own tablet from the kit bag. Opened his personal Field Guide. The entries were not there.

His personal guide was clean. The contamination was in the county repository, not his device.

But the system didn't care about the distinction, and the repository was the source. Personal guides synced to it on every certification review. His next review was in nine days.

"Rachel. When was your next sync scheduled?"

She checked her laptop. "Tomorrow. 0600."

"Your personal guide pulls from the county repository on sync day. If it pulls these entries, they go into your guide. And your guide is the targeting document. If the mimic has access to your personal guide, it gets the damage bonus."

"I know."

"You touched the black tablet. Did it sync?"

"I don't know."

Dale picked up the black tablet.

Held it at arm's length. The screen was still showing Entry 50.

The blank entry with the 0.0 vulnerability window, and he turned it over. The back was smooth. No serial number.

No certification mark. No Authority seal.

"It's not a Field Guide device," he said. "It's a writing tool. The mimic used it to file entries into the county repository. It has administrative access."

"How?"

"Same way you do.

It's using my certification number.

My access. My Thoroughness Rating." Dale set the tablet down.

"It passed a certification exam. Or it faked one. The system doesn't verify the applicant's biology.

It verifies the applicant's documentation."

Rachel stared at him. "You're saying the mimic is a licensed exterminator."

"I'm saying the mimic is using the licensing system exactly the way it was designed.

It identified targets. It documented weaknesses. It filed them in the official repository under a valid certification.

The system granted it the damage bonuses.

It's not hacking the system. It's working within it."

He pulled out his clipboard.

Started writing. Not a report. A correction request.

Form 7-C. Documentation Correction Request.

The form he'd filed twice in his career, both times for misidentified species entries.

The form that, when approved, forced the Authority to quarantine contaminated entries and flag them for review.

But Form 7-C had a requirement he'd never needed to invoke before. Section 4, subsection B: the requesting technician must provide a verified copy of the contaminated entry in their personal Field Guide to establish the correction baseline.

He had to transcribe Rachel's vulnerability data into his own guide.

"You're not going to do that," Rachel said.

"I have to. If I don't file the correction, the entries stay live. If they stay live, the mimic keeps the damage bonus. And if they sync to your guide tomorrow, you become a target in your own device."

"If you transcribe them, your guide becomes a target document."

"Yes."

"Then the mimic can use them against you."

"Yes."

Rachel stood up. "There has to be another way."

"There isn't. The system doesn't allow corrections without baseline documentation.

I've read the protocol manual. All nine levels. Section 4, subsection B is non-negotiable.

It's the same rule that applies to species misidentification. You have to prove the entry is wrong by showing what the correct entry should be."

"That's not the same thing. These aren't species entries. These are people."

"The system doesn't make that distinction.

I've been saying that for six months and no one listened." Dale filled in the form header. His certification number. His name.

The date.

"Rachel. I need you to do something for me."

"What?"

"Pull up the county repository. Find Entry 50. The blank one. Tell me what's in the species classification field."

She turned back to her laptop. Scrolled. Her face changed.

"It's not blank," she said.

"It has a classification code. It's a mimic entry. Class VII.

Dimensional. But the species name field says..."

"Say it."

"It says Examiner."

Dale stopped writing.

Looked at the form.

Looked at the black tablet. Entry 50.

The blank entry. The one that had been forming when he closed the tablet in the truck.

"Rachel. The Authority has a certification examiner assigned to my Level V review."

"I know. They emailed you last week."

"Pull up the examiner's file. Name. ID number."

She typed. Pulled up the county certification board roster. Scrolled to the Level V examiners assigned to his region.

There was one. Name: Robert Alderman.

ID: 7741-KW.

Assigned to Dale's review. Scheduled for next Tuesday.

Dale looked at the black tablet.

Entry 50. Species classification: mimic.

Class VII.

Species name: Examiner.

"Rachel. What's Robert Alderman's certification history?"

She pulled up the file. "Certification Level VII.

Filed through the county board.

Exam passed six weeks ago.

Practical component was a live demonstration against a Class IV infestation. Scored 9.8 on Thoroughness."

"Six weeks ago."

"Yeah."

"I was fighting a Class IV infestation six weeks ago. In the Meridian complex."

Rachel's hands stopped moving on the keyboard.

"The Authority sent an examiner to observe that job," Dale said. "Someone watched me work. Watched me document.

Watched me file. And then they filed their own entries using my method.

My format.

My certification number."

He looked at the form in his hand. Section 4, subsection B. Transcribe the contaminated entries into your personal Field Guide. Establish the correction baseline.

If he transcribed Entry 48, Rachel's entry, into his personal guide, the system would flag his guide as a targeting document. The mimic, if it had access to his guide, could use Rachel's vulnerability against her, and the correction would quarantine the county entries. The damage bonus would be suspended.

Unless the mimic was the examiner.

Unless the mimic had already scheduled the Level V review; a Level V review required the examiner to access the candidate's personal Field Guide. To verify entries. To check for contamination.

The examiner would have access to Dale's personal guide. The examiner would be the mimic. The mimic would see the transcribed entries. The mimic would already have the vulnerability data.

The correction would be pointless.

Dale looked at the form.

Looked at Rachel. Looked at the black tablet.

Entry 50.

Robert Alderman. Certification Level VII. Examiner.

He picked up his pen. Started writing Entry 50 in his personal Field Guide.

Not a correction.

A new entry.

The mimic's entry. His own vulnerability.

Protocol adherence. Trigger: authority directives. Response: compliance.

Vulnerability window: continuous, and he wrote it because he had to know what it looked like from the other side. Because if he was going to be targeted, he wanted to be the one who documented it first. Because the system rewarded thoroughness, and thoroughness meant knowing your own weakness before someone else used it.

He closed the tablet. Stood up.

"Rachel. Cancel the Level V review."

"I can't. Only the examiner can cancel a scheduled review."

"Then we go to Tuesday."

"Dad, if that examiner is the mimic, it will have full access to your personal Field Guide during the review. Everything you've documented. Every vulnerability you've transcribed. Including mine."

"Yes."

"Then what's the plan?"

Dale picked up his clipboard. The correction request was half-finished. He tore the page off and set it on the table.

"There's a rule in the certification manual. Section 7, subsection A. If a candidate identifies contamination in the examiner's credentials during a live review, the review is suspended pending investigation. The examiner loses access to the candidate's guide until the investigation is complete."

"You have to catch it during the review."

"I have to catch it during the review. With my own Field Guide open. With its entries visible. With the bait laid out."

Rachel looked at the form on the table. At the black tablet. At her father, who was pulling off his nitrile gloves one finger at a time.

"That's not a plan," she said. "That's a trap."

"It's a protocol."

"It's you walking into a room with a monster and hoping it makes a mistake."

Dale put the gloves in the kit bag.

Zipped it. Checked the sealant gun. Checked the emitter.

Checked the canisters.

Everything in its place.

"It makes mistakes," he said.

"It filed Entry 49. It wrote my weakness in my handwriting. It left a black tablet on your porch with no serial number and no Authority seal.

It's sloppy. It's thorough but it's sloppy.

And sloppy work gets flagged."

He picked up the clipboard. Walked to the door. Stopped.

"Rachel. Tomorrow at 0600, your personal guide syncs. Don't let it."

"I can disable sync."

"Disable it.

Then drive to the county office. File a personal contamination report on your own guide.

Flag it as compromised. The system will lock it until an examiner clears it."

"An examiner like Robert Alderman?"

"An examiner like Robert Alderman will not be able to access a locked guide. That buys us time."

He stepped outside. The Authority sedan was still in the driveway.

The engine had stopped ticking, and the windows were dark. Dale walked to his truck. Opened the door.

Sat down.

His tablet pinged. A notification from the Authority.

Standard formatting.

Blue banner. Certification level icon.

NOTICE: Your Level V certification review has been rescheduled. New date: tomorrow. New location: County Authority Office, Room 3B. Examiner: Robert Alderman.

Tomorrow. Not Tuesday.

Dale read it twice. Looked at Rachel's house. The kitchen light was still on. He could see her through the window, standing at the counter, phone pressed to her ear.

He started the truck.

The notification appeared again. Same formatting.

Same blue banner.

This time, an extra line appeared at the bottom in smaller print.

The kind of text that usually appeared in the fine print of a violation notice.

EXAMINER NOTE: Candidate is advised that the practical component will involve live documentation of a Class VII specimen. The specimen will be present in the examination room. The candidate is expected to identify, document, isolate, and eliminate the specimen using only their personal Field Guide entries.

The specimen will be present in the examination room.

Dale read it a third time. Put the tablet down. Looked at his hands on the steering wheel.

The mimic had moved the review up. Twenty-four hours. And it had changed the format.

The practical component was no longer a simulation. It was a live encounter.

In a room.

With the mimic. And the only tool allowed was his personal Field Guide.

His personal Field Guide, which he had just contaminated with Rachel's entry. And his own.

He pulled out of the driveway, and the Authority sedan sat empty in Rachel's driveway, engine cold, windows dark, no one inside. He drove past it slowly. Looked at the driver's seat through the window.

The seat was wet.
