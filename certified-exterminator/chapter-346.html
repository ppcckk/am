Chapter 346: Certification Level IX

# Chapter 346

The smell hit him first. Not the copper tang of blood or the sulfur stench of rift-rot, but the distinct, chemical sharpness of industrial-grade adhesive and ozone.

Dale blinked. The grey suit was gone.

The older version of himself had vanished with the closing elevator doors.

Instead, Dale stood in a corridor that stretched infinitely in both directions, lined with filing cabinets made of obsidian glass. Each cabinet hummed with a low, vibrating frequency that rattled Dale's teeth.

He wasn't in Ohio. He wasn't anywhere he recognized.

A man in a crisp, white uniform stood before the nearest cabinet, holding a clipboard that seemed to be made of bone. He didn't look up.

"Certification Level III," the man said. His voice was like gravel grinding under a boot. "You're late for your intake. Again."

Dale straightened his back, and he checked his hands. No blood.

No duct tape residue.

Just the standard-issue grey jumpsuit, wrinkled at the knees from the fall in the previous reality.

"Late?" Dale asked. "I was just—"

"Cut the chatter." The man tapped the bone clipboard. "Name?"

"Kowalski. Dale. Level II Technician. Currently pending PROMOTION TO LEVEL III."

The man looked up. His eyes were empty white sockets, filled with swirling grey mist. He didn't have a nose, but he breathed. A wet, rattling sound.

"Wrong file," the man said. "This is a Class IX intake. You are here to answer for the violation of Protocol 7-Alpha: Unauthorized Temporal Interference."

Dale froze. His hand instinctively went to his pocket, searching for his pen. It wasn't there.

"I didn't interfere with time," Dale said, his voice dropping into that flat, procedural register that usually kept OSHA inspectors at bay. "I was contained in a dimensional anomaly. The Authority flagged the event as a misidentification, not a violation."

The white-uniformed man smiled. It was a terrible, stretching thing.

"The Authority flagged it," he agreed. "But the Licensing Authority's bylaws, Section 4, Paragraph 9, states that any handler who interacts with a 'Pre-Certification Variant' of themselves without a signed waiver is subject to immediate audit."

Dale frowned. He didn't know that paragraph. The manual he'd studied for the exam didn't mention pre-certification variants.

"Show me the text," Dale said.

"Read it." The man pointed a skeletal finger at the air.

A holographic text block flickered into existence. *SECTION 4, PARAGRAPH 9: The handler must not acknowledge the presence of past or future selves unless a Class X Licensee is present to act as a mediator.*

Dale stared at the floating text.

"I didn't acknowledge him. I entered the elevator because the door was open. That's standard transit protocol."

"Transit protocol requires a destination," the man said. "You entered a lift with a destination marked 'Origin.' That is a violation of spatial integrity.

You attempted to erase your own history, which is an extermination of the self.

And you, Dale, are not licensed to exterminate humans.

"

Dale felt a cold knot form in his stomach. He forced himself to breathe. In through the nose.

Out through the mouth. Count to four.

"I need to file a discrepancy report," Dale said.

"You are filing it now," the man said. "The issue is that you have no pen. And no paper. And no jurisdiction."

Dale looked around the infinite corridor. There were thousands of cabinets. Thousands of clerks. All of them ignoring him.

"If I don't have a pen," Dale said, "how am I supposed to document the violation?"

The man shrugged.

"You don't. That's the penalty. Your Field Guide is locked.

You cannot identify new species.

You cannot target weaknesses. You are a Level II technician with no toolkit, trapped in a bureaucracy that predates language.

"

Dale clenched his jaw. His right hand twitched toward his hip, where his spray canister usually sat. Empty.

"Who runs this place?" Dale asked.

"The Archivist," the man said.

"And he is currently reviewing your Thoroughness Rating. It has dropped. A 9.2 is impressive.

But a 9.2 with a pending temporal violation? The Authority is considering revoking your certification entirely.

"

"Revoking it?" Dale's voice cracked.

"I've been exterminating dimensional pests for six years.

I saved this county. I saved this state.

You can't just take it away.

"

"We don't take it away," the man said. "We downgrade it.

You become Unlicensed.

You become a hazard. And hazards are removed.

"

The word *removed* hung in the air, heavy and final.

Dale looked at the bone clipboard. He looked at the empty air where his tools should be. He looked at the man with the mist-filled eyes.

"Then tell the Archivist," Dale said, "that I have a loophole."

The man tilted his head.

"Loopholes are for lawyers. You are a technician."

"I am a technician who knows his own equipment," Dale said.

He pointed to the obsidian cabinets.

"These cabinets.

They're made of glass.

Obsidian glass.

"

"Yes."

"Glass is porous," Dale said. "At a microscopic level, if not a macroscopic one.

If I touch the cabinet, I can leave a trace.

A residue.

I can mark it."

The man frowned.

"That is vandalism."

"No," Dale said. "It's documentation. I'm marking a specimen.

I'm creating a record of location. If I mark the cabinet, I establish presence.

If I establish presence, I'm not 'interfering.' I'm 'occupying space.' Occupying space is a right of any licensed individual on duty.

"

The man's misty eyes swirled faster.

"You are arguing semantics."

"I am arguing protocol," Dale said. "Show me the rule for 'Marking'."

The man waved a hand. A new text block appeared. *PROTOCOL 12-C: Field Technicians may use non-lethal marking agents to identify infestation boundaries.*

"Is my finger a marking agent?" Dale asked.

"No. Fingers are biological contaminants. You cannot use bare skin to mark official records."

Dale smiled. A small, dry, dangerous smile.

"I'm not using my finger," Dale said. "I'm using my breath."

The man paused.

"Explain."

"Breath contains moisture and carbon dioxide," Dale said. "It's a chemical agent. It's non-lethal.

It's natural.

And it's part of my body. If I breathe on the glass, I am marking the boundary.

I am asserting my presence in this space without using a tool.

I am not using government property. I am using my own physiology.

"

The man stared at him. The silence stretched. The hum of the cabinets seemed to grow louder.

"That is... technically correct," the man admitted.

"Then let me mark the cabinet," Dale said. "Let me establish that I am here.

If I am here, I am not gone.

If I am not gone, I am not violating the temporal law.

I am simply present.

"

The man looked at the Archivist's office door at the end of the hall. It was massive, black iron, with a single keyhole.

"If you mark the cabinet," the man said, "the Archivist will see the mark. And he will know you are trying to trick him."

"Trick him?" Dale said. "No. I'm following the rules.

You can't deny me the right to breathe. That's a human rights violation.

And the Authority prides itself on its adherence to universal laws.

"

The man hesitated. Then, slowly, he nodded.

"Proceed."

Dale stepped forward. He approached the nearest obsidian cabinet. He could feel the cold radiating off it.

He leaned in, and he exhaled slowly, deliberately, focusing on the surface where his breath fogged.

For a second, nothing happened.

Then, the fog didn't fade. It stayed. It etched itself into the glass, forming a perfect, white square. A seal.

The bone clipboard in the man's hand snapped in half.

The man looked down at his broken clipboard. Then he looked at Dale.

"You've triggered a System Error," the man said.

His voice was no longer gravelly. It was terrified.

"You've created a permanent record of your presence in a non-existent timeline.

The Authority doesn't know how to classify you anymore.

"

Dale stepped back.

"Classify me however you want. I'm still licensed. I'm still on duty."

The man backed away.

"The Archivist is coming."

"Good," Dale said. "I have a report to file."

The iron door at the end of the hall groaned. It swung open.

Standing in the doorway was not a monster. Not a god.

It was a child.

A young girl, no older than ten, wearing a miniature version of the Authority's grey jumpsuit. She held a clipboard made of light. Her eyes were the same swirling mist as the clerk's, but deeper, darker.

She looked at Dale. She looked at the fog-mark on the cabinet.

"Mark accepted," the girl said. Her voice was sweet, innocent. "But your license is suspended."

Dale stiffened.

"On what grounds?"

"The mark is unauthorized," the girl said. "It wasn't signed. It wasn't dated. It lacks a witness signature."

"I am the witness," Dale said.

"You cannot witness yourself," the girl said. "That is a conflict of interest."

Dale felt the air leave his lungs. He looked at the clerk. The clerk was gone. The corridor was empty except for Dale and the girl.

"Then who witnesses me?" Dale asked.

The girl smiled. It was the same smile the older Dale had worn in the elevator.

"Me," the girl said. "But I am not a technician. I am the Examiner."

She raised her hand. A pen appeared in her fingers. It was made of light.

"Sign here," she said, pointing to the air next to the fog-mark.

Dale looked at the signature line.

If he signed, he acknowledged the violation, and he admitted he had tampered with time. He lost his certification.

He became Unlicensed.

If he didn't sign, he remained in limbo. He couldn't leave. He couldn't work. He was stuck in this hallway forever.

"There is another option," Dale said.

The girl tilted her head.

"Name it."

"Appoint a witness," Dale said. "Someone who was present when I entered the elevator. Someone who can attest that I was acting under duress."

"Who?" the girl asked.

"The older version of me," Dale said. "The one in the elevator.

He saw me enter, and he knew I was confused. He can testify that I was acting in self-defense against a dimensional anomaly.

"

The girl's smile vanished.

"That entity has been erased," she said.

"He was a Level IX Licensee," Dale said.

"His testimony carries weight. Even if he's gone, his record remains.

Pull his file.

Check the Thoroughness Rating. It was 10/10. A perfect score.

A perfect score means no errors. No errors means no violations.

If he says I was acting correctly, then I was acting correctly.

"

The girl looked at the fog-mark. She looked at Dale.

"You are trying to use a dead man's reputation to save your own license."

"I'm using the System," Dale said. "That's what it's for."

The girl raised her pen. She wrote a single word in the air.

*REJECTED.*

The word burned itself into Dale's vision.

"Your application for a witness is denied," the girl said.

"The older version of you is not a Licensee. He is an Infestation.

A temporal parasite. And you are being charged with harboring a parasite.

"

Dale's blood ran cold.

"Harboring?"

"You entered the elevator with him," the girl said. "You traveled with him.

You were in proximity, which is a Class IV violation. Contamination.

"

The floor beneath Dale's feet cracked. The obsidian cabinets shattered, one by one, exploding into black dust.

"You have ten seconds to surrender," the girl said. "If you do not, you will be exterminated. As a pest."

Dale looked at the cracks spreading across the floor. He looked at the girl. He looked at his empty hands.

He needed a tool. He needed a protocol.

He remembered the white file. The photo. *YOU WERE HERE. BEFORE.*

He remembered the phrase. *First to sign.*

Dale closed his eyes. He didn't look at the girl. He didn't look at the cracks.

He looked inward, and he thought about the smell of antiseptic. The sound of a spray can clicking. The satisfaction of a clean, sealed crack.

He opened his eyes.

"I invoke Protocol 1-A," Dale said. "Self-Isolation."

The girl laughed.

"That's for containment breaches. You are not breached."

"I am a breach," Dale said. "I am the violation. I am the infestation. I am the thing that shouldn't be here."

He stepped forward. Not away from the girl. Toward her.

"If I am the infestation," Dale said, "then you have to treat me. You have to document me. You have to isolate me. You have to eliminate me."

The girl stopped laughing.

"Stay back," she said.

"You can't eliminate what you haven't identified," Dale said.

"And you haven't identified me. I'm not a person.

I'm not a monster.

I'm a Technician.

"

He reached out. His hand passed through the air, but he grabbed nothing. He grabbed the concept.

"I am the protocol," Dale said. "And protocols don't die. They wait."

The girl raised her pen. She tried to write.

But the pen shattered.

The light in her eyes flickered.

"System Error," she whispered.

The world went white.

Dale felt a pull. A tug at his navel. A sensation of being sucked through a straw.

He screamed. Not in pain. In recognition.

He knew this feeling, which was the feeling of waking up from a nightmare.

He opened his eyes.

He was standing in the basement of the duplex. The crawlspace.

The broodmother was dead. The giant rats were dead. The acid beetles were dead.

Dale was holding his clipboard. His pen was in his hand.

The woman in the grey suit—the inspector from the previous chapter—was standing over him. She was holding a file.

"You're alive," she said. Her face was pale.

Dale looked at his clipboard.

There was a new entry in his Field Guide.

*Species: Archivist Variant.* *Weakness: Paradoxical Identification.* *Status: UNDOCUMENTED.*

He looked up at the woman.

"Did you see me?" Dale asked.

The woman shook her head.

"I saw you standing here.

You were screaming.

For three seconds.

Then you stopped.

"

Dale looked at his hands. They were shaking.

He looked at the file in the woman's hand.

"Give me that," Dale said.

The woman hesitated, then handed it over.

Dale opened it.

Inside was not a photo, which was a contract.

Signed by Dale Kowalski.

Dated tomorrow.

And the signature was not his.

It was the signature of the girl.

Beneath the signature, a single line of text:

**CERTIFICATION LEVEL: IX.**

**STATUS: ACTIVE.**

Dale dropped the file.

The elevator doors behind him opened.

The grey-suited man was waiting.

But this time, he wasn't smiling, and he was holding a spray can.

And it was full.
