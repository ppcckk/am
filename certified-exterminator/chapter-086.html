Chapter 86: Regulator Unlicensed

# Chapter 86

Dale Kowalski did not blink. He did not raise his voice. He simply lowered his clipboard to waist height, held it out with the steady hand of a man who had filled out ten thousand inspection forms, and waited.

The man in the suit did not move. The briefcase remained open, the geometric rows of pulsating black eggs glistening under the fluorescent hum of the breakroom lights. The air smelled of ozone and old paper.

"I don’t have a license," the man said. His voice was smooth, devoid of the grit that usually accompanied Authority notifications.

"We are the Authority.

We issue licenses. We do not require them for our own personnel."

Dale felt the corner of his mouth twitch.

A violation. A procedural error.

If the Authority issued licenses, they were bound by the same statutory framework that governed Class II technicians.

You couldn’t just show up, declare yourself the regulator, and skip the paperwork; that was how the German cockroach infestations started. Someone assumed the rules didn’t apply to them because they were too big or too fast, and then the walls started eating the drywall.

"Then you’re operating without proper documentation," Dale said. He tapped the pen against the clipboard.

"Section 4, Paragraph B of the Licensing Act.

Any entity granting certification authority must display valid credentials and a current Thoroughness Rating.

If you don’t have a rating, you’re not the Authority. You’re a contractor.

And if you’re a contractor, you’re unlicensed."

The man’s smile didn’t waver, but his eyes narrowed. "You’re stalling, Dale. You know what’s in the briefcase."

"I know what a nest looks like," Dale said. "I also know what a bait station looks like. Right now, this briefcase is an unauthorized attractant. If I were to file a complaint right now, based on the visible egg count and the lack of containment, you’d be shut down before you finished your pitch."

Alderman, standing three feet behind Dale, let out a choked sob. "Dale, please. Just take the job. They’ll kill us."

Dale didn’t look back. He kept his eyes on the man.

"They won’t kill me.

They need me to document the infestation. If they kill me, the field data is lost. They’re not killers.

They’re bureaucrats.

And you don’t fire the person who keeps your books balanced."

The man in the suit reached into his pocket.

Dale tensed, hand hovering over the spray can at his hip. It was empty, but the motion was instinctive. The man pulled out a small, laminated card.

It was black, with gold lettering that seemed to shift when viewed from different angles.

He flipped it over. On the back was a QR code and a certification number.

"Level X," the man said. "Certified by the Old Guard. Predating your current system iteration. We don’t need a Thoroughness Rating because we set the standard."

Dale squinted at the card. He didn’t have the System ability to read it yet. His vision blurred slightly as he tried to focus on the gold text, and the System interface flickered in his peripheral vision, trying to parse the data.

*Unrecognized Certification Level. Cross-Contamination Risk Detected.*

A red warning flashed across his retinas.

Dale stepped back.

"You’re not the Authority," he said, his voice dropping an octave.

"The Authority uses a decimal-based rating system for Thoroughness. A Level X certification doesn’t exist in our database.

It’s a fabrication."

"It’s an expansion," the man said. "The System is growing, Dale.

And you’re obsolete.

We’re offering you a promotion. Class III. Maybe IV.

Do you know what Class III unlocks?

Area-of-effect extermination. You won’t have to treat individual nests.

You’ll treat whole blocks.

Whole cities. Instantly."

Dale looked at the briefcase. The eggs were vibrating faster now.

A low hum emanated from them, a sound that vibrated in Dale’s teeth.

He knew that frequency, which was the sound of a chitinous carapace grinding together.

He looked at his Field Guide. No entry. No weakness.

But he had something else. He had the clipboard.

"Show me your inspection history," Dale said.

The man paused. "What?"

"If you’re hiring me, you’re hiring me as a consultant. I need to know your last ten audits. Your error rates.

Your complaint log. If your error rate is above 5%, I’m walking out.

I don’t work with sloppy operators."

The man’s composure cracked. Just a fraction. A micro-expression of irritation.

"We don’t have error rates.

We have outcomes."

"Outcomes are the result of process," Dale said. "If the process is flawed, the outcome is temporary. You’re trying to sell me a solution, but you haven’t shown me the methodology. That’s a violation of professional standards."

Dale took a step forward. He wasn’t looking at the man anymore. He was looking at the briefcase. Specifically, at the latch.

The latch was broken. The plastic hinge was cracked, held together by a piece of industrial adhesive. The seal was compromised.

Dale’s mind raced.

The System rewarded identification. He had identified the object. He had identified the structural failure.

But he hadn’t identified the species, and he needed to trigger a System response without engaging the creature.

He reached into his pocket and pulled out a small, sealed sample vial; it was empty, but it was clean. Sterile. He uncapped it.

The man in the suit frowned. "What are you doing?"

"Collecting a sample," Dale said. "For lab analysis."

He didn’t approach the briefcase. Instead, he took a piece of tape from his belt, slapped it onto the broken latch, and then used his pen to poke a tiny hole in the adhesive seal.

It was a stupid move. It was dangerous. It was exactly what a pest control technician would do to verify a seal failure without opening the package.

The moment the air hit the eggs, the System pinged.

*Field Guide Entry Unlocked: Dimensional Broker (Homo sapiens mimic, variant).*

The text burned into Dale’s vision.

*Weakness: Bureaucratic Rigidity. The entity cannot process illogical procedural deviations. It requires a clear chain of command to act.*

Dale froze.

The man in the suit blinked. He looked down at the briefcase, then at Dale. The eggs stopped humming.

"What did you do?" the man whispered.

"I identified a procedural anomaly," Dale said.

His voice was deadpan.

"You’re not the Authority. You’re a middle manager trying to bypass procurement rules.

And you’re bad at it."

The man’s face contorted. The skin around his eyes rippled, revealing scales beneath the human facade.

He wasn’t fully human, and he was a mimic. A high-level infestation that had learned to wear suits.

"Take the job," the mimic hissed. "Or I will revoke your license permanently."

"You can’t revoke it," Dale said. "You haven’t filed the paperwork."

The mimic lunged.

It didn’t strike with claws or teeth. It struck with red tape.

A wave of red light erupted from the briefcase, not an attack, but a barrier. A containment field. The air inside the circle turned thick, viscous, and red.

*Status Effect: Administrative Hold. Movement Speed reduced by 80%. Verbal Communication locked.*

Dale tried to speak, and he couldn’t. His jaw locked.

The System had frozen him.

Not physically, but legally. The mimic had invoked a protocol Dale didn’t know existed. An override command.

He looked at Alderman. Alderman was screaming, but no sound came out. The red light had swallowed him too.

Dale struggled. His muscles burned.

He was fighting against the System itself, and he looked at the clipboard.

The pen was still in his hand.

He couldn’t move. He couldn’t speak. But he could write.

The System allowed for administrative input even during a hold. That was a loophole. You could file a complaint while under arrest. You could appeal a decision while in custody.

Dale focused on the clipboard. His hand shook, but he forced the pen to the paper.

He didn’t write a counter-attack. He didn’t write a spell.

He wrote a citation.

*Violation: Unauthorized use of Administrative Hold without proper jurisdiction. Ref: Code 77-B. Immediate Action Required: Release of Subject.*

He wrote it fast. He wrote it with the precision of a man who had written a thousand cease-and-desist letters. He added his license number.

He added the date. He added the witness statement (Alderman, present, distressed).

He signed it.

*Submitted.*

The red light flickered.

The mimic stared at the clipboard.

Its eyes widened. It had expected Dale to fight. It had expected Dale to run.

It had not expected Dale to audit him.

The System interface pulsed.

*Complaint Received. Processing...*

The red light intensified, then shattered. The pressure lifted from Dale’s chest. He gasped, air rushing into his lungs.

The mimic stumbled back, clutching its head. "You... you filed a grievance?"

"It’s in the manual," Dale said. He straightened his tie.

"Page 402.

Rights of the Accused Technician. You can’t hold someone if you can’t prove the infestation is active. And you haven’t.

You’re just talking."

The mimic snarled.

The scales rippled across its face again.

"You’re making a mistake, Dale. This isn’t a game.

This is a takeover."

"Takeovers require a vote," Dale said. He held up the clipboard. "And you don’t have the quorum."

The mimic looked at the briefcase. The eggs were cracking.

Not from Dale’s attack, but from their own pressure, and the mimic had lost control; the containment field had failed because the System had flagged the hold as illegal.

The briefcase exploded.

Not with fire. With mass.

Thousands of tiny, black larvae shot out of the case like shrapnel.

They hit the floor, the walls, the ceiling. They didn’t bounce.

They stuck, weaving into the drywall, the carpet, and the air.

The mimic screamed. It wasn’t a human scream. It was the sound of a hive mind fragmenting.

"Containment!" Alderman yelled, finally able to speak. "Dale, do something!"

Dale didn’t run. He didn’t shoot.

He looked at the chaos, and the larvae were moving fast, but they were disorganized.

They had no queen. The mimic was trying to re-establish control, but the Administrative Hold had severed its link to the swarm.

Dale looked at his Field Guide. The entry for *Dimensional Broker* was updating.

*New Weakness Discovered: Hive Dependency. If the leader is disconnected from the swarm, the swarm defaults to survival mode, and they will flee if the threat exceeds their capacity for replication.*

Dale looked at the spray can at his hip. It was empty. He looked at the fire extinguisher.

It was empty. He looked at the clipboard.

He looked at the mimic, who was now on his knees, trying to command the larvae. They were ignoring him. They were looking at Dale.

Dale realized something.

The mimic wasn’t trying to hire him. The mimic was trying to merge with him. The offer of certification wasn’t a reward.

It was a trap.

The Authority wasn’t a bureaucracy. It was a parasite.

And it had been feeding on Dale’s need for order.

Every time Dale followed the protocol, every time he documented, he was feeding the hive. His thoroughness was the glue that held the infestation together.

He looked at the notification. *License Revocation Pending.*

It wasn’t a punishment. It was a severance. The Authority was cutting him off because he was becoming a threat to the hive mind.

Dale looked at Alderman. "Get to the car."

"What?"

"Get to the car. Now."

Dale reached into his vest. He pulled out his personal inspection seal. A small, red rubber stamp. He used it to stamp the center of the clipboard.

*DENIED.*

He threw the clipboard at the mimic.

The mimic caught it, confused. The stamp landed on its chest.

The System pinged.

*Action: Rejection of Contract. Effect: Hive Mind Disruption. The broker is now marked as 'Non-Compliant.' Swarm aggression increased by 200%.*

The larvae turned on the mimic, and they swarmed him, biting, tearing, consuming their own leader. The mimic shrieked, dissolving into a pile of black goo and chitinous shards.

Dale didn’t watch. He turned and ran.

He ran past the breakroom, past the hallway, toward the exit. His heart was pounding, but his mind was clear. He had broken the contract. He had rejected the Authority.

But as he burst out the door into the night, the sky wasn’t dark.

The Rift was open. But it wasn’t spilling monsters.

It was spilling paper.

Millions of forms. Red tape. Certificates.

Permits. They fluttered down like snow, covering the street, covering the cars, covering the screaming survivors.

And on the ground, crawling out of the cracks in the asphalt, were not rats.

They were auditors.

Humanoid figures in gray suits, with faces made of shredded documents. They stood up, brushing paper off their shoulders. They looked at Dale.

There were hundreds of them.

Dale stopped. He looked at his Field Guide.

*New Species Detected: Regulatory Enforcer (Bureaucratic Construct).*

*Weakness: None. They are the law.*

One of the enforcers stepped forward. It held out a hand. In its palm was a pen.

"Sign here," it said. "Your license renewal is due."

Dale looked at the pen. He looked at the thousands of enforcers. He looked at his hands, trembling with adrenaline.

He had one option. He could run. He could fight. He could hide.

Or he could sign.

If he signed, he remained compliant. He remained part of the hive. He survived.

If he didn’t, he was an outlier. And outliers were exterminated.

Dale took the pen. He looked at the enforcer. He looked at the clipboard in his mind, the one he had thrown, now gone.

He smiled, a dry, thin stretch of lips.

"I don’t sign," Dale said. "I certify."

He pulled the pen back, and he didn’t sign the form.

He crossed it out.

The enforcer tilted its head. The paper skin rustled.

"Penalty," it said.

"Acceptable," Dale said.

The enforcer raised its hand. The air around Dale started to warp. The ground beneath his feet turned to ink.

Dale closed his eyes. He waited for the first hit.

And then he heard a voice in his head. Not the System. Not the Authority.

A voice that sounded like his old supervisor, the one who had fired him for refusing to fake a report.

*"You’re on your own, Dale. Welcome to the union."*
