Chapter 17

The nozzle screamed. Martinez had the rig set to maximum pressure and the Class III compound hit the foundation like a fire hose, foaming white across the siding and the porch steps. Three of the creatures peeled away from the wall, their chitinous legs scrabbling on the wet surface. The other two kept pressing.

Dale counted them. Three flushed, two holding.

He knew what this was. He'd seen it a hundred times in residential work. You spray a colony without sealing the perimeter and you don't kill them. You relocate them. You make them angry. You make them find new entry points in the walls of the house next door.

"Martinez." Dale's voice was flat. Professional. The tone he used when a homeowner asked if he could just "spray a little." "You're flushing."

Martinez didn't look up. The spray rig was forty pounds on his back and his arms were locked at the elbows, the nozzle tracking the two creatures still pressed against the house. "They're pinned. Two left. I'm going to pin them and treat."

"They're not pinned. They're testing." Dale stepped closer. His clipboard was in his left hand. His right hand held a pencil. He was writing. "Watch the one on the left. It's not pressing in. It's pressing *out*. It's measuring the wall."

The creature on the left had stopped pushing. It stood on its hind legs, front claws flat against the siding, and its head moved in a slow arc. Left to right. Right to left. Like a technician checking for gaps.

Dale wrote: *Behavioral note: directional scanning. Not aggressive. Locational.*

Martinez adjusted his aim. "You're in my workspace."

"I'm in support."

"Support means you don't interfere."

Dale looked at the Authority notification again. He'd been reading it since Martinez took the job. JOB STATUS: TRANSFERRED. ROLE: SUPPORT. He'd read it four times now. Each time, the text was the same. But the fourth time, he noticed something he hadn't seen before. A small line of text beneath the role designation, in the same bureaucratic font, so small it looked like a footnote.

SUPPORT PROTOCOL: Maintain workspace integrity. Authority to issue Stop-Work Order if workspace fails to meet minimum operational standards.

He read it again.

Then he looked at Martinez's spray pattern. The compound was pooling in the gap between the foundation and the siding. A two-inch gap. The same gap he'd identified on his initial inspection. The same gap he'd written up as a structural entry point. The compound was flowing into it. Downward. Into the crawlspace.

Dale's stomach dropped.

"You're contaminating the substrate," he said.

Martinez shut off the nozzle. The silence that followed was the kind of silence that happens right before someone says something stupid. "What?"

"The Class III compound is solvent-based. You know that. It's on the label. It breaks down dimensional residue. But it also breaks down the sealant on the foundation." Dale pointed at the gap. "That gap is the only thing separating the exterior infestation from the interior substrate. You just poured forty gallons of solvent into it."

Martinez looked at the gap. The foam was draining down into it, disappearing into the dark space between the house and the ground.

"I'm going to need you to stand down," Martinez said. His voice was careful. The voice of a man deciding whether to escalate.

Dale held up his clipboard. The Authority notification glowed in his peripheral vision, and he could see the SUPPORT PROTOCOL line pulsing faintly, like a highlighter mark.

"Stop-Work Order," Dale said.

Martinez stared at him.

Dale didn't explain. He didn't argue. He held the clipboard out like a parking ticket. The Authority recognized it. A second notification appeared, overlapping the first.

STOP-WORK ORDER ISSUED. TECHNICIAN: KOWALSKI, D. (SUPPORT). REASON: SUBSTRATE CONTAMINATION RISK. AUTHORITY: SUPPORT PROTOCOL, SECTION 4.2.

Martinez's spray rig powered down with a hiss. The Class III compound in the nozzle stopped flowing. He looked at his rig, then at Dale, then at the notification. His jaw worked.

"You're going to have to justify this," Martinez said.

"I already did. Section 4.2. Substrate contamination risk. You're pouring solvent into a structural gap that separates an active infestation from an uninfested interior space. If the substrate gets contaminated, every entry point in the house becomes compromised. You don't have five creatures. You'll have twenty."

Martinez set the spray rig down. He pulled out his own clipboard. He started writing. Dale knew what he was writing. A rebuttal. A complaint. A note to the Authority that the support technician was interfering with a Class IV operation.

It didn't matter. The Stop-Work Order was already logged. The Authority had it. Martinez could file his complaint, but the order stood until the workspace was cleared.

Dale walked to the foundation. He knelt beside the gap. The foam was still draining, but slower now. The compound was already in the crawlspace. He couldn't undo that. But he could assess the damage.

He pulled his flashlight from his belt. He clicked it on. He put his face close to the gap and shone the beam inside.

The crawlspace was dark. The flashlight beam caught the underside of the floor joists, the vapor barrier, the dirt floor. The Class III compound had pooled in a shallow depression near the center of the house. It was dissolving the vapor barrier. The plastic sheeting was bubbling and shrinking, revealing bare earth beneath.

Dale watched the earth.

Something moved.

Not a creature. The ground itself. The dirt was *breathing*. A slow expansion and contraction, like a lung. The compound was pooling directly over it. The solvent was eating through the last layer of sealant, and the breathing was getting faster.

He pulled his flashlight back. He stood up.

"The infestation isn't in the house," Dale said. "It's under it."

Martinez had stopped writing. He looked at Dale.

"The creatures aren't trying to get in," Dale said. "They're trying to get *down*. They're trying to reach the substrate breach. The gap in the foundation isn't an entry point. It's an *access point*. They've been using it to feed whatever's down there."

He looked at his Field Guide. The entry for Dimensional Vermin was still there, populated from the first job. He flipped through the pages. Rattus profundus. Acid beetles. The broodmother. All Class I. All from the first Rift event.

He flipped to the back. The pages were blank. No entry for the creatures Martinez was dealing with. No entry for whatever was breathing under the house.

He looked at the notification. Seventy-two hours. Five Class III creatures. No Field Guide entry. No damage bonus. No protocol.

But he had something else. He had a gap in the foundation, a breathing substrate, and a behavioral note.

*Locational.*

He pulled out his pencil. He wrote on his clipboard: *Hypothesis: substrate breach is a secondary nest site. The creatures are not attacking the house. They are tending the nest. Eliminating the creatures without addressing the nest will result in replacement within six weeks.*

He knew that number. Six weeks. He'd seen it a hundred times. You kill the workers, the queen replaces them. You spray the colony, the colony splits. You treat the symptom, the disease wins.

He looked at Martinez. "You need to treat the substrate, not the creatures."

Martinez looked at the house. He looked at his spray rig. He looked at Dale.

"I don't have a protocol for substrate breaches," Martinez said.

"Neither do I." Dale flipped his clipboard to a fresh page. "But I know how to find one."

He walked to the corner of the house. He crouched. He put his ear against the foundation. He listened.

The breathing was slow. Maybe four seconds between expansions. He counted. He wrote the number down. He moved to the next corner. Same rhythm. He moved to the midpoint of the north wall. The rhythm was faster. Three seconds. He moved to the midpoint of the south wall. Two seconds.

He was mapping it. The same way he'd map a termite trail. Follow the activity, find the concentration, locate the source.

He walked the perimeter. He wrote numbers. He stopped at the east wall, near the back porch. The breathing was fast. One and a half seconds. The foundation was warm under his palm. Not hot. Warm. Like a living thing.

He knelt. He put his ear to the concrete.

The breathing was different here. It wasn't expanding and contracting. It was *inhaling*. A slow, deliberate draw of air from the crawlspace through the gap in the foundation. The gap Martinez had just poured solvent into.

Dale stood up. He looked at Martinez.

"It's not a nest," Dale said. "It's a lung."

Martinez didn't answer. He was looking at his own clipboard. He was reading something.

"What?" Dale asked.

Martinez turned the clipboard around. On it was a new Authority notification, one Dale couldn't see from his angle. Martinez's face was the face of a man who had just been told he was wrong and didn't know how to say it.

"Substrate Breach confirmed," Martinez read. "Classification: Level IV. Designation: Dimensional Anchor. Status: Active. Threat: The anchor is feeding. The creatures are maintaining it."

Dale felt something cold move through his chest. Not fear. Recognition. He'd seen this before. Not in a monster. In a house. A house with a leaking pipe behind the drywall. The water damage wasn't the problem. The pipe was the problem. The creatures were the water damage.

"What's the anchor?" Dale asked.

Martinez looked at him. "The Authority doesn't classify anchors. It classifies infestations. This is the first time they've used the term."

Dale looked at the house. The creatures had stopped pressing against the wall. They were still there, but they'd gone quiet. Waiting. Like a colony that knows the technician has left and is settling back into its routine.

He looked at his clipboard. The Thoroughness Rating from the last job was still visible. 6.4. Below threshold. Seventy-two hours.

He looked at Martinez. "If you kill the creatures now, what happens to the anchor?"

Martinez didn't answer. He didn't need to. The answer was in the breathing. The anchor was feeding on something, and the creatures were the delivery mechanism. Remove the delivery, and the anchor would find another way to feed. Or it would grow. Or it would open wider.

Dale wrote on his clipboard: *Protocol required: Isolate anchor before eliminating creatures. If eliminated without isolation, anchor may escalate to Level VII within 48 hours.*

He didn't know if that was true. He didn't have a Field Guide entry. He didn't have a protocol. But he had fourteen years of knowing that if you don't treat the source, the problem comes back bigger.

He looked at the notification. JOB STATUS: TRANSFERRED. He was support. He had authority to stop work, not to direct it.

He looked at Martinez. "You need to seal the gap before you treat the creatures. You need to starve the anchor before you kill the delivery system."

Martinez picked up his spray rig. He looked at the nozzle. He looked at the gap. He looked at Dale.

"If I seal the gap and the creatures can't feed the anchor," Martinez said slowly, "what does the anchor do?"

Dale thought about it. He thought about every infestation he'd ever encountered where the source was cut off. Termites with no wood. Ants with no food. Rats with no entry point.

They dig," Dale said. "They find a new way."

They looked at the house. The breathing was still there. Slow. Patient. Waiting.

Dale pulled out his pencil. He wrote: *Contingency: If anchor is starved, anticipate new entry points within 24 hours. Monitor all structural interfaces.*

He looked at Martinez. "We need to seal the gap, treat the creatures, and be ready for the anchor to find another way in. All three, in that order."

Martinez set the spray rig down again. He pulled out a roll of expanding foam from his truck. He looked at Dale.

"You're going to need to show me where to apply it," Martinez said.

It wasn't an apology. It wasn't a concession. It was a technician recognizing that another technician knew something he didn't. Dale had seen that look before. On plumbers. On electricians. On the one time a general contractor had asked him to point out the ant trails in a kitchen he'd already inspected.

Dale nodded. He walked to the gap. He pointed.

Martinez knelt. He started applying foam.

Dale watched. He wrote. He counted the creatures. Three. Still present. Waiting.

The foam expanded. The gap sealed. The breathing slowed.

One creature moved. It walked to the sealed gap. It pressed its face against the foam. It pushed. The foam held.

The creature turned. It looked at the house. It looked at the other side of the yard. It started walking.

Dale watched it go. He wrote: *First relocation confirmed. Monitoring required.*

Then the second creature moved. And the third. They followed the first. Away from the house. Away from the sealed gap.

Dale looked at Martinez. "They're leaving."

Martinez looked up from the foam. His face was careful. "That's not good."

"No. It's not good. They're looking for a new entry point." Dale looked at the yard. The neighbors' houses. The fence line. The storm drain at the end of the street. "And they're going to find one."

Martinez stood up. He looked at his spray rig. He looked at the empty gap. He looked at the three creatures disappearing into the dark between houses.

"I can't chase them," Martinez said. "My job is this house."

"Your job is this house," Dale agreed. "But the anchor is still under it."

They both looked at the ground. The breathing was faint now. Almost stopped. The anchor was waiting.

Then Dale's notification changed.

JOB STATUS: REVOKED. NEW LEAD TECHNICIAN: MARTINEZ, R. (CLASS IV). ROLE: SUPPORT.

Martinez looked at his own notification. His face went pale.

"They're pulling me off," Martinez said.

Dale read his own notification. It wasn't the same as Martinez's.

NEW ASSIGNMENT: KOWALSKI, D. (CLASS II). ROLE: LEAD TECHNICIAN. TASK: LOCATE AND NEUTRALIZE DIMENSIONAL ANCHOR. DEADLINE: 48 HOURS.

He looked at Martinez. Martinez looked at him.

"They gave you the anchor," Martinez said.

"They gave me the anchor," Dale confirmed.

He looked at the house. He looked at the sealed gap. He looked at the ground where the breathing had been.

He pulled out his clipboard. He turned to a fresh page. He wrote: *Protocol: Unknown. Target: Unknown. Entry: Unknown.*

Then he wrote: *Begin.*

He walked toward the back porch. The breathing was gone. The ground was still. But the warmth was still there, under his feet, and it was moving. Not under the house anymore.

Under the yard.

Under the street.

Under the storm drain at the end of the block.

Dale stopped walking. He looked at the drain. The grate was rattling. Slowly. Rhythmically.

Like something breathing.

Like something waking up.
