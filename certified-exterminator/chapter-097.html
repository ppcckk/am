Chapter 97: Accusation Is Grammar

# Chapter 97

The hand did not pull him in. It pinned him.

The chitinous limb locked onto the bed rail of Miller’s pickup, the human fingers at its tip curling over the metal with a wet, snapping sound. The crate had split like a ripe melon, spilling a mass of segmented legs and pulsating, translucent sacs into the truck bed. The air smelled of copper and ozone, a scent Dale associated with high-voltage shorts and old blood.

Miller was screaming. Not words.

Just a raw, animal shriek that cut through the sudden stillness of the industrial park.

He fumbled for the ignition, his keys slipping from sweaty palms. The engine coughed, died, coughed again.

Dale did not scream. He watched the leg. He watched the joints.

*Six segments,* he noted. *Articulated. Load-bearing.*

The Authority’s notification hadn’t appeared.

No text flashed. No chime sounded. Just the black sky, the crawling void above, and the creature trying to climb into the cab.

This was the anomaly.

Miller had said the Authority called it a visitor. Not an infestation. The distinction was bureaucratic, but in Dale’s world, it was lethal.

Infestations had protocols. Visitors had rights.

The creature’s other hands emerged from the shattered crate, and they were pale, fleshy, and twitching.

They looked like the hands of the people missing from the subway station, but wrong, and the skin was too smooth.

The nails were fused into the fingertips.

Miller kicked the door open, tumbling out of the truck. He scrambled backward, eyes wide, pointing at Dale. "Get out! It’s grabbing you!"

Dale didn’t move. He stood on the running board, one foot in the mud, one foot on the truck bed.

He had his clipboard, and he had his pen. He had his eyes.

He focused on the primary limb—the one gripping the rail.

The Authority’s cross-contamination rule stated that deploying a protocol for one species against another caused a debuff. But Dale had no protocol active.

He had no Field Guide entry for this creature, and the entry was blank. Unknown.

But the Field Guide wasn’t just a bestiary. It was a diagnostic tool.

Dale scribbled furiously. *Subject: Unidentified. Morphology: Hybrid.

Chitin-analogous exoskeleton.

Soft-tissue extremities.

Primary threat: Physical restraint via digit-lock.*

The pen scratched against the paper. As he wrote the classification, a faint grey text shimmered into existence over the creature’s main leg.

**FIELD GUIDE ENTRY: PRELIMINARY DRAFT.** **STATUS: UNVERIFIED.** **WEAKNESS DETECTED: ARTICULATION POINT 3.**

Dale blinked. He hadn’t identified a weakness through combat. He had identified it through documentation.

The System rewarded precision, not power. By writing down exactly what he saw, he had forced the System to acknowledge the data.

The creature lunged. The hand shot toward Dale’s face.

Dale didn’t dodge, and he stepped back, letting the hand miss his nose by inches; the wind of its motion ruffled his hair. He looked at the joint between the third and fourth segments of the chitinous leg.

A seam was visible.

A gap where the armor plates overlapped, filled with a darker, softer membrane.

"Miller," Dale said. His voice was flat. "The truck’s hydraulic line."

Miller, crouched on the asphalt ten feet away, looked up, confused. "What?"

"The brake line. It’s severed. Look at the fluid."

Miller looked. Dark red oil pooled under the rear axle.

"If I cut the line," Dale said, "the pressure drops. The bed tilts. The crate slides."

"Why would you—"

"Because gravity is a universal constant, and this thing is trying to climb up. If the angle changes, its grip fails."

Dale reached into his belt and pulled out his multi-tool. It was a standard-issue technician’s tool, not a weapon. It had a wire cutter.

The creature’s head emerged from the crate. It had no eyes. Just a smooth, chitinous dome and a mouth that opened horizontally, revealing rows of needle-like teeth. It shrieked, a sound like grinding glass.

Dale dropped to one knee. He wasn’t fighting the monster. He was fixing the vehicle.

He jammed the wire cutter into the exposed hydraulic line. *Snip.*

A hiss of pressurized fluid sprayed across the truck bed. The rear end of the pickup dropped an inch. Then two. The bed, already listing from the weight of the crate, shifted further.

The creature’s hand, anchored to the rail, lost its leverage. The angle of the limb changed. The chitinous plate shifted, stressing the joint at articulation point three.

A pop occurred.

The leg buckled.

The creature’s weight shifted, throwing its balance off; it flailed, its other hands scrabbling for purchase on the slick, oil-covered bed. It slipped, sliding backward into the crate.

Dale stood up. He walked to the edge of the bed and looked down, and the creature was thrashing, but it was trapped in the debris.

"It’s not dead," Miller yelled. "Kill it!"

"I can't," Dale said.

He pointed at the empty space in his vision where the Treatment Points counter should have been. "No entry. No points.

And if I use a Class-II insecticide spray on a Class-X anomaly, I contaminate my Guide. I lose my license for six months.

Maybe forever."

Miller stared at him. "Your license? Dale, we’re going to die!"

"Death is a terminal condition," Dale said, checking his watch. "Losing my certification is a career-ending condition. I prefer the former. It’s permanent, but it’s clean."

Above them, the black sky pulsed. The crawling void was getting closer. The clouds didn’t drift; they undulated. Faint, geometric shapes formed within the darkness, rotating slowly.

Miller looked up, his face draining of color. "They’re coming down. Look at the horizon."

To the east, the silhouette of the city skyline was being obscured. Not by fog, but by a wall of chittering static. It sounded like a billion cicadas singing at once.

"We need to go," Miller said. He finally got the engine to turn over. The headlights flickered on, cutting through the gloom.

Dale climbed into the passenger seat. He still held his clipboard. He had written three more lines. *Weakness: Structural instability at joint 3. Environmental hazard: Hydraulic fluid reduces traction.*

He tore the page off the clipboard and handed it to Miller. "Don’t throw it away. This is the only record we have."

Miller took the paper, his hands shaking so hard the paper tore. "This is insane. You’re writing reports while the world ends."

"I’m building a case file," Dale said. "If we survive, I’ll need to justify why I didn’t kill it. If we don’t survive, the Authority will audit my logs. I want the audit to show I was thorough."

Miller gunned the engine. The truck lurched forward, tires spinning in the mud before finding grip. They sped away from the industrial park, leaving the creature’s angry shrieks fading in the rearview mirror.

As they drove, Dale stared out the window. The road was empty. The streetlights were flickering in a rhythmic pattern. *On-off-on-off.* Like a heartbeat.

"Miller," Dale said. "Check the glove box."

"Are you serious? Now?"

"The manual. Page 142. Emergency shutdown for the ignition system."

Miller didn’t ask why. He drove with one hand and rummaged with the other. He found the manual. He flipped to page 142.

"There’s nothing here," Miller said. "Just warranty info."

Dale looked at the manual. The text was there, but it was wrong. The words were shifting. *Warranty* became *Warfare*. *Coverage* became *Corruption*. The ink was crawling across the page.

"Close it," Dale said sharply.

Miller slammed the glove box shut. "What was that?"

"Contamination," Dale said.

"The black sky. It’s not just monsters.

It’s data corruption. The Authority’s network is being overwritten."

Miller gripped the wheel. "So what do we do? We can’t fight data."

"We don’t fight it," Dale said. "We contain it."

He pulled out his phone. No signal.

But the Camera app was open, and he had been taking photos of the truck bed before they left. Photos of the crate. Photos of the leg.

He scrolled through the gallery. In one photo, the creature’s leg was visible. In the background, reflected in the shiny hubcap of the truck, was a figure.

It wasn’t Miller. It wasn’t Dale.

It was a tall, thin figure standing in the shadows of the warehouse. It wore a suit. A sharp, black suit. And it held a clipboard.

Dale zoomed in. The figure’s face was blurred, but the clipboard was clear. It had the same Authority seal as Dale’s.

"Miller," Dale said, his voice dropping to a whisper. "Slow down."

"What?"

"Pull over."

"We can’t! They’re behind us!"

"I know."

Dale pointed at the phone screen. Miller glanced at it, then back at the road. "Who is that?"

"It’s an inspector," Dale said. "A Class V Inspector. They don’t hunt. They audit."

The realization hit Dale with the force of a physical blow.

The creature in the crate hadn’t been attacking them. It had been fleeing.

The black sky wasn’t an invasion, which was a cleanup crew.

The Authority hadn’t changed its mind. It was cleaning house.

And Dale had just witnessed a crime scene.

If the Inspector was auditing, it was looking for violations. Dale’s violation was simple: he had identified a Class-X entity without proper clearance, and he had populated a blank Field Guide entry without Authority authorization.

That was unauthorized practice.

The penalty for unauthorized practice was not death. It was erasure.

The truck skidded as Miller slammed on the brakes. Ahead of them, the road was blocked.

Not by a barricade.

By a wall of black ink.

It rose from the asphalt, forming a barrier ten feet high.

Miller tried to reverse. The tires spun uselessly.

Dale looked at the ink wall. Text appeared on its surface, glowing white.

**VIOLATION: UNLICENSED FIELDWORK.** **SUBJECT: DALE KOWALSKI.** **ACTION: CEASE AND DESIST.**

Miller looked at Dale. "Can you talk to it? You’re the expert."

Dale looked at the wall. He looked at his clipboard. He looked at his pen.

The System rewarded documentation. It rewarded protocol.

If he wrote a formal objection, citing Section 4, Paragraph B of the Licensing Act (Right to Preliminary Identification During Emergent Threat), he might stall the audit.

But he had no proof of emergent threat. He had no witnesses. Miller was a civilian.

Dale looked at Miller. Miller was terrified. Miller was useless.

Dale looked at the ink wall. The text shifted.

**SUBMISSION WINDOW: 30 SECONDS.**

Dale opened his mouth. He didn’t speak. He started writing.

He didn’t write an objection. He wrote a confession.

*I, Dale Kowalski, Class II Technician, hereby report an unauthorized interaction with a Class-X entity. I acknowledge the breach of protocol. I request immediate suspension of my license pending investigation.*

The ink wall pulsed. The white text faded.

*Processing,* the wall seemed to say.

Dale held his breath. He had given up his license. He had admitted guilt.

By the rules, the Authority should stop. The audit would pause while the suspension was processed.

It was a bureaucratic delay.

A loophole.

The wall started dissolving. The ink flowed back into the asphalt.

Miller stared. "You... you gave up your license?"

"I bought us time," Dale said. He felt a hollow ache in his chest. The green light in his peripheral vision—the one that always showed his certification level—flickered and went dark.

He was unlicensed, and he was nobody.

"Drive," Dale said.

Miller slammed the gas. The truck surged forward, crossing the spot where the wall had been.

But as they passed the point of dissolution, the black sky above them tore open. Not a hole. A tear.

Something dropped from the tear. It wasn’t a monster. It was a person.

It landed on the road ahead of them, rolling to stop in a crouch, which stood up. It was tall, wearing the same black suit as the figure in the reflection. Its face was smooth, featureless except for a single, vertical slit where a mouth should be.

It raised a hand. In its hand was a stamp. A large, red rubber stamp.

It stamped the air.

**DENIED.**

The ground beneath the truck’s tires turned to glass. The wheels spun, shrieking against the frictionless surface. The truck fishtailed, sliding sideways toward a concrete pillar.

Miller screamed, bracing for impact.

Dale didn’t brace. He looked at the Inspector.

The Inspector walked toward the truck. It didn’t run. It glided.

Dale opened the door.

"Dale!" Miller yelled. "Stay in the truck!"

Dale ignored him. He stepped out onto the glass road. He walked toward the Inspector.

He had no license. He had no weapons. He had no points.

But he had a clipboard, and he held it out. Blank.

The Inspector stopped ten feet away. It tilted its head.

Dale spoke. "I am invoking the Right to Appeal. Form 77-B. Request for review of Thoroughness Rating prior to termination."

The Inspector paused. The slit in its face widened.

**APPEAL REQUIRES EVIDENCE.**

Dale looked at the truck. He looked at the crate they had left behind, still visible in the distance. He looked at the sky.

"I have no evidence," Dale said. "But I have a theory."

The Inspector waited.

"The creature in the crate wasn’t an infestation.

It was a vessel. A carrier.

And you’re not cleaning an infestation.

You’re covering up a breach. You’re not here to exterminate. You’re here to censor."

The Inspector’s face remained blank. But the red stamp in its hand started glowing.

**FALSE ACCUSATION. PENALTY: IMMEDIATE TERMINATION.**

Dale didn’t flinch. He reached into his pocket and pulled out the torn page from his clipboard; the page where he had written the creature’s weakness.

He threw it.

The paper fluttered through the air. It landed at the Inspector’s feet.

The Inspector looked down. It picked up the paper. It read the text.

*Weakness: Structural instability at joint 3.*

The Inspector looked up. For the first time, something like emotion crossed its featureless face. Confusion.

That weakness was classified. It was in the Authority’s sealed files. No Level II technician could have known it.

Unless the technician had seen the creature’s internal structure.

Unless the technician had seen it *die*.

But the creature hadn’t died. It had been trapped.

Unless...

Dale looked at the sky. The crawling void was spreading faster. It was moving toward the city. Toward the people.

"You didn’t trap it to save it," Dale said. "You trapped it to study it.

And you’re letting it escape now because you want to see what it does to the population.

You’re not exterminators.

You’re experimenters."

The Inspector stepped forward. The glass road cracked beneath its feet.

**DELETION IMMINENT.**

Dale looked at Miller, who was watching through the shattered windshield, tears streaming down his face.

Dale looked back at the Inspector.

"I’m not appealing my license," Dale said. "I’m appealing the System."

He pulled his pen from his pocket. He didn’t write on the clipboard. He wrote on the air.

He drew a line. A straight, black line between the Inspector and the truck.

**CROSS-CONTAMINATION WARNING.**

He had deployed a protocol he didn’t have, and had accused a Class V entity of violating the Thoroughness Scale.

The System didn’t care about truth. It cared about rules.

And the rule for accusing a superior was a mandatory audit of the accuser’s entire history.

The Inspector froze.

The air shimmered.

**AUDIT INITIATED.**

**HOLDING PATTERN: ACTIVE.**

The Inspector was stuck. Caught in a loop of System verification.

Dale had bought them three seconds. Maybe four.

He turned and ran toward the truck.

Miller had the door open. "Get in!"

Dale jumped into the passenger seat. Miller floored the gas. The tires found traction on the cracked glass.

They sped away.

But as they drove, Dale looked at his hands. They were shaking. Not from fear. From realization.

He had triggered an audit. His entire career, every report, every inspection, every mistake, was now being reviewed by the Authority.

And in the middle of that audit, he had just discovered the one thing that made him different from every other pest control technician in the world.

He hadn’t just identified the weakness, and he had identified the *lie*.

The Authority wasn’t trying to stop the monsters.

It was farming them.

And Dale Kowalski was now the only witness who could prove it.

If the audit found him guilty of fabrication, he would be erased.

If it found him innocent, he would be promoted.

Dale looked at the blank page on his clipboard. He picked up his pen.

He started writing the accusation.

And for the first time in fourteen years, he didn’t check his grammar.

The grammar was the point.
