Chapter 54: Blind Amendment

# Chapter 54

The pen was shaking. Dale pressed it harder against the clipboard and the shaking stopped.

Ninety seconds was not a lot of time, but he had written code violations in the middle of active infestations.

He had written them while a broodmother the size of a washing machine was pushing through drywall behind him. He had written them while a man was screaming in the next room and the screaming was getting wetter. Ninety seconds was a luxury.

He looked at the space where the original entry would appear, which was blank, and the Authority had not shown him the entry.

It had granted access but not visibility.

He would have to write the amendment blind.

Forty seconds gone.

Dale thought about what he knew. Not what he felt.

What he knew.

The Authority processed reports.

The Authority awarded points. The Authority maintained the Field Guide and the certification structure and the protocol toolkit.

It was a system. Systems had dependencies. Dependencies were entry points.

Entry points were what he looked for in every inspection, every time, for fourteen years before the Rift, and for however many weeks it had been since.

He knew the Authority's behavior, observing it as he would a colony. Not directly. Through its outputs.

Through the notifications that appeared in his peripheral vision.

By how points were awarded, violations flagged, and protocols unlocked. The Authority did not create.

It responded, which did not act. It processed. It did not decide.

It applied rules that already existed.

That was the weakness.

The Authority was not an actor.

It was a process, which was a set of rules that ran on a substrate, and the substrate was technician compliance. Every protocol the Authority deployed required a licensed technician to execute it.

Every point it awarded required a report to justify it. Every classification it maintained required field data to keep current. Without technicians filing reports, the Authority had no input.

Without input, it had no process. Without process, it was not a system.

It was dead text, and he started writing.

*Amendment to Field Guide entry: The Authority. Classification: Systemic.*

He crossed out Systemic. He wrote: *Parasitic.*

The Authority's rules were clear. A parasitic entity was defined as one that derives operational capacity from a host without contributing to the host's survival. The Authority derived its operational capacity from technicians.

It contributed nothing to their survival except protocols that kept them alive long enough to file more reports, which was not a contribution. That was a nutrient cycle.

He underlined the classification.

He wrote the justification in the Authority's own format. Observation. Inference.

Conclusion. Three lines.

Clean.

Auditable.

Thirty seconds.

Now the weakness. A parasitic classification required a weakness entry. The Authority's own rules demanded it. He could not amend the classification without providing the vulnerability, or the amendment would be rejected as incomplete.

He wrote: *Weakness: Self-Identification Obligation. If the Authority is identified within a Field Guide entry, it must process that identification as a valid treatment protocol input.

Processing requires acknowledgment. Acknowledgment requires the Authority to treat itself as a subject, and a subject that is treated is a subject that can be terminated.*

He checked his work. The logic was tight.

The Authority could not reject the identification because it was in its own Field Guide.

It could not reject the protocol because the protocol was in its own rules; could not refuse to process because processing was its function. And processing itself as a subject meant it was now a target.

Fifteen seconds, and he signed the amendment.

Technician Dale Kowalski. Certification Level VII.

Date.

Time.

The Authority processed it, and the response came in three lines.

*Amendment accepted. Classification updated. Self-Identification Obligation triggered. Processing.*

Then the world changed. Not visually.

Not physically.

But the peripheral notifications shifted.

The Authority's font, the one that had always been slightly wrong like a photocopy of a photocopy, went flat. Correct.

Clean. Like a document that had never been copied. Like an original.

Dale felt it in his toolkit. His protocols were still there, but they felt different.

Lighter.

As if under real-time audit. As if the Authority verified its output during execution.

Then the first technician moved, and the notification came in a format Dale had never seen. Not a notification. A citation.

*VIOLATION: Unauthorized amendment of protected Field Guide record.

Classification: Class-IX Contamination. Technician: Dale Kowalski.

Penalty: Immediate suspension pending review.

Treatment protocol: Termination.*

Dale read it twice. Then he understood.

The first technician had not been filing a closing report, and it had been filing a closing report on the Authority.

But now that the Authority had been reclassified as parasitic, the closing report was no longer valid.

You could not close a case on a parasitic entity.

You could only treat it. And treatment required a licensed technician.

And the only licensed technician in the field was Dale, and the first technician had just made Dale the Authority's exterminator. And it had just made Dale a violation.

The Authority processed the violation citation. Dale watched it happen.

The toolkit's response revealed the processing.

Protocols were being locked. One by one. The Authority was applying its own rules to him.

He was a contamination.

Contaminations were treated. Treatment protocols were being prepared.

His hands moved before he decided they would, and he pulled his clipboard up; he looked at the Field Guide entry for the Authority. His entry.

The one he had just amended. The weakness was there.

It was real.

It was active, and he could use it. He could write a treatment plan. He could execute it. He could exterminate the Authority.

But if he exterminated the Authority, he exterminated the system. The system that gave him his toolkit.

The system that gave him his certification. The system that gave him the Field Guide. The system that was, right now, the only thing standing between him and whatever was crawling out of the Rift.

And he could not refuse the treatment. The Authority had cited him.

He was a contamination, and the Authority was applying protocols to him. If he resisted, he would be classified as non-compliant. Non-compliant technicians were terminated.

That was not a metaphor.

He had seen it happen to two other technicians in the county. The Authority simply stopped recognizing them.

Their notifications went dark.

Their toolkits went empty. They became unlicensed. And in a world where unlicensed meant unprotected, unlicensed meant dead within the hour.

Dale looked at the citation again. *Penalty: Immediate suspension pending review.*

Pending review, which was the loophole. The Authority could not terminate him until the review was complete.

And a review required a hearing.

And a hearing required both parties to present evidence.

And Dale was the only person in the field who had evidence about the Authority. Because he had written the entry.

Because he had written the weakness. Because the Authority's own Field Guide now contained information that only he possessed.

If he presented that evidence, the Authority would have to process it. Processing it would require the Authority to re-read its own entry.

Re-reading was identification.

Identification was a treatment step. And the Authority was already in a self-identification loop.

He could stall the review, and he could use the Authority's own processing against itself. Every time it tried to terminate him, it would have to re-identify itself.

Every re-identification would deepen the loop.

The Authority would be trapped in its own protocol, processing its own entry, treating itself, identifying itself, treating itself again.

But stalling was not solving. The first technician was still out there.

Still filing.

Still working.

And the Authority, locked in its self-processing loop, could not respond to anything else. Could not deploy protocols against the Rift.

Could not process new infestations. Could not protect anyone.

Dale looked at the notification. His toolkit was empty.

Every protocol locked.

Every ability greyed out. He was a technician with no tools. A man with a clipboard and a pen and an entry in a book that the Authority had not yet deleted.

He started writing a hearing request.

His hand was steady. His handwriting was clean.

He wrote in the Authority's format.

Observation. Inference. Conclusion.

*Observation: The Authority has been reclassified as parasitic. Inference: The Authority's continued operation constitutes an active infestation. Conclusion: A hearing is required before termination. Requesting: 72 hours to prepare evidence.*

The Authority processed it.

*Granted. Review scheduled. Evidence due: 72 hours.*

Seventy-two hours. The Authority would not terminate him for seventy-two hours.

But it would also not function for seventy-two hours.

And the first technician would use those hours to file something else.

Something Dale had not seen yet. Something the first technician had been building toward since the beginning.

Dale stood up. His knees ached. He was thirty-eight years old and he had been standing for a long time.

He looked at the clipboard. At the pen.

At the Field Guide entry for the Authority, which was now the most dangerous document in the world.

He looked at the notification. His certification status had changed. It no longer read Licensed.

It read Suspended.

Below that, in smaller text, a line he had never seen before.

*Pending reclassification. New certification track: Authority-Adjacent. Requirements: Unknown. Consequences: Unknown.*

He had not requested this, and the Authority had not asked. It had simply appeared. As if the system independently deemed Dale no longer a technician.

He was something else. Something the system did not have a name for yet.

And then the first technician filed its next document. Dale felt it arrive before he saw it. A pressure behind his eyes. A notification that did not appear in his peripheral vision but in the center of his thoughts, like a thought that was not his own.

*Closing report amended.

New subject: Dale Kowalski.

Classification: Class-IX Infestation. Status: Active.

Requesting: Permission to treat.*

The Authority processed the request.

*Permission granted.*

Dale looked down at his hands. They were still holding the clipboard.

They were still holding the pen, and they were still his hands; the Authority was looking at them now. The Authority was looking at him.

Not as a technician.

Not as a violation. As a subject.

As a thing that needed to be treated, and he had seventy-two hours to prepare evidence. He had no toolkit. He had no protocols. He had one Field Guide entry and a pen and a certification that no longer meant anything.

And the first technician was coming. Not with a notification. Not with a citation. With a treatment protocol.

Dale started walking. He did not know where. He knew only that he needed to find something.

A weakness in the first technician that the Authority's Field Guide did not already contain, and a weakness he could write.

A weakness that would make the closing report inaccurate, and he had seventy-two hours. He had a pen. He had a clipboard.

He had been writing code violations for fourteen years.

He knew how to find a weakness in a structure. He had done it ten thousand times.

He had done it in kitchens and basements and crawlspaces and walls. He had done it while things screamed behind the drywall.

He had done it while the world was ending, and he started walking faster.

The world was not ending. It was breeding.
