Chapter 371: Uncertified Extermination

# Chapter 371

The fluorescent light in the waiting room hummed at a frequency that Dale Kowalski recognized as failing ballast. It was a sixty-hertz buzz, intermittent, with a slight pitch shift that indicated a loose connection in the fixture's junction box. He noted the sound, cataloged it, and then remembered that he was no longer licensed to note anything.

The red text hovering in his peripheral vision pulsed like an infected wound. *STATUS: PROBATION.* The words overlaid the faces of the three people sitting across from him. They were not monsters. They were worse. They were auditors.

One of them tapped a pen against a glass table. The sound was sharp, deliberate.

It broke Dale's concentration, and he shifted his weight in the plastic chair, feeling the cold sweat prickle along his spine. He kept his hands on his lap, fingers interlaced, knuckles white.

"Mr.

Kowalski," the woman in the grey suit said. Her voice was dry, stripped of any empathy.

She didn't look at him, and she looked at the file folder on the table between them.

"You have been summoned for a compliance review regarding your recent conduct."

Dale cleared his throat.

"I did what was necessary."

"Necessary is a subjective term in this jurisdiction," the man on the left said. He was younger, with a tie that cost more than Dale's truck. "The Authority defines necessity through adherence to protocol. You deviated."

"The entity was a Class X threat," Dale said. He forced his voice to remain level, the tone he used when explaining to a homeowner why their ants were coming from under the slab. "Standard containment protocols were insufficient. Immediate neutralization was required to prevent sector-wide contamination."

"You destroyed the Archivist," the third auditor said. This one was older, his face a map of deep lines and tired eyes.

"You did not document its weak point.

You did not log the environmental variables.

You simply... killed it.

"

"It was screaming," Dale said. "It was trying to rewrite the local physics. I couldn't wait for a Field Guide entry to populate.

That restriction is new. I didn't have a choice.

"

The woman looked up.

Her eyes were hard, like polished stones.

"Ignorance of the law is not an excuse. But incompetence is a different matter." She slid the red-wax-sealed folder across the glass.

"Open it."

Dale stared at the seal. His hand twitched.

He wanted to tear it open.

He wanted to grab his history, his fourteen years of service, and hold it against the cold air of this room. But he didn't move.

"Open it, Dale."

He picked up the folder. The weight was wrong.

It felt hollow, and he broke the wax seal.

The sound of the crack echoed in the small room.

Inside was a single sheet of paper. It was blank.

"What is this?" Dale asked.

"Your record," the woman said. "Or rather, what remains of it after we applied the penalty for Unlicensed Action."

Dale felt the blood drain from his face. He flipped the page. Nothing.

He checked the back. Nothing.

"You erased my history?"

"We revoked your access," the man in the expensive tie corrected.

"Your field guide is locked. Your protocols are disabled. But the data you collected?

That is proprietary to the Authority.

You failed to file it correctly, so we confiscated it. You have no leverage.

You have no proof you ever existed.

"

Dale's jaw tightened. He set the folder down.

"I saved this city."

"You created a citation," the older auditor said. "A massive one. You left a void where a regulatory framework should be.

Now, there is a hole in the sector's defense grid. Something is crawling through it.

"

"Then fix it," Dale said. "You have the data. Use the data."

"We cannot," the woman said.

"Not without a licensed technician to validate the threat.

The system requires human confirmation for certain escalations. It prevents automated overreaction.

It also prevents unauthorized heroes from breaking things.

"

She pushed a second document across the table. It was a job order.

Standard format.

Company letterhead, client details, problem description. But the client name was blank. The location was listed as *Sector 4, Sub-Level 2, Maintenance Corridor.*

"Your task," she said, "is to clear the blockage.

You are not allowed to fight. You are not allowed to use protocols.

You are allowed to observe.

And if you can identify the blockage, you may request a remote strike from the Authority.

"

Dale looked at the paper.

"That's not extermination. That's a suicide mission."

"It is a probationary task," the man said. "Complete it, and your license is restored. Fail it, and we will label you a Class Y invasive species. We will exterminate you."

Dale picked up the job order. His fingers brushed the paper. He could feel the faint vibration of the rift energy leaking from the ink.

"There's a catch," Dale said.

The woman smiled. It was a thin, cruel thing.

"Of course there is.

You are working without a team.

Without tools. And you have a six-hour window.

The blockage is growing. If it breaches the mainline, the entire sector collapses. If you survive, you keep your life.

If you don't, we assume you died on the job. Clean, tidy, no paperwork needed.

"

Dale stood up. His legs felt weak, but he locked his knees.

"I need a flashlight."

"You have your eyes," the older auditor said. "Use them."

Dale walked out of the office. The door clicked shut behind him. The lock engaged with a heavy thud.

He walked down the hallway, his boots squeaking on the linoleum.

The lights flickered overhead. The same sixty-hertz buzz. He counted his steps.

One, two, three.

Four, five, six, and he needed to think.

The System was blind to him now. He couldn't see enemy health bars. He couldn't see damage numbers.

He couldn't use his spray. He couldn't use his trap.

But he could still see, and he reached the elevator.

He pressed the button for the basement.

The doors slid open. Inside, a janitor was mopping the floor.

The man looked up, his face pale.

"Hey, Kowalski," the janitor said. "You hear what's going on down in Four?"

Dale stepped into the elevator.

"What's going on?"

"Smell," the janitor said. He pointed to the floor.

The mop was grey, but there was a slick, oily residue on it.

"Like burning sugar and rot. And the pipes are screaming."

"Pipes?"

"Thermal expansion," the janitor said. "Or something pushing them from the inside. My supervisor says it's a blockage. Says it's biological."

Dale looked at the janitor's mop handle. It was wrapped in blue tape. Not industrial grade. Just office supply tape.

"You have a wrench?" Dale asked.

"In the closet," the janitor said.

He looked at Dale with pity.

"Whatever you're doing, don't go down there.

The air filtration is down.

You'll breathe in the spores before you even see the thing.

"

Dale nodded. "Thanks."

He got off at the basement level. The air was cold.

It smelled of ozone and damp concrete, and he walked toward the maintenance corridor.

The lights were off. He used his phone's flashlight.

The beam cut through the darkness, illuminating dust motes and peeling paint.

He found the utility closet. He rummaged through the shelves.

He found a pipe wrench, which was heavy, rusted at the jaws. He tested the grip. Solid.

He moved to the corridor.

The floor was wet. The water was black.

He stepped carefully.

The black liquid seeped from a crack in the concrete. It moved sluggishly, defying gravity, creeping up the walls.

Dale stopped. He knelt.

He dipped a finger in the sludge, which was warm.

It coated his skin. He wiped it on his pants.

His heart hammered against his ribs.

*System Alert: Biohazard Detected. Recommendation: Quarantine.*

The text appeared in red. It was a passive notification. He couldn't act on it. He couldn't deploy a protocol.

He looked up. At the end of the corridor, the main service door was bulging.

The metal was warped outward, and the hinges were screaming. A high-pitched metal shriek echoed down the hall.

Dale walked toward it.

He pulled out his clipboard. It was empty.

He pulled out his pen, and he clicked it. No sound.

He approached the door. The heat radiating from it was intense. He could feel the vibration in his teeth.

"Who's there?" a voice called out.

Dale froze. It wasn't a monster's voice. It was human.

He looked through the small, wire-mesh window in the door. On the other side, a man was sitting on a crate. He was wearing a blue jumpsuit. He looked terrified.

"Hey!" the man shouted. "Can you help me? I'm trapped!"

Dale looked at the door. He looked at the wrench in his hand. He looked at the black sludge pooling around his boots.

"If I open this," Dale said, "you'll die."

"What?" The man slammed his fist against the door. "Let me out! There's... there's something in the walls. It's eating the cables."

Dale closed his eyes. He listened.

He heard the wet tearing sound, and he heard the crunch of metal.

He heard the man's breathing.

"What kind of cables?" Dale asked.

"Fiber optics! Power lines! It's... it's glowing. It's pulsing."

Dale opened his eyes. He looked at the door. The hinges were failing. The bolts were shearing.

"What's your name?" Dale asked.

"Mike," the man said. "Please. I have kids."

Dale looked at the black sludge. He looked at the door. He looked at his empty clipboard.

He remembered the auditor's words. *If you can identify the blockage, you may request a remote strike.*

He didn't have a protocol to kill the thing. But he had knowledge. He had the layout of the building. He knew where the main power shut-off was.

But the auditor said he couldn't use combat protocols. They didn't say he couldn't use building codes.

Dale pulled out a piece of paper from his pocket. It was a sketch he had made in the elevator. A diagram of the corridor's ventilation system.

He looked through the window.

"Mike, listen to me. Is the thing near the ceiling vent?"

Mike looked up.

"Yes! It's hanging there! Like a spider!"

Dale nodded. "It's not a spider. It's a gas exchange parasite.

It feeds on thermal differentials. It's using the heat from the servers to incubate.

"

"What does that mean?"

"It means it's vulnerable to cold," Dale said. "And it means the vent system is its nursery."

Dale stood back, and he looked at the control panel on the wall. It was covered in dust. He wiped it with his sleeve.

The buttons were faded.

He couldn't use a combat protocol to freeze the creature. But he could trigger the HVAC emergency purge.

It wasn't a weapon, which was a maintenance function. It vented the room's air to the outside atmosphere.

If the creature was attached to the vent, the sudden drop in pressure and temperature would shock it. It wouldn't kill it. But it would stun it. It would create a window.

Dale looked at Mike.

"When the fans start screaming, run to the left tunnel. Don't look back."

"What are you doing?"

"I'm filing a complaint," Dale said.

He slammed his wrench against the emergency manual override lever. It stuck.

He hit it again.

The metal rang out, and he hit it a third time. The lever gave way.

A siren wailed. The lights in the corridor turned red. The vents overhead groaned. The black sludge boiled.

From behind the door, a scream tore through the air. It wasn't human. It was the sound of tearing wet meat.

The door burst open.

Dale didn't run. He stepped back.

A mass of chitinous legs and pulsating sacs erupted from the doorway. It was large, the size of a minivan. It reeled, its eyes blinking rapidly in the sudden cold air. It shrieked, a sound of pure agony.

Mike scrambled past the creature, falling to his knees, then running.

Dale watched the monster. It was disoriented. Its limbs spasmed. The cold had disrupted its thermal regulation.

Dale raised his hand. He pointed at the creature.

"Target acquired," he said. "Thermal instability confirmed. Structural weakness located at the thoracic junction."

He waited. He waited for the red text. He waited for the *Strike Request* button to appear.

It didn't appear.

Instead, a new message flashed in his vision.

*ERROR: UNLICENSED ACTION DETECTED. INTERVENTION BLOCKED.*

Dale gritted his teeth. The monster recovered. It turned toward him.

It clicked its mandibles. It lunged.

Dale dove, and he rolled across the wet floor, the wrench still in his hand. He scrambled to his feet. The creature slashed at him, its claw missing his head by inches.

It hit the wall.

Concrete shattered.

Dale backed up. He had no weapons. He had no armor. He had no protection.

He looked at the control panel again. The purge was still running. The cold air was blasting out. The monster was slowing down, but it was still dangerous.

He needed to buy time, and he looked at the fire extinguisher mounted on the wall. Red. Heavy.

He grabbed it. He pulled the pin.

He wasn't trying to freeze it. He wasn't trying to drown it.

He aimed the nozzle at the creature's eye.

"Fire suppression system discharge," he said.

He sprayed.

The chemical foam coated the monster's face. It blinded it. It confused it.

The creature thrashed, slamming into the walls.

Dale kept spraying. He kept moving. He kept talking.

"Subject is non-responsive to standard deterrents. Requesting immediate containment via structural collapse."

He pointed the extinguisher at the ceiling above the creature. At the support beam.

The Authority's system couldn't strike the monster. But it could strike the environment.

*Request Processed,* the text flashed. *Environmental Hazard Detected. Auto-Mitigation Active.*

The ceiling beam groaned. The metal supports, weakened by the heat and the vibration, snapped.

The creature looked up. It saw the falling concrete.

It tried to move. But its legs were tangled in the cables it had eaten.

The beam fell.

It crushed the creature. Dust exploded into the corridor. The floor shook.

Dale stood still. He watched the dust settle.

He didn't have a body to verify. He didn't have a head to document.

He had a crushed shape under tons of concrete, and he clicked his pen.

The text appeared.

*INFESTATION NEUTRALIZED. THOROUGHNESS RATING: 4.2.* *LICENSE RESTORED: PARTIAL.* *NEW RESTRICTION: YOU ARE NOW A WITNESS.*

Dale frowned. *Witness?*

He looked at the crushed mess. He looked at the running water. He looked at his empty clipboard.

A voice came from the shadows.

"Good work, Kowalski."

Dale turned.

The man with the red stamp was standing there. He was smiling.

"You didn't kill it," the man said. "You just buried it. And now, it's going to dig its way out. And when it does, it will remember your face."

The man pointed to the pile of rubble.

"Something else is coming. And this time, you won't be allowed to fight. You'll just have to watch it eat everyone."

Dale looked at the rubble. A single, black leg twitched.

He didn't run. He picked up his pen.

"Show me the next job order," Dale said.
