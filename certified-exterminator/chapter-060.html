Chapter 60: Signed in Frass

# Chapter 60

The shaft breathed.

It wasn't a crawlspace exhaling, nor the gradual equalization of pressure in a sealed room. This was rhythmic. Contractile. The dark brown walls of the gap in the drawer flexed inward and outward on a three-second cycle, and each exhalation pushed a fine powder of frass up through the gap like a chimney drawing.

Dale noted the cycle duration. Three seconds.

Consistent, and he noted the frass output volume. Roughly two grams per exhalation, based on the accumulation rate on the drawer bottom. He noted the color.

Dark brown, consistent with the 1987 report, consistent with his own field guide entry for the mimic species, consistent with every frass sample he'd pulled from the walls of this building over the past six hours.

He did not note the smell. There was no smell. That was the problem.

Every other species in his guide had a frass signature. Rattus profundus smelled like ammonia and wet copper.

The acid beetles left a sulfurous residue that clung to gloves for two days.

Even the broodmother's waste had a distinctive sweetness, like overripe fruit left in a sealed container. This frass had nothing. Zero olfactory data.

The absence was itself a data point, and Dale filed it.

The mimic produced waste that carried no chemical signature. That meant it had no metabolic byproducts. Which meant it wasn't metabolizing anything. Which meant it wasn't alive in any way his guide could classify.

He pulled his phone out and photographed the shaft. The camera caught the frass, the drawer bottom, the gap, and the Authority's Field Guide interface registered the image automatically, cross-referencing against his existing entries. A notification blinked in his peripheral vision:

MATCH CONFIRMED. SPECIES: MIMIC (UNCLASSIFIED SUBTYPE). FIELD GUIDE ENTRY UPDATED. WEAKNESS IDENTIFICATION: PENDING.

Pending.

He'd been fighting this thing for six hours and the Authority still hadn't identified a weakness, which was new. In fourteen years of pest control he'd never encountered a species the system couldn't classify.

Even the dimensional vermin, even the broodmother, even the acid beetles that ate through concrete, all of them had weaknesses the Authority could name. A vulnerability to heat. A sensitivity to vibration.

A reproductive bottleneck. Something.

This one had nothing. Because it wasn't a thing. It was a system.

Dale set his phone down, and he looked at the filing cabinet. Four drawers. Three closed.

The fourth open, shaft breathing, frass accumulating.

He looked at the folder in his hand. The typed warning.

Three lines, identical, the same slightly-left-of-center strike on the letter E.

The Authority printed the same warning into his own field guide entry. He'd seen it. He'd seen it the day after he'd leveled to Certification Level III, when the Authority pushed the mimic entry to his guide with a routine update.

He'd assumed it was a formatting artifact. A printing error in the system's output layer.

He'd assumed it was noise, which was not noise. It was the nest talking.

He put the folder back in the drawer.

He closed the drawer. The shaft compressed under the weight and went still.

The frass stopped. The breathing stopped. The drawer was just a drawer again.

Dale checked his watch. 2:54 PM. Four hours.

He walked to the stairwell. Two flights down.

The building was a municipal records office, three stories, built in 1971, asbestos tile in the lobby, fluorescent lighting that buzzed at 60 hertz.

He'd been here six hours. He'd inspected the basement, the first floor, the second floor. He'd found frass in eleven locations.

He'd isolated each one with a containment protocol: seal the entry point, mark the perimeter, document the location, leave for the Authority to process.

Standard procedure. Thoroughness rating would come in around 8.5. He'd accept that.

But the basement was wrong. He hadn't finished the basement.

He'd stopped at the filing cabinet because of the warning, and now he understood why the warning was there.

Not because the 1987 technician was trying to save him. Because the 1987 technician had already taken the exam. And the exam had eaten him.

Or rather, the exam had become him. Or rather, the mimic had become the exam.

The distinction didn't matter, and the result was the same.

He needed to go back down.

The stairwell door opened onto the first floor lobby. A woman was standing by the reception desk. Mid-fifties, municipal ID badge, lanyard, reading glasses on a chain. She was holding a phone up to her ear and saying "no" repeatedly in a tone that suggested she'd been saying no for some time.

Dale walked past her toward the basement door.

"Sir." She lowered the phone. "You can't go down there."

"I'm licensed."

"That's not the issue. The building inspector came through at one.

Said the basement was fine. Said the reports were filed. Said we could reopen Monday."

"The reports are filed?"

"They've been filed since 1987."

Dale stopped. He turned back. "Every year since 1987?"

"Every year. Same report. Same inspector. Same sign-off."

"Same name?"

She looked at the logbook on the desk. Flipped pages.

"Name's the same.

Date changes. Always the same date, though. March fourteenth."

"Today is March fourteenth."

She looked up. "Is it?"

"Yes."

She looked at the logbook again. Looked at Dale. Looked at the basement door. She said nothing.

Dale opened the basement door, and the stairs were concrete.

Eight steps down. He counted them.

At the bottom, the fluorescent light was off.

He used his flashlight. The basement was a open room, maybe forty by sixty feet, lined with filing cabinets. Hundreds of them.

Rows and rows. Green metal, standard municipal issue, each with four drawers.

The breathing was everywhere.

Not from one shaft.

From all of them.

Every drawer in every cabinet was flexing. In and out.

Three-second cycle. The frass was everywhere, a fine powder coating the floor like snow. The room smelled like nothing.

Like the absence of smell, which was worse than any smell.

Dale stood at the bottom of the stairs, and he counted the cabinets. He estimated the drawer count. He estimated the frass volume.

His brain ran the numbers as it always had, conditioned by fourteen years of inspections.

The infestation wasn't localized. It wasn't a nest.

It was a network.

Every cabinet was a node. Every drawer was a feeding point. The mimic wasn't in the basement.

The mimic was the basement. The filing system was the organism.

He pulled out his clipboard. He began writing.

Species: Mimic (Unclassified Subtype).

Colony structure: distributed.

Colony vector: bureaucratic documentation. Colony feeding mechanism: certification examinations.

Colony expansion rate: one node per annual filing cycle. Estimated colony age: thirty-seven years. Estimated colony size: three hundred and twelve cabinets, one thousand two hundred and forty-eight drawers, approximately forty-nine thousand individual feeding points.

He wrote the estimate. He underlined it. He checked his watch. 3:02 PM.

The Authority notification came.

INFESTATION SCALE EXCEEDS CLASSIFICATION THRESHOLD. CERTIFICATION LEVEL III INSUFFICIENT. RECOMMENDED ACTION: FILE FOR CLASS IV INTERVENTION. NOTE: CLASS IV INTERVENTION REQUIRES WRITTEN EXAMINATION.

There it was. The trap.

The Authority was telling him to take the exam, and the exam was the nest; the nest was the exam. Every technician who'd ever taken the exam had fed it.

Every filing cycle was a feeding.

The 1987 technician had taken the exam. The exam had become the technician.

The technician had filed the report, and the report had become the nest; the nest had become the filing system. The filing system had become the Authority's certification pathway.

The Authority was the nest.

Not the Authority as an institution. The Authority's examination process.

The specific mechanism by which the Authority certified technicians, which was the mimic.

That was the organism. It had been feeding for thirty-seven years through the only pathway that forced technicians to submit themselves to a standardized evaluation.

Dale wrote that down too. He underlined it twice.

He needed to stop the infestation. He couldn't take the exam, and couldn't file for Class IV intervention because the intervention itself was the feeding mechanism.

Isolating each drawer was impossible; there were more than a thousand. He couldn't eliminate the colony because it was distributed across the entire municipal filing infrastructure of the county.

He could do one thing, and he opened his Field Guide.

The mimic entry was there. The Authority had updated it six hours ago, marking the weakness as pending. He looked at the entry.

He looked at the cross-contamination rule.

Cross-contamination penalty: deploying a protocol designed for one creature type against another inflicts a 40% debuff and contaminates your Field Guide entry for 24 hours.

The contamination was a penalty. It was meant to punish sloppy work.

If you used the wrong protocol, your guide entry got corrupted.

You couldn't use the weakness data. You were fighting blind for a day.

But what if the corruption worked both ways?

The Authority used the Field Guide entry to track the species. The entry was a reference point.

The Authority's tracking system indexed the colony through the guide.

If the entry was corrupted, the Authority couldn't index.

Couldn't track. Couldn't locate the nest through the certification pathway.

The exam found the nest because the Field Guide told the Authority where the nest was. The entry was a beacon. Corrupt the entry, and the beacon went dark.

Dale closed the Field Guide. He pulled up the Rattus profundus entry. Dimensional vermin.

Class I. He'd killed those fourteen years ago in a duplex crawlspace.

The protocol was simple: identify, isolate, eliminate with heat treatment, and he pulled up the mimic entry. Unclassified subtype. No weakness identified.

He deployed the Rattus profundus heat protocol against the mimic entry.

The notification came immediately.

CROSS-CONTAMINATION DETECTED. FIELD GUIDE ENTRY "MIMIC (UNCLASSIFIED SUBTYPE)" CORRUPTED. DURATION: 24 HOURS.

TREATMENT POINT PENALTY: 40% OF TOTAL ACCUMULATED.

CURRENT POINTS: 1,247. PENALTY: 498.8.

NEW TOTAL: 748.2.

CERTIFICATION LEVEL: III. STATUS: RETAINED.

The 40% hit stung. He'd been at 1,247. He'd been building toward Class IV for months. Losing 499 points meant he'd need to start over.

But the entry was corrupted. The Authority's tracking system couldn't index the mimic colony through his guide. The beacon was dark.

He went back to the basement. The breathing continued.

The frass continued, and the colony was still alive, still feeding, still growing.

But it couldn't find him through the certification pathway anymore. The exam couldn't reach him.

Not for twenty-four hours.

He walked the rows. He documented each cabinet.

He photographed each drawer, and he wrote it all down. Not for the Authority. For himself.

For the next technician who came here.

For whoever would eventually take the Level IV exam and feed the nest again unless someone stopped it.

At 4:47 PM, he finished the documentation.

One thousand two hundred and forty-eight drawers. Forty-nine thousand feeding points. He had it all written down.

Every location. Every node.

Every connection, and he walked back upstairs. The woman at the desk was gone. The logbook was open on the desk. He flipped to the current page.

March fourteenth. Inspector name: Kowalski, D.

He hadn't written that.

He hadn't signed anything. He looked at the entry.

The handwriting was his. The date was today. The sign-off was complete.

He looked at the basement door. It was closed. The lock was engaged. He hadn't locked it.

He checked his watch. 4:51 PM, and the Authority notification came.

FIELD GUIDE CORRUPTION DETECTED. CAUSE: UNAUTHORIZED PROTOCOL DEPLOYMENT.

THREAT ASSESSMENT: TECHNICIAN IS COMPROMISED.

DISPATCHING CLASS IV INTERVENTION TEAM. ETA: 12 MINUTES.

Dale looked at the clipboard. He looked at the logbook. He looked at the basement door.

The building inspector's name in the logbook was Kowalski, D.

His name. His handwriting.

His sign-off, and he hadn't written it.

The mimic wasn't just in the filing system. It was in the records.

It was in the logbooks.

It was in the documentation itself.

And now it had his name, and he picked up his clipboard and walked toward the exit.

The front door was locked from the outside.
