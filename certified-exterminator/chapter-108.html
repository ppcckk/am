Chapter 108: Rival Certification Level

# Chapter 108

The beetle did not crawl. It marched.

Its six legs moved in a rhythm that defied the chaos of the surrounding street. Around it, the shattered glass of the pharmacy window glittered like confetti from a parade no one had asked for. The air smelled of ozone and rotting insulation, a scent that Miller was currently trying not to vomit on.

Miller watched the insect with the wide, unblinking stare of a man who had just watched his boss get electrocuted by a sentient power grid. He didn't know what to look at.

The beetle?

The smoking boots? The blank, flickering holographic screen hovering over Dale's shoulder like a dying firefly?

"Hello, Dale."

The voice wasn't loud. It didn't echo. It sounded like wet gravel sliding down a pipe.

Miller looked at Dale.

Dale was not looking at the beetle. Dale was looking at his hands.

Specifically, at the tremor in his left pinky finger.

He held his clipboard with both hands, gripping the plastic so hard his knuckles turned the color of bone.

"Miller," Dale said. His voice was flat. Procedural. "Don't touch the grate."

"I wasn't going to," Miller whispered. He stepped back, his sneaker crunching on a shard of safety glass.

"That thing talked.

Did you hear that?

It talked."

The beetle raised its front two legs. They weren't legs. They were antennae, thick and segmented, twitching toward the darkness of the storm drain.

"Hello, Dale," the beetle said again. "Your warranty has expired."

Dale wiped his glasses on his shirt. He put them back on. He looked at the Field Guide. The loading bar still sat at 1%.

*Data corruption severity: Critical.* *Recommendation: Manual re-identification required.*

Dale stared at the beetle. It was small. Harmless. A single specimen.

But the System notification was screaming in red text.

*WARNING: CLASS-4 CONTAMINATION DETECTED.*

Miller looked at the notification. He looked at Dale. "Class four?

That's... that's city-wide infestation levels. That's the stuff that eats buses."

Dale didn't answer.

He pulled a pen from his pocket. He clicked it. Once.

Twice.

The click was the only sound in the street besides the distant wail of sirens that were getting closer but not fast enough.

"Identify," Dale muttered.

The beetle tilted its head. "I am the Audit."

Dale's pen hovered over the blank page of his notebook.

His hand shook. Not from fear. From the phantom sensation of the voltage still humming in his nerves.

He forced his arm to stay steady. He had to write something.

If he didn't write it down, it didn't count.

If it didn't count, he had no points.

No points meant no certification. No certification meant he was just a guy with a clipboard in a war zone.

He wrote: *Specimen 001. Small. Carapace: Obsidian.

Behavior: Vocalization. Threat Level: Unknown.*

The beetle stopped twitching. It seemed to wait.

"Thoroughness Rating: 2/10," the beetle said. "Incomplete."

Dale flinched. "It's a start."

"It is a lie," the beetle said.

"You know what I am. You saw the Foreman. You felt the grid.

You know the source code."

Dale felt a cold sweat prickle the back of his neck. He hated this.

He hated the ambiguity.

In pest control, you identified the species, you found the harborage, you applied the IPM protocol. You didn't negotiate with the cockroach. You didn't let the termite talk to you.

And yet, the System allowed it. Or rather, the System punished the lack of information, and this creature held the key.

Dale took a step forward. His boot crunched on drywall dust.

"State your species," Dale said.

"I am a proxy," the beetle said. "A fragment of the Foreman's will. A scout for the upcoming audit."

"Audit," Miller said. "What audit?"

Dale ignored him. He was thinking about the corruption message. *Data Lost.* If the Field Guide was wiped, he had no weakness bonuses.

He had no history.

He was starting from zero.

But he still had his clipboard. He still had his pen.

He still had his protocol.

"Proxies don't speak," Dale said. "Proxies don't have vocal cords.

Unless you're mimicking.

Which means you're studying me. Which means you're learning my weaknesses."

The beetle made a clicking sound.

It sounded like applause. "Clever.

The Technician adapts.

The Technician documents. The Technician is dangerous."

"Get out of my inspection zone," Dale said.

"Your zone is gone, Dale. The Foreman took it.

It took your memory.

It took your Field Guide.

What are you going to document? How do you exterminate a threat you can't remember killing?"

Dale looked at the loading bar. 2%.

It was too slow.

He looked at the beetle. It was sitting on the edge of the grate. If he kicked it, he could kill it.

Easy. But killing a Class-4 proxy without identification yielded zero points.

And worse, if it was a mimic, it might have spores.

If he broke the shell, he could contaminate himself.

He needed to identify it first. But the Field Guide was blank.

Loophole time.

Dale remembered a footnote in the Certification Manual, page 412, subsection C: *In the absence of digital Field Guide data, the Technician may use analog cross-referencing to establish a provisional entry. Provisional entries grant a 10% damage bonus but carry a 50% penalty on Thoroughness if the identification is incorrect.*

It was a gamble. A massive one. If he guessed wrong, he'd be debuffed. If he guessed right, he'd have a weapon.

He looked at the beetle.

Black shell. No eyes.

Voice from the throat?

No, the vibration came from the legs. Stridulation. Like a cricket.

But the movement was wrong. Too deliberate.

Too human.

It wasn't a natural insect. It was a construct.

Dale wrote: *Specimen 001. Constructed Proxy. Material: Unknown composite. Weakness: Structural integrity at joint articulation.*

The beetle froze.

"Provisional entry accepted," the System pinged. *Damage Bonus: +10%.* *Penalty: Thoroughness Rating capped at 5/10 for this encounter.*

Dale gritted his teeth. A 5/10 was garbage. It was barely passing grade. But it was better than zero.

"You're guessing," the beetle said.

"I'm hypothesizing," Dale corrected.

Miller watched Dale's face.

He saw the sweat dripping from Dale's chin. He saw the way Dale's eyes darted around, calculating.

Miller had seen Dale spray for roaches for ten years. He had never seen him look like he was holding back a hurricane.

"Dale, what do you want me to do?" Miller asked.

"Stand back," Dale said. "Don't breathe too hard. If this thing is a mimic, it might be aerosolizing the data."

"What does that even mean?"

"It means don't inhale the dust."

Dale reached into his belt. He didn't have his sprayer. He didn't have the electro-mace. He had a roll of duct tape and a pair of heavy-duty rubber gloves he'd kept in his pocket for emergency glove changes.

He pulled on the gloves. They were thick, black nitrile.

He walked toward the beetle.

The beetle didn't run. It stayed on the grate. It waited.

"Come closer, Dale," it said. "Tell me what you remember. Tell me about the Foreman. Tell me about the wire in the concrete."

Dale stopped three feet away. He raised the duct tape.

"I don't remember," Dale lied.

The beetle vibrated. "Liar.

You saw the face.

You heard the hum. You felt the current."

"I felt the current," Dale said. "But I don't remember the face."

He pulled a strip of tape. The adhesive made a tearing sound in the quiet street.

The beetle tensed. Its legs dug into the metal grate.

Dale threw the tape.

It wasn't a fancy move. It was a sloppy, desperate toss, aimed at the beetle's legs. The tape stuck to the front two legs, binding them together. The beetle lost its balance and tipped over.

Dale lunged. He grabbed the beetle with his gloved hand. It buzzed, its legs kicking against the rubber. He squeezed.

The shell cracked.

A black fluid leaked out. It wasn't blood. It was ink. Thick, oily, viscous ink that smelled of burning circuitry.

Dale held it up. The beetle struggled.

"Identify," Dale whispered to himself.

He looked at the ink.

It pulsed. It moved.

It wasn't just fluid.

It was code. Tiny, microscopic letters swam in the black sludge.

*SYSTEM ERROR.* *MEMORY PURGE.* *REBOOT.*

Dale's eyes widened.

It wasn't a bug. It was a storage drive.

The Foreman hadn't just stolen his memory.

It had offloaded it.

The Foreman had uploaded Dale's Field Guide into a disposable carrier and sent it down the drain.

The beetle was the backup.

And Dale was holding it.

If he crushed it, he destroyed the data. If he kept it, he could retrieve the data.

But the System was screaming.

*CONTAMINATION WARNING: BIO-DIGITAL SYNC DETECTED.* *Do not expose biological tissue to data stream.*

Dale looked at his glove. A tiny tear in the nitrile. A pinhole. The ink was touching his skin.

He felt a headache spike behind his eyes. A flash of memory. Not his own.

A memory of the Foreman. The feeling of concrete pouring over a nervous system.

The taste of copper in the air.

Dale dropped the beetle.

It hit the pavement and cracked open completely. The ink pooled, spreading out like a shadow.

"Oops," the beetle's voice came from the puddle. "You broke the seal."

The ink rose. It defied gravity. It formed shapes.

A hand.

A face.

Miller stepped back, tripping over a curb. "Dale, run!"

Dale didn't run. He pulled out his pen. He looked at the forming face. He looked at his blank Field Guide.

He had one chance.

If he could document the ink, he could trap it. But how do you document a liquid? How do you isolate a shadow?

He looked at the street. The drain. The grate was still open.

"Miller," Dale said. "Get the fire extinguisher from the truck. The dry chemical one."

"The what?"

"The fire extinguisher. Now."

Miller scrambled toward the service van parked fifty yards away.

Dale stood alone with the rising ink. It was forming a torso. It was getting bigger. It was trying to pull itself out of the puddle.

Dale pulled out his clipboard.

He wrote: *Specimen 001. Class: Data-Phage. State: Decompressed.

Protocol: Containment via particulate disruption.*

He didn't know what that meant. He was making it up. He was hoping the System would accept the jargon if it sounded professional.

The System paused.

*Analyzing...* *Accepting. Thoroughness Rating: 4/10.*

It was garbage. But it was a protocol. And protocols had power.

The ink lunged at him.

Dale dodged. The ink splashed against the side of a parked car, eating the metal like acid.

Miller ran back, holding a red fire extinguisher. He yanked the pin.

"Spray it!" Dale yelled.

Miller aimed and squeezed the trigger. A cloud of yellow powder blasted out, hitting the ink face.

The face screamed. A digital, screeching noise that shattered the remaining windows.

The ink dissolved into the powder. The face collapsed. The voice stopped.

Silence.

Dale stood there, breathing hard. His gloves were covered in yellow powder and black sludge.

He looked at his Field Guide.

*Recovering Data...* *5%...*

It was moving faster.

But then, the ground shook.

Not a tremor. A rhythmic pounding. Like footsteps. Huge, heavy footsteps.

Dale looked down the street.

At the end of the block, where the streetlights were dead, something was walking toward them.

It was tall. It was made of twisted rebar and broken asphalt. It had no head. Just a pile of rubble where the head should be.

And it was holding something.

It was holding a clipboard.

Dale's blood turned to ice.

He looked at Miller. Miller was staring at the figure, his mouth open, the fire extinguisher still sputtering out.

"Dale," Miller whispered. "Who is that?"

Dale looked at the figure. He looked at the clipboard in its hand. It was a standard municipal clipboard. The same brand Dale used.

The figure stopped. It raised the clipboard.

*New Licensee Detected,* the System announced. *Competitive Overlap.*

Dale felt a new notification burn in his vision.

*Rival Certification Level: III.* *Status: Active.* *Objective: Terminate.*

The figure pointed a jagged arm of rebar at Dale.

And then, the figure spoke. Its voice was Dale's voice.

"Your workspace is not up to code."
