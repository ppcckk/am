Chapter 309: Administrative Hold

# Chapter 309

The chittering wasn't external. It echoed against the wet gray matter of Dale's inner ear, a rhythmic, wet clicking that synced with his heartbeat. *Click. Click. Click.*

Dale blinked.

The void vanished, and he was standing in a room that smelled of old coffee and ozone. Fluorescent lights buzzed overhead, flickering with a sickly yellow hue.

The floor was linoleum, cracked and peeling at the seams.

A metal desk sat in the center of the room, bolted to the floor. On it lay a stack of papers, a single mechanical pencil, and the red stamp from the man in the suit.

Dale looked down at his hands, and they were trembling. The holes in his shirt remained.

His spray canister was gone.

In its place was a clipboard.

A notification burned into his peripheral vision, stark white against the darkened edges of his sight.

**NOTICE: ADMINISTRATIVE HOLD PLACED.** **SUBJECT: DALE KOWALSKI (FORMERLY CLASS III).** **STATUS: PENDING RE-CLASSIFICATION HEARING.** **LOCATION: SUB-BASEMENT 4, LICENSING AUTHORITY INTERNAL REVIEW BOARD.**

Dale picked up the clipboard. His fingers brushed the cold metal clip. He checked the date. It was yesterday.

The man in the suit stepped out of the shadows near the filing cabinets. He hadn't moved. He hadn't blinked. His white eyes were fixed on Dale's chest, right over his heart.

"Sit," the man said.

Dale didn't sit. He adjusted his stance, feet shoulder-width apart, center of gravity low. A defensive posture. Or a preparatory one.

"I want my badge," Dale said.

"You don't have one."

"I did the work. Rattus profundus.

Class III nest.

I isolated the broodmother.

I applied the containment protocol.

"

The man opened the briefcase. It was empty except for a single sheet of paper, folded in thirds. He unfolded it with surgical precision.

"Your file is corrupted," the man said. "You rejected the Authority's classification. That is a violation of Protocol 1, Section A: *Submission to Taxonomy*."

"I identified the species correctly."

"Identification is not submission."

The man slid the paper across the desk. Dale looked at it. It was a standard intake form, but the fields were filled with red ink.

Not blood. Ink.

The kind used for official rejections.

**FORM 99-Z: DISPUTED TAXONOMY AUDIT.** **CASE NUMBER: 000-000-NULL.** **TECHNICIAN: KOWALSKI, D.** **ALLEGATION: UNAUTHORIZED RECLASSIFICATION OF ENTITY TYPE.**

Dale read the fine print. His eyes scanned the lines, looking for the loophole. He had spent fourteen years reading code violations.

He knew where the money lived, and he knew where the liability lay.

"This is a trap," Dale said. "You're trying to strip my license to cover up the Rift expansion."

The man tilted his head. A subtle tilt.

"The Authority does not cover up. The Authority categorizes."

"Then categorize this." Dale pointed to the ceiling.

"The chittering. It's not in the walls. It's in my head.

That's a new symptom. You didn't list it in the field guide.

"

The man smiled. It was the same thin, predatory curve.

"Because you didn't submit a report for it."

Dale's jaw tightened. The trap snapped shut.

If he didn't document the symptom, it didn't exist. If it didn't exist, he couldn't treat it. If he couldn't treat it, he was liable for the infection spreading.

He picked up the mechanical pencil. The tip snapped. He didn't flinch. He grabbed a pen from his pocket, a cheap ballpoint he'd kept for writing violation codes.

"I'm filing a supplemental report," Dale said.

"You have no authority to file."

"I have a license.

You revoked it.

But you didn't revoke my *obligation* to report. Protocol 4, Section C: *Mandatory Reporting of Anomalous Physiological Responses*.

Even suspended technicians must report hazards.

"

The man stopped smiling. For the first time, something flickered in those white eyes. Confusion? Annoyance?

"Protocol 4 applies to Class I and II threats," the man said. "You are currently unclassified."

"I was Class III at the time of the incident. The incident is ongoing. The threat is active. Therefore, my prior classification applies to the reporting requirement until re-evaluation is complete."

Silence stretched. The fluorescent light buzzed louder. *Bzzzzt.*

The man looked at the stamp. He looked at Dale. He picked up the red stamp.

"Prove it," the man said. "Prove the chittering is a Class III threat, not a Class IV hallucination."

Dale looked at the paper. *Form 99-Z*. He looked at the empty desk. He looked at his own reflection in the polished surface of the briefcase.

He needed a witness. He needed a baseline.

"Call the neighbor," Dale said.

"The who?"

"The tenant. Mrs.

Gable, and she lives next door. She heard the chittering. She called the police.

The police called us.

If the police report exists, the infestation is documented.

"

The man hesitated. His hand hovered over the stamp.

"We do not deal with municipal law enforcement," the man said. "We deal with the Authority."

"The Authority recognizes the Rift," Dale said.

"The Rift caused the chittering. The chittering is inside my head. That means the Rift is *inside* me.

That's a Class IV breach. If you stamp me as a Class III rejection, you're mislabeling a Class IV hazard.

That's a Level 1 Contamination Violation.

You'll lose your own certification.

"

The man went still. The air in the room seemed to drop ten degrees.

"You're bluffing," the man said.

"I'm citing code," Dale said. "Check the manual.

Page 402. *Cross-Dimensional Physiological Integration*. It's listed under 'Emergent Symptoms' for Class IV Rifts. It was added last Tuesday.

Did you read the updates?

"

The man's fingers twitched. He pulled a thick, leather-bound book from the briefcase. He flipped through the pages. His white eyes scanned the text, moving too fast for a human.

He stopped.

Dale held his breath.

His heart hammered against his ribs, but his face remained blank. He had spent his career betting on the fact that bureaucrats were lazy. They didn't read the appendices.

They relied on summaries.

The man looked up.

"It is not on page 402."

"Page 403," Dale said. "Addendum B. Revised for the Ohio Sector."

The man turned the page.

There it was.

**ADDENDUM B: PHYSIOLOGICAL INTEGRATION.** *Symptoms: Auditory hallucinations mimicking species calls. Tactile sensations of larval movement. Requires immediate quarantine and re-classification of technician as 'Host Vector'.*

The man slammed the book shut. Dust motes danced in the yellow light.

"You knew," the man said. His voice was low, dangerous.

"I guessed," Dale said. "I always check the addenda."

The man reached for the red stamp. He hovered it over the paper.

"If I stamp this," the man said, "you become a Host Vector. You will be detained. Indefinitely. You will never touch another pest again."

"And if you don't stamp it," Dale said, "you're hiding a Class IV breach. The next audit will find it. Your Thoroughness Rating will drop to zero. You'll be demoted to janitorial duty in Sector 9."

The man stared at him. The chittering in Dale's head grew louder. *Click. Click. Click.* It sounded like mandibles grinding together.

Dale felt a sharp pain in his left temple. A phantom itch. Like something was crawling under his skin.

He didn't scratch. He didn't rub. He just watched the man.

The man picked up the pen. Not the stamp.

He wrote something on the bottom of Form 99-Z.

**STATUS: UNDER INVESTIGATION. SUSPENSION PENDING RE-CLASSIFICATION TO HOST VECTOR.** **NOTES: TECHNICIAN DEMONSTRATES HIGH LEVEL OF PROTOCOL ADHERENCE. RECOMMEND IMMEDIATE ISOLATION.**

He stamped it.

**ACCEPTED.**

Not Rejected. Accepted.

Dale exhaled. His knees felt weak. He grabbed the edge of the desk to steady himself.

"You're not a technician anymore," the man said. "You're a sample."

"I'm still a technician," Dale said. "I just have a different classification."

The man closed the briefcase.

"You have one hour to prepare your defense.

After that, you will be transferred to Quarantine Zone 4.

Do not leave this room. Do not attempt to escape. The walls are lined with salt and iron.

"

The man turned and walked toward the door.

"Wait," Dale said.

The man stopped. He didn't turn around.

"The chittering," Dale said. "It's getting louder.

If you put me in Quarantine, you're putting a ticking bomb in a sealed box.

You'll need to monitor me.

Constantly. With audio sensors.

"

The man turned slowly. His white eyes narrowed.

"Why would we do that?"

"Because I know the species," Dale said. "I heard it. I didn't just identify it.

I *listened* to it. And it's not Rattus profundus anymore.

It's something else.

Something the Authority hasn't named yet.

"

The man stepped closer.

"What is it?"

Dale closed his eyes, and he focused on the sound in his head. It wasn't just clicking.

It was a pattern.

A rhythm.

It was Morse code.

Or something close to it.

*Click-click-click... pause... click-click.*

Dale's eyes snapped open.

"It's calling for help," Dale said. "Or it's calling for reinforcements."

The man's expression changed. The predatory smile vanished. He looked, for the first time, genuinely unsettled.

"Help from where?"

Dale looked at the door. Beyond it lay the dark. The void. The filing cabinet of the damned.

"From the other side of the stamp," Dale said.

The lights flickered.

And then, they went out completely.

In the darkness, Dale heard the sound of the briefcase latch clicking open.

He didn't wait. He lunged for the desk, grabbing the mechanical pencil, the pen, and the clipboard.

The door handle turned.

Dale threw the clipboard.

It hit the door with a heavy thud, wedging the mechanism just enough to jam it.

The lights stayed off.

And in the silence, the chittering stopped.

A new voice spoke. Not from the man. Not from the door.

From the air itself.

**NEW NOTIFICATION:** **FIELD GUIDE UPDATE DETECTED.** **SPECIES: UNKNOWN.** **THREAT LEVEL: CLASS V (PENDING).** **WEAKNESS: ADMINISTRATIVE ERROR.**

Dale stared at the glowing text in the darkness, and he didn't have a weapon. He didn't have a spray. He had a clipboard, a jammed door, and a secret that could get him killed.

He wrote.
