Chapter 89: Violation Existence

# Chapter 89

The fluorescent tube above the cubicle flickered. It didn’t buzz.

It didn’t hum.

It just stopped, then restarted, a rhythmic strobe that turned the beige carpet into a pulsing grid of shadow and light. Dale Kowalski didn’t look at the light. He looked at the pen in his hand.

It was a standard-issue ballpoint.

Blue ink. The barrel was slightly warm, as if it had been held by someone with a high body temperature for a long time.

The dead woman—his ex-wife, or the thing wearing her face—sat motionless.

Her gray skin had a waxy sheen, like a mannequin left in a hot car. Her eyes were open, but the pupils were dilated to the point where the iris was barely visible, just a thin ring of brown around a void of black.

"Show me the work," she whispered again. The voice didn’t come from her throat. It vibrated through the desk, traveling up the wood grain and into Dale’s palms.

Dale clicked the pen. Once. Twice. He wrote on the termination notice, right below his own forged signature.

*Correction: Employee did not resign. Employee was terminated without cause. Violation of Authority Statute 44-C: Unlawful Termination of Licensed Operator.*

The ink didn’t dry. It sat on the paper, wet and glistening. The dead woman’s head tilted. A sound like dry leaves crunching echoed in the small space.

"Insufficient documentation," she said.

Dale ignored her. He turned to the next sheet of paper on the desk.

It was blank, and he flipped it over.

Another blank sheet. He flipped it again.

Blank. He grabbed the stack. Fifty sheets.

All blank, and he needed a template.

He needed a standardized form. This wasn’t a workplace; it was a trap. The Authority didn’t issue termination notices with pre-filled signatures.

That was sloppy, which was inefficient. And efficiency was the only currency that mattered here.

He looked at the wall.

It was drywall. Standard commercial grade. Painted beige.

No windows. No door handle.

The door he’d come through was gone.

In its place was a solid sheet of metal, seamless and cold.

Dale stood up. His knees cracked.

He felt heavy, weighted down by the *Penalty: Permanent Loss of Reality Anchor* notification that still burned in his peripheral vision. The text was fading, dissolving into static, but the sensation remained. Something seemed to try dragging him backward through the air, a pull behind his navel.

He walked to the metal door. He ran his hand over it.

No seams.

No hinges. He pulled a small canister of aerosol spray from his belt. It was empty.

He’d used the last of it in the rift, and he checked his pockets. Lint.

A paperclip.

The brass key.

The key was hot now. Burning hot.

He turned back to the desk. He sat down.

He couldn’t leave.

If he left, he was abandoning the case.

Abandoning a case meant a Thoroughness Rating of zero. Zero meant revocation.

Revocation meant he was just a man in a room with a corpse.

"Mr. Kowalski," the dead woman said. "You have not filed your daily log."

Dale picked up the pen again. He started writing on the blank sheets instead of the termination notice. He created a new form from scratch.

*Incident Report: Unauthorized Office Environment.* *Subject: Deceased Former Spouse / Authority Construct.* *Location: Non-Euclidean Administrative Sector, Level 9.*

He wrote fast. His handwriting was cramped, jagged.

He didn’t worry about legibility, and he worried about structure. He needed to identify the entity. He needed to document its behavior.

He needed to isolate the threat.

*Behavioral Observation: Entity mimics familiar human forms to induce psychological distress. Voice projection is non-local. Physical integrity is compromised; skin lacks elasticity.*

He paused. He looked at her hand.

It rested on the desk, and the fingers were too long. The joints bent backward slightly.

*Weakness Identification: Construct relies on emotional resonance to maintain cohesion. Disruption of emotional anchor causes structural failure.*

Dale looked at her. "I don’t have an emotional anchor," he said.

His voice was flat.

Procedural.

"I have a certification. I have a protocol.

You are a violation of that protocol."

The dead woman smiled. Her jaw unhinged wider. Black fluid leaked from her mouth and pooled on the desk. It smelled like old toner and copper.

"You are late," she said. "The shift ends in three minutes. If you do not clock out, you are fired."

"I’m not clocking out," Dale said. "I’m working overtime."

He looked at the system interface. It was glitching. The text was scrolling too fast, unreadable. But he saw one line, stable and clear:

**CURRENT OBJECTIVE: FILE COMPLETE TERMINATION RECORD.** **TIME REMAINING: 02:58**

He looked at the timer. Two minutes and fifty-eight seconds.

He looked at the form. He hadn’t written the elimination step.

He hadn’t written the verification step, and he couldn’t. Not without a weapon. Not without a way to break the construct.

He looked at the pen.

The pen was the only tool he had.

He looked at the key.

The key was a Level 9 Access token.

It opened doors. It granted permissions.

But it didn’t kill things.

Unless...

Unless the thing wasn’t a monster. Unless the thing was a bureaucrat.

And bureaucrats could be audited.

Dale stood up. He walked to the metal door. He jammed the brass key into the air next to the door.

The air rippled. A tear opened.

Not a door.

A crack.

A hairline fracture in reality.

Through the crack, Dale saw another office. And another. And another.

Rows of cubicles stretching into infinity. Each one occupied.

Each one by a dead version of someone he knew.

His father. His boss. His landlord.

They were all filing reports. They were all writing. The sound was deafening. The scratch of millions of pens.

Dale stepped back. He didn’t go through. He closed the tear with his hand.

The pain was immediate.

His skin blistered. He shook his hand, ignoring the burn.

He turned back to the desk. The dead woman was gone.

In her place was a pile of dry skin. And a chair. And a laptop.

The laptop was open. The screen glowed blue.

On the screen was a spreadsheet.

Columns: *Operator Name. Certification Level. Thoroughness Rating. Termination Date.*

Dale’s name was at the top. *Dale Kowalski.* *Level: IX.* *Thoroughness: 0.0.* *Status: Pending Termination.*

He sat down. He put his hands on the keyboard. He typed.

*Query: Why is my Thoroughness Rating 0.0?*

The cursor blinked.

*Answer: File incomplete.*

Dale typed: *Define incomplete.*

*Answer: Lack of Elimination Proof.*

He looked around the empty office.

There was no monster to kill. No bug to squash. The threat was the paperwork itself.

The system was trapping him in a loop of documentation, demanding proof of an extermination that had already happened.

He needed to change the classification, and he highlighted his name. He right-clicked. A menu appeared.

*Change Classification: Vermin / Operator / Construct / Error.*

He hovered over *Error*.

If he classified himself as an Error, he would be deleted. System purge. Instant death.

He hovered over *Construct*.

If he classified himself as a Construct, he would be processed as a threat, and he would be exterminated by the Authority.

He hovered over *Vermin*.

Vermin were pests, and they were infestations.

They were cleaned up. They were not terminated.

They were *treated*.

If he was Vermin, he wasn’t an employee. He wasn’t a victim. He was a contaminant.

And contaminants had a protocol.

Dale clicked *Vermin*.

The screen flashed red.

**CLASSIFICATION UPDATE: OPERATOR RE-CLASSIFIED AS CONTAMINANT (CLASS IX).** **PROTOCOL ACTIVATED: PURGE.**

The lights went out completely.

Darkness. Absolute, suffocating darkness.

Then, a sound. The click of a printer.

Dale didn’t move. He sat in the dark. He listened.

The printer was behind him. To his left. He turned his head.

A dot-matrix printer sat on a filing cabinet. It was old. Gray. Chugging.

*Chk-chk-chk-chk.*

It spat out a page. Then another. Then another.

Dale stood up. He walked toward the sound.

He reached out, touching the wall to navigate, and the carpet was soft. The air was cold.

He found the printer. He ripped the paper off.

It was a report.

*Thoroughness Rating: 10.0.* *Reason: Self-Identification as Contaminant.* *Action: Initiate Self-Purge.*

A knife appeared in his hand, and he hadn’t pulled it.

It was just there. A utility knife.

Sharp.

Clean, and he looked at the paper. He looked at the knife. He looked at his own reflection in the dark monitor.

If he cut himself, he would complete the protocol. He would verify the extermination. He would earn the points. He would survive.

But he would be cut, and he would bleed.

And bleeding attracted other things.

Dale hesitated. His contamination anxiety spiked.

If he cut himself, he created an entry point, and he created a wound.

A wound was a violation. A violation was an infestation.

If he didn’t cut himself, he failed the protocol. He remained a Vermin. He remained a contaminant.

He needed a third option, and he looked at the printer. The machine was chugging. It was producing more paper. He grabbed the ream.

He held it up.

He didn’t have a blade to cut himself. But he had paper. Paper was sharp.

Paper was clean. Paper was documentation.

He took a sheet. He tore it in half. The edge was ragged, but it was still thin.

He looked at his forearm, and he didn’t cut his skin. He cut the air above his skin.

He slashed the empty space.

The air tore. A wound opened. Not in his flesh. In the room.

Through the tear in the air, he saw the rift again. The descending horror. The one that had laughed.

It was closer now. It was pressing against the membrane of the office.

Dale didn’t pull back. He pushed his hand through the tear he had made.

His fingers disappeared.

Pain shot up his arm.

Not physical pain. Conceptual pain. The feeling of being erased.

Of being un-written, and he felt the horror’s attention snap to him.

It knew he was there.

It reached for him.

Dale pulled his hand back. He held a scrap of something.

It wasn’t flesh.

It wasn’t metal. It was a piece of the horror’s essence. A fragment of the rift.

It looked like black glass, jagged and sharp, and he held it up.

**NEW ITEM ACQUIRED: FRAGMENT OF THE SOURCE.** **FIELD GUIDE UPDATE: UNKNOWN ENTITY (SOURCE).** **WEAKNESS: UNSTABLE.** **ACTION: DOCUMENT AND DISCARD.**

Dale dropped the fragment on the floor. It shattered.

The lights came back on, and the dead woman was back in the chair.

But she was different.

Her face was blurred. Her features were shifting, morphing.

She looked like Dale. Then she looked like the rat. Then she looked like the broodmother.

"You are not licensed to handle Class IX materials," she said. Her voice was a chorus of screams.

"I’m not handling it," Dale said. "I’m reporting it."

He picked up the pen. He wrote on the final sheet.

*Report: Contamination Source Identified.* *Location: Level 9 Administrative Sector.* *Status: Active Breach.* *Action: Seal and Escalate.*

He didn’t sign it. He didn’t verify it. He didn’t eliminate it.

He filed it.

The system froze, and the screen went black.

The dead woman dissolved. Not into skin. Into data. Bits of code raining down like ash.

The office walls dissolved.

Dale fell, and he didn’t fall down. He fell *forward*. Into the paper. Into the report.

He landed on a concrete floor.

The smell hit him first. Damp earth.

Rot.

And something else. Something sweet and cloying. Like flowers left too long in a vase.

He stood up. He brushed off his pants.

He was in a sewer.

But not the sewer under his house. Not the sewer under the duplex.

This was vast. The ceiling was arched, brick, centuries old. The water was black and still.

And in the center of the room, standing in the water, was a man.

He was wearing a suit.

A gray suit. He was tall.

He was pale, and he held a clipboard that was the size of a door.

The man looked at Dale. He smiled.

His teeth were perfect.

White.

Too white.

"Dale Kowalski," the man said. "Level IX. Certified Exterminator. Congratulations."

Dale looked around. He saw other people. Dozens of them.

Standing in the water. All wearing suits.

All holding clipboards, and they were all looking at him.

"You passed the exam," the man said. "But you failed the practical."

"I documented the breach," Dale said.

"You escalated it," the man corrected. "You didn't contain it. You opened a door. You let the Source touch the System."

The man stepped forward. The water didn’t ripple around him.

"Welcome to the Licensing Authority, Dale," he said. "You’re not the technician anymore."

He held out a new badge.

"Welcome to the Audit."

The badge was black. It had no level. It had no name.

It had a word: *JUDGE.*

Dale looked at the badge. He looked at the man. He looked at the other technicians.

They weren’t technicians, and they were judges.

And they were all looking at him like he was the infestation.

The man took a step closer.

"Your first assignment," he said. "Is to audit yourself."

He pointed at Dale’s chest.

"Find the violation, Dale. Before we delete the file."

Dale looked down.

His shirt was open. His chest was bare.

And carved into his skin, in letters of glowing red light, were words.

*VIOLATION: EXISTENCE.*

The judges raised their clipboards.

The water rose.
