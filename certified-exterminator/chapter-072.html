Chapter 72: Writing Linda Marchetti

# Chapter 72

Dale's pen had stopped working an hour ago. Not broken.

Empty.

He'd been writing petitions on a clipboard he'd borrowed from a hardware store, and the ballpoint had given out somewhere between petition four and petition five. He'd switched to a pencil. The pencil was dull.

His hand was cramping in a way that made the letters come out like a child's.

Petition six. Linda Marchetti. 4412 Birchwood Lane. Classification: Structural Intrusion, Non-Reproductive. Behavioral markers: prolonged occupancy of enclosed structure, refusal to vacate, accumulation of organic material, reduced metabolic output.

He'd been writing these for nine hours. Six petitions.

Forty-one to go.

Sixty-three hours remaining. The math hadn't changed since he'd first done it, but the numbers felt different now. Heavier.

Like they had mass.

The Field Guide pulsed.

PETITION #6 SUBMITTED. PENDING.

PETITION #7 REQUIRES FIELD VERIFICATION.

PETITION #8 REQUIRES FIELD VERIFICATION.

PETITION #9 REQUIRES FIELD VERIFICATION.

Dale stopped. Read it again.

He flipped back through the System documentation he'd been carrying in his Field Guide since Certification Level III.

He'd read this section before.

He'd skimmed it. The rule was buried under a subsection about "evidentiary thresholds for non-standard classifications," and he'd assumed it meant a quick photo.

A timestamp. Something simple.

It didn't mean that.

FIELD VERIFICATION: To support a petition for reclassification, the filing technician must submit a completed Inspection Report conforming to Protocol 7-C (Behavioral Assessment, Habitat Analysis, Reproductive Status Confirmation). Report must include: (a) minimum 6 hours of continuous observation, (b) photographic or equivalent documentation of habitat conditions, (c) written analysis of behavioral patterns against species-typical infestation markers, (d) signed attestation of technician confidence level.

Six hours. Per entity. Forty-one entities. Two hundred and forty-six hours.

He had sixty-three.

Dale set the clipboard down. He looked at the house across the street.

The garage door was open.

Inside, visible in the blue light of a work lamp that had been running for three days, Linda Marchetti sat on a concrete floor surrounded by a wall of shredded newspaper, broken pallets, and what appeared to be a nest of insulation batting. She hadn't moved in the last four hours. He'd been watching from his kitchen window.

She was the strongest behavioral match. The Authority's algorithm had flagged her first. If the algorithm was right, she was an infestation. If it was wrong, she was a woman in a garage.

Dale picked up his clipboard. He walked across the street.

The garage smelled like old paper and something chemical.

Not acid. Not the ammonia-and-sulfur signature of a true structural intrusion.

Something closer to adhesive.

Contact cement, maybe. Or polyurethane. Linda was sitting cross-legged in the center of the nest with her hands folded in her lap and her eyes open but not focusing on anything.

Her breathing was slow. Too slow.

Maybe eight breaths per minute.

A resting rate for someone in deep sleep, but her eyes were open.

Dale set up his observation position at the garage entrance. He started his timer. He started a new page on the clipboard to write the habitat analysis.

The nest was wrong. That was the first thing.

A structural intrusion nest followed specific architectural logic: entry points sealed, waste directed to a corner, nesting material arranged in concentric layers with the center reserved for rest. Linda's pile was none of those things. The newspaper was shredded but scattered randomly.

The pallets weren't stacked, they were just leaning against the walls. The insulation batting was torn into strips and draped over the pallets like someone had been trying to build something and given up halfway through.

He documented it.

Photographed it with his phone. Wrote the behavioral assessment in real time. Linda's breathing rate, her blink frequency, her lack of response to his presence.

He noted the contact cement smell again.

Found the source: a tube of construction adhesive on the workbench, uncapped, dried at the nozzle. She'd been trying to glue something.

The pieces were on the floor.

A birdhouse. A small wooden birdhouse, broken into four pieces, with adhesive on the joins.

Dale stared at the birdhouse for a long time, and he finished the six-hour observation at 4:47 in the morning. Linda had not moved.

Her breathing had not changed.

Her blink rate had dropped to one blink every forty seconds, which was consistent with catatonia but not with infestation.

True structural intrusions didn't blink at all. They didn't have eyes that worked the same way.

He wrote the attestation. Confidence level: high. Classification error: probable.

Behavioral pattern consistent with acute stress response in a non-structural organism. Not infestation.

Grief, maybe.

Or shock. The birdhouse was a detail that mattered. Someone had built it.

Someone was trying to fix it.

Infestations didn't fix things, and he submitted the Field Verification.

PETITION #7 (MARCHETTI, L.) FILED. PENDING.

He looked at the timer.

Fifty-one hours. Thirty-four petitions remaining. He could do Linda.

He could not do thirty-four.

The Precedent Weight rule was in the System documentation too.

He'd read it once, months ago, in the section about classification disputes.

It said that when a reclassification was approved for one entity within a grouped classification, the Authority's algorithm would automatically review all other entities sharing that classification tag. Not approve them.

Review them. But review was enough. It was enough to reset the timer.

To buy time.

One petition. Won. That was the play.

He needed the Authority to approve Linda's reclassification. And he needed it to happen before the original reclassification order reactivated on any of the other forty-six.

Dale was still in the garage when Maren Voss arrived, and she walked up the driveway at 6:15 a.m. In a gray coverall with the Authority's insignia on the left breast.

She was maybe fifty. Close-cropped hair. A Field Guide holstered at her hip like a sidearm.

Her clipboard was already full.

Dense handwriting. Tabs.

She stopped at the garage entrance and looked at Dale. Dale looked at her.

"You're filing petitions," she said.

"Yes."

"On the Marchetti woman."

"Yes."

She stepped inside. She didn't look at Linda. She looked at the nest. She pulled a pen from her coverall and started writing.

"You know what I'm filing?" she said.

"I don't."

"Expeditions."

Dale's pen stopped. "You're filing Expeditions on all forty-seven."

"Twelve so far. They're processing faster than your petitions. Expeditions go to the front of the queue."

"Why?"

Maren looked at Linda. She looked at the nest. She looked at the birdhouse. She wrote something on her clipboard.

"Because she's not grieving," Maren said.

"She's not in shock. She's nesting. You're reading a human behavioral pattern into a non-human one.

That's a common error for Level III technicians. The Authority's algorithm doesn't make that mistake."

"The algorithm flagged her as a match. It doesn't mean it's right."

"The algorithm is the system, Dale.

You're petitioning the system to override itself." She turned a page on her clipboard.

"I've been doing this for eleven years. I've filed two hundred and thirty petitions.

I've won forty-one. You know why I won those forty-one?"

"Why?"

"Because the entity was already reclassified. I filed after the fact.

I didn't fight the algorithm.

I worked with it. " She looked at him. " You're fighting the algorithm. On forty-seven entities.

With a Level III certification.

You're going to lose, and the clock is going to run out, and the Authority is going to do what it does."

"What does it do?"

Maren didn't answer, and she finished her observation notes; she walked to Linda, crouched, and held her hand in front of Linda's face. Linda didn't react.

Maren counted. She wrote the number down.

"Three minutes to full non-response," she said.

"That's not grief.

That's a structural intrusion in dormant state. The metabolic rate matches.

The blink rate matches. The nesting architecture matches."

"The nesting architecture doesn't match," Dale said. "The layers are wrong.

The entry points aren't sealed.

There's no waste management. She's not orienting toward the structure. She's sitting in the center of a pile she built in a panic, and she's trying to fix a birdhouse."

Maren stood up. She looked at the birdhouse. She looked at the adhesive. She looked at Dale.

"You're confident," she said.

"Yes."

"That's not the same as being right."

She walked out of the garage. Dale watched her go. She was already writing on her clipboard. Already filing.

He turned back to Linda, and he sat down on the concrete floor; opened his Field Guide and pulled up the Precedent Weight section again.

He read it three times.

He checked the submission timestamp on his petition. It was pending. He checked the Expeditions Maren had filed.

They were also pending, but the Authority's queue showed them at the front.

Then his Field Guide pulsed.

CONTESTED VERIFICATION DETECTED.

SUBJECT: MARCHETTI, L.

OPPOSING VERIFICATION FILED BY: VOSS, M. (CERT IV).

CONTESTED VERIFICATIONS TRIGGER AUTOMATIC ADJUDICATION.

ADJUDICATION STATUS: INITIATED.

Dale stood up.

His hands were shaking.

Not from fear. From the cold.

It was February and he'd been sitting on concrete for six hours.

The next line appeared.

ADJUDICATION PARAMETERS: BOTH TECHNICIANS PRESENT AT SUBJECT LOCATION. VERIFICATION TO BE CONDUCTED SIMULTANEOUSLY. ADJUDICATOR ARRIVING: 06:47.

Dale looked at his watch. It was 6:31. Sixteen minutes.

He looked at Linda. She was still sitting. Still breathing. Still not blinking at a human rate.

He looked at the birdhouse. Four pieces. Contact cement. A person who had been trying to fix something.

The Field Guide pulsed one more time.

ADJUDICATOR IDENTIFIED: AUTHORITY OPERATIVE, CLASS 9.

Dale's stomach dropped. Class 9.

He'd never seen a Class 9 operative.

He'd never even seen a Class 5; the highest he'd interacted with was the man who'd delivered the original reclassification notice. That man had been Class 3.

A Class 9 adjudicator meant this wasn't a routine review. This was a precedent-setting case. The Authority was going to decide, right here, right now, whether a human being was an infestation.

He sat back down on the concrete. He opened his clipboard. He picked up the pencil.

Sixteen minutes, and the report had a beginning.
