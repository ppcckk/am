Chapter 143: One Can, One Clipboard

# Chapter 143

The clipboard was missing a corner.

Gwen Thorne noticed it first. She stood on the cracked asphalt of Dale Kowalski's driveway, her gray suit immaculate, her void-black eyes fixed on the battered wooden board tucked under the man's arm.

It was chipped.

A small splinter jutted from the bottom left, sharp enough to catch on fabric. To Gwen, who had processed ten thousand personnel files for the Void Bureau, that splinter was an obscenity.

It represented disorder. Sloppiness. A lack of structural integrity in the tool meant to enforce order.

"Mr.

Kowalski," she said. Her voice did not echo.

It landed flat, absorbed by the humid air of suburban Ohio.

"Your filing system is compromised."

Dale did not look at her. He was looking at the street. Specifically, at the storm drain three houses down, where the water was rising in thick, oily swirls that defied gravity.

"Ma'am," Dale said, not turning. "I'm on a clock.

If you're from the Homeowners Association, my citation for improper mulch placement is currently under appeal with the municipal clerk.

If you're from the Authority, I have a pending grievance regarding unauthorized entry by a non-licensed entity.

And if you're from that other place—the one with the hourglass—then you're trespassing on private property during active remediation."

Gwen tilted her head. The motion was jerky, like a bird adjusting its gaze.

"We are not trespassing. We are conducting an audit. Your file is flagged for irregularities.

You terminated a Class IV Void-Stalker without filing the requisite Post-Elimination Decomposition Report. You also failed to sanitize your Field Guide entry for three days, resulting in a cross-contamination penalty that should have stripped your certification."

"It wasn't a Void-Stalker," Dale said.

He finally turned. His face was pale, but his hands were steady. One held the clipboard.

The other held a heavy-duty industrial flashlight, the kind used in crawlspaces.

"It was a Misidentified Class III Shade-Worm. I documented the frass.

I isolated the nest.

I eliminated the biomass. The Authority's automated system glitched. I filed the correction form.

You can check the timestamp."

"The correction form was rejected."

"Because the auditor didn't exist."

Gwen smiled.

It was a thin, bloodless thing.

"Correct. The auditor did not exist.

Which means you submitted the form to a non-entity. That is fraud, Mr. Kowalski.

Not against the Authority. Against reality."

Dale's jaw tightened. He looked at the black car idling behind her. The engine hummed, a low, vibrating thrum that felt less like combustion and more like a headache forming behind the eyes.

"You're wasting my time," Dale said. "And you're violating protocol. Step back. I have an infestation to clear."

"Infestation?" Gwen's black eyes narrowed.

"The scan shows no active biological threats in this residential zone. The Rift closure was successful. The local population has been stabilized.

You are chasing ghosts, Dale.

Or you are hiding something."

Before Dale could answer, the storm drain erupted.

It wasn't a splash.

It was a geyser of black sludge. From the depths of the pipe, something emerged. It was long, segmented, and slick with a mucus that shimmered with an iridescent, oily sheen.

It had too many legs. And they were moving in a pattern that suggested coordination, not instinct.

Gwen stepped forward, her hand moving into her suit pocket. She pulled out a pen. It looked like a ballpoint, but the tip glowed with a faint, sickly violet light.

"Contamination," she said. "Unlicensed. Unregistered."

Dale didn't reach for his weapon. He reached for his clipboard.

He flipped the page.

His handwriting was cramped, dense, and legible.

He didn't write a spell. He didn't chant.

He wrote a date. He wrote a time. He wrote a location.

Then he looked up.

"Identify," Dale said.

The creature shrieked. It was a wet, tearing sound. It lunged at Gwen.

She didn't move. The violet light from her pen flared. A barrier of geometric shapes snapped into existence around her, a hexagonal lattice of pure force. The creature slammed into it and bounced off, its carapace cracking.

"Hostile entity," Gwen said. "Initiating containment protocol."

"No," Dale said. He stepped forward.

"That's a cross-contamination risk.

Your barrier is calibrated for ethereal entities. This is physical. You're going to shatter its carapace, and the fragments are acidic.

Look at the residue."

Gwen paused. She looked at the sludge pooling on the pavement. It was eating into the asphalt, bubbling and hissing.

"Acidic?" she asked.

"Precursor to a Phosphor-Caustic reaction. If you break it, you burn the neighborhood. You burn the house. You burn your suit."

Gwen looked at Dale. For the first time, uncertainty flickered in those endless black depths.

"What do you propose?"

"Isolate," Dale said. He pointed to the storm drain.

"Seal the exit.

I'll handle the entry. You handle the perimeter. Don't touch it.

Don't look at it directly. The pheromones it releases cause visual hallucinations if you're not wearing a respirator.

Do you have a respirator?"

"I have a suit," Gwen said.

"That's not a respirator.

That's a containment field. It won't filter airborne particulates." Dale pulled a dust mask from his breast pocket.

It was an N95, yellowed with age. He held it out.

Gwen stared at the mask. Then at Dale.

"You want me to wear a paper mask?"

"It has a valve. It fits over your face. It's not stylish. But it works."

Gwen hesitated. The creature was circling the hexagonal barrier, testing the integrity of the force. Its legs clicked against the pavement, a sound like dry bones breaking.

"This is irregular," Gwen said.

"This is pest control," Dale corrected. "Do it, or we both get dissolved."

Gwen took the mask. She put it on.

The suit's collar sealed around the straps.

It looked absurd. A woman in a sharp gray suit, wearing a yellow medical mask, standing in front of a monster.

But it worked.

The creature stopped.

It tilted its head. It could not sense their fear.

It could not sense their magical resonance.

It could only sense the physical presence of two biological organisms wearing protective gear.

"Isolate," Dale repeated.

He moved. Not with speed, but with precision.

He kicked a heavy plastic drop cloth from his bag.

It unfolded in the air, a large sheet of polyethylene.

He spread it over the storm drain opening, weighing the edges down with cinder blocks he pulled from a nearby garden wall.

The creature shrieked. It tried to dive back down.

"Block the exit," Dale shouted.

Gwen raised her pen. The violet light shifted. Instead of a barrier, she created a cage. A grid of light, suspended in the air, trapping the creature in the center of the driveway.

"Document," Dale said. He pulled out a camera. Not a smartphone.

A bulky, old-fashioned digital camera with a flash. He snapped a picture.

Then another.

Then a close-up of the legs.

"Verify," he muttered.

The System notification appeared in the corner of his vision, invisible to Gwen but clear to him.

**FIELD GUIDE UPDATE: PHOSPHOR-CAUSTIC LARVAE.** **WEAKNESS IDENTIFIED: OXYGEN DEPRIVATION DURING MOULTING CYCLE.**

The creature was molting.

That was why it was on the surface. It was vulnerable.

Its shell was soft, translucent.

The acid was a defense mechanism, but it relied on a steady supply of oxygen to maintain the chemical reaction.

"Dale," Gwen said, her voice muffled by the mask. "It's getting angry. The barrier is holding, but the acid is eating the asphalt. We have maybe two minutes before it breaches the foundation of the house to your left."

"Good," Dale said. "Move it to the center of the lawn."

"What?"

"The barrier. Move it. Lure it out. I need open space to work."

Gwen gritted her teeth. She stepped forward, dragging the cage of light with her hand. The creature followed, confused by the movement. It dragged its long, slimy body across the grass.

Dale reached into his toolbox. He pulled out a canister.

It wasn't bug spray.

It was a pressurized tank of nitrogen.

Used for freezing pipes in winter.

"Oxygen deprivation," Dale said. "Nitrogen displaces oxygen. It's inert.

It won't react with the acid. It will suffocate it."

"That's not a protocol," Gwen said. "That's improvisation."

"It's a loophole," Dale said. "The Authority recognizes chemical agents as valid extermination tools if the dosage is correct. Nitrogen is a Class II agent. I have the license."

He pulled the pin.

The hissing was loud.

A jet of white fog blasted toward the creature. It hit the translucent, molting shell. The creature writhed.

It didn't burn.

It didn't explode. It simply slowed.

Its legs tangled.

Its movements became sluggish. The acid stopped bubbling.

The air around the creature grew cold. The nitrogen froze the mucus on its body. The chemical reaction died.

"Verify," Dale said. He walked closer.

He used his flashlight to check the creature's spiracles.

They were closed.

The creature was suffocating. It was dying.

"Is it dead?" Gwen asked.

"It's incapacitated. I need to finish it. Documentation requires elimination."

Dale pulled out his primary tool. It wasn't a sword. It wasn't a staff.

It was a heavy, steel-shod shovel. He had modified the edge with a serrated tip, specifically for cutting through chitin.

He swung.

The blade bit into the soft, frozen carapace.

The creature spasmed. Once. Twice.

Then it went still.

Silence returned to the street.

Dale straightened up. He wiped the shovel on the grass. He pulled out his notebook. He wrote.

**INCIDENT REPORT #44-B.** **SUBJECT: PHOSPHOR-CAUSTIC LARVAE (UNREGISTERED VARIANT).** **ACTION: ISOLATION VIA PHYSICAL BARRIER, OXYGEN DEPRIVATION VIA NITROGEN INJECTION, MECHANICAL ELIMINATION.** **THOROUGHNESS: 8.4.**

The notification pinged.

**TREATMENT POINTS AWARDED: 45.** **CERTIFICATION LEVEL I MAINTAINED.** **WARNING: UNAUTHORIZED AGENT USED. VOID BUREAU OBSERVATION LOGGED.**

Dale looked at the log. He didn't care.

Gwen lowered her hand. The violet light faded. She took off the mask. Her face was expressionless.

"You used nitrogen," she said. "It's not on the approved list for Class IV threats."

"It was a Class III misidentification," Dale said. "I corrected the file.

You saw the Field Guide update.

I identified the weakness *before* combat. That's a targeted damage bonus. It's in the rules."

"The rules don't apply to the Void Bureau."

"Rules apply to everyone who breathes air in this zip code," Dale said. He pointed to the dead creature.

"Look at the carcass.

See the way the shell cracked?

That's a structural failure. It means the infestation came from below.

Not from the Rift. From the sewer lines."

Gwen looked at the body. Then at the drain.

"Seewers?"

"Rats," Dale said. "Dimensional Vermin.

Rattus profundus.

Remember? My first kill. They nest in the dark, wet places.

They eat the waste.

They breed. They grow big when they eat magic."

Gwen's eyes widened. Just a fraction.

"You're saying the Rift didn't open," she said. "You're saying something *pushed* it open from below."

"I'm saying the infestation is systemic," Dale said.

"And your 'auditors' didn't come to kill me. They came to stop me from finding the source. Because if I find the source, I can fix it.

And if I fix it, your budget gets cut."

Gwen took a step back.

She reached into her pocket again.

This time, she didn't pull out a pen. She pulled out a business card.

It was black. Embossed with the hourglass.

"We don't have budgets, Mr. Kowalski. We have quotas."

She handed him the card.

"This is a summons. To the Void Bureau.

You have twenty-four hours to report.

If you don't, we will revoke your license. Not just your Authority license. Your legal existence.

Your tax ID.

Your social security number. All of it."

Dale took the card. He looked at it. He looked at the dead monster. He looked at the rising sludge in the drain.

"I can't go," Dale said. "I have to treat the nest."

"Then you are an anomaly," Gwen said. "And anomalies are purged."

She turned to walk back to the car.

"Wait," Dale said.

Gwen stopped. She didn't turn around.

"Why?" Dale asked. "Why help me? You could have let it kill me. Or you could have killed it yourself."

Gwen's shoulders tensed.

"Because you documented it," she said. "And for the first time in a century, the paperwork was correct."

She got into the car. The door slammed. The engine roared, louder now. The headlights snapped on, blindingly bright.

The car didn't drive away.

It reversed.

It drove *over* the dead creature.

Then it turned the corner, accelerating hard.

Dale stood there. The wind from the car tugged at his jacket.

He looked at the business card in his hand.

On the back, in tiny print, was a list.

**ACTIVE CASES:** **1. THE SUBURBAN SWARM.** **2. THE MALL OF HORRORS.** **3. THE SEWERS OF CLEVELAND.**

He looked at the sewers entry. It was highlighted in red.

Dale pulled out his flashlight. He clicked it on. He shone it into the storm drain.

The sludge was gone.

But the darkness below was moving.

Not one creature.

Thousands.

And they were climbing up the pipe.

Dale dropped the card. He picked up his shovel.

He didn't have a respirator anymore.

He didn't have nitrogen.

He had one can of spray.

And he had a clipboard.

He started running.
