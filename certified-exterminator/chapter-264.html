Chapter 264: Audited Reality

# Chapter 264

The ink didn't dry. It writhed.

Dale watched the letters on the clipboard shift like disturbed sediment. His own handwriting, recorded seconds ago, was rewriting itself into a dense, bureaucratic block text that glowed with a faint, radioactive violet. The pen in his hand clicked twice, a nervous tic he couldn't suppress.

*Code Violation: Structural Integrity of Local Reality. Section 4, Subsection B: Unauthorized Expansion of Dimensional Boundaries.*

The words floated above the paper. A tooltip hovered beside them, pulsing gently.

**TREATMENT POINTS FOR DOCUMENTATION: 500** **STATUS: PENDING APPROVAL** **RISK OF REALITY COLLAPSE: HIGH**

The agent on the ground was sobbing. Not the dramatic, heaving sobs of a movie villain, but the wet, pathetic sounds of a man realizing his career and his life were ending simultaneously.

His license had been voided by the Auditor's gaze.

In this system, that wasn't just a firing. It was an erasure. The agent's hands were turning translucent, the edges of his fingers blurring into the asphalt like watercolor on wet paper.

Dale didn't look at the agent. He looked at the sky.

The blackness wasn't just covering the stars, which was a lid. A heavy, suffocating lid pressing down on the suburban street, bending the light around the streetlamps until they looked like bent wire.

The Rift hadn't closed; it had inverted.

The hole in the world was now a ceiling, and something massive was scraping against it from the outside.

A sound echoed through the neighborhood. Not a roar, but a chittering. A million tiny legs moving in unison.

Dale clicked his pen again. He needed to stabilize the documentation before the audit team arrived.

If they came and found the paperwork incomplete, or worse, if they found it *incorrect*, the penalty wasn't just a loss of points.

It was a citation.

And a citation for Reality Collapse carried a mandatory suspension of existence for the inspector.

He turned to the nearest house. The front yard was littered with debris—shingles, gutters, and what looked like a pile of discarded human skin that wasn't actually skin, but shed chitin from a Class-4 Skitterer that had died in the walls.

"Ma'am," Dale said, his voice flat, professional. He didn't raise it. Shouting attracted attention. Attention attracted audits.

The door creaked open. A woman stood in the doorway, holding a frying pan. Her eyes were wide, fixed on the black sky, then darting to Dale.

"Is it... is it gone?" she asked.

"Dominion status is pending," Dale said. He pointed his pen at the pile of chitin in her yard. "I need to inspect that accumulation. Is it yours?"

The woman blinked.

"The what?"

"The exuviae. The shed exoskeletons. Did you dispose of them, or are they part of the infestation cycle?"

"I... I swept them up," she whispered. "But they keep coming back. Every time I put them in the bin, they crawl out."

"Improper containment," Dale noted. He didn't write it down yet. He waited for the system to prompt him.

A notification chimed in his peripheral vision.

**NEW ENTRY: REALITY CHITIN. CLASS: UNSTABLE.** **WEAKNESS: DOCUMENTED REALITY ANCHORS.** **HINT: You cannot treat a violation if you do not acknowledge the code.**

Dale frowned. The system was changing. Usually, you identified the monster, found its weakness, and sprayed it.

Here, the "monster" was the physics of the neighborhood itself. The "weakness" was bureaucratic compliance.

He looked at the chitin pile. It was pulsing. The black sludge from the Auditor's sewer creature had seeped into it. The chitin was reacting to the presence of the Void.

"If you don't separate the foreign material from the local substrate," Dale said, tapping the side of his head, "the contamination spreads. Your house will be cited for non-compliance with Local Physics Ordinance 114."

The woman lowered the frying pan.

"What does that mean?"

"It means your house is becoming a portal," Dale said. "And I need to file a variance request before the Inspector General gets here."

He turned away from her and walked toward the center of the street. The translucent agent was gone now. Just a faint stain on the pavement, smelling of ozone and regret.

Dale opened his Field Guide. The interface was different. The usual clean, white pages were marred by static. The entries for *Rattus profundus* and *Acid Beetle* were grayed out, marked **SUSPENDED PENDING RE-EVALUATION**.

A new section had appeared at the top. **CROSS-DIMENSIONAL COMPLIANCE.**

He tapped it.

**PROTOCOL: TEMPORAL STASIS VIA PAPERWORK.** **COST: 100 TP PER MINUTE.** **EFFECT: Freezes a localized area of reality, preventing further dimensional bleed-through, provided the observer maintains strict adherence to procedural narrative.**

Dale stared at the words. It was absurd. It was the most absurd thing he had ever read, and he had once read a manual for treating bed bugs that required singing a specific lullaby to the nest to induce sleep.

But he had no other options, and the black lid was lowering. The chittering was getting louder. From the storm drains, from the cracks in the sidewalk, things were emerging.

Not monsters. *Shadows.* Shapes that looked like people, but stretched, wrong, made of static and shadow.

They were looking at him.

Dale clicked his pen. He wrote.

*Inspection Report: Neighborhood Zone 4. Time: 14:02. Observer: Dale Kowalski, License No. 8940-B.*

As he wrote, the world slowed. The rain, which had begun to fall, hung suspended in the air. The shadows creeping out of the drains froze mid-crawl. The black lid above stopped its descent.

**TP DRAIN: 100** **TP DRAIN: 100** **TP DRAIN: 100**

His Treatment Points counter, which had been hovering at a comfortable 12,400, ticked down.

He had to keep writing. He had to describe the scene with perfect, legal precision. Any ambiguity, any error, would break the stasis.

*The shadows are not entities,* he wrote, his hand moving faster than his brain. *They are procedural errors. Manifestations of unfiled complaints. They lack mass because they lack documentation.*

The shadows twitched. One of them, a tall, thin figure with too many joints, snapped its head toward him.

Dale didn't look up. He kept writing.

*To resolve this infestation, the inspector must validate the local code. The residents are compliant.

The infrastructure is sound, and the violation is external. The source is the Rift.*

The TP counter dropped. 12,000. 11,500.

He was burning through his reserve. He had earned these points by cleaning up the county's worst infestations, by finding the nests, by being thorough. Now he was spending them to hold the sky up.

A figure stepped out from behind a parked car. It wasn't a shadow.

It was a man.

A human man, wearing a suit that was too perfect, too symmetrical.

His face was a blur of features, like a painting left in the rain.

Dale stopped writing.

The man smiled. It wasn't a nice smile. It was the smile of a manager who has found your file.

"Mr. Kowalski," the man said. His voice was smooth, like polished stone. "You are exceeding your authority."

Dale kept his pen moving. *The entity claims jurisdiction over the inspection, but lacks a Class-IX license. This is a trespass.*

"You are wasting points on a temporary fix," the man said. He walked closer. The pavement didn't crack under his feet; it seemed to absorb him.

"You are treating the symptom. You are not treating the cause."

"The cause is the Rift," Dale said, not looking up. "And the Rift is a structural violation. I am filing the report."

"The report is a fiction," the man said. "Reality does not care about your forms, Dale. Reality cares about power."

The man raised a hand. The air around him distorted. The shadows behind him surged forward, breaking the stasis. The rain crashed down.

Dale felt the weight of the sky hit him. His knees buckled. The TP counter flashed red. **CRITICAL LOW.**

He scrambled up, dropping the clipboard, and he couldn't maintain the stasis. He had to switch to combat protocol. But he had no weapons.

He had no protocols loaded for *Bureaucratic Overlap*.

He reached into his vest pocket and pulled out a small, laminated card. His Certification Card. Level IV. It felt hot in his hand.

"Level Nine," the creature in the sewer had said. *The Auditor is coming.*

This man wasn't the Auditor. He was a middle manager. A regional supervisor.

"Give me the clipboard," the man said. He extended his hand.

"I will file it.

I will close the case. You will be reprimanded, but you will survive.

"

Dale looked at the clipboard on the ground. The ink was moving faster now. The text was forming a new sentence.

**CITATION ISSUED: UNLICENSED INTERVENTION.** **PENALTY: DEMOTION TO CLASS I.** **COUNTERMEASURE AVAILABLE: APPEAL VIA PARADOX.**

Dale stared at the words. *Appeal via Paradox.*

He didn't know what that meant. The system never gave hints unless you had unlocked the advanced tier. And he was Level IV.

But he had been a pest control technician for fourteen years. He knew one thing better than anyone else. He knew how to exploit the fine print.

He looked at the man in the suit.

"You say I'm exceeding my authority."

"Your license is Class IV," the man said. "You are inspecting a Class IX phenomenon. That is a breach."

"Is it?" Dale asked. He picked up the clipboard.

He didn't put it down, and he held it like a shield.

"My license covers 'Pest Control.' Does the definition of 'Pest' in the Licensing Code include 'Divine Interventions'?"

The man paused. The blur of his face sharpened for a second, revealing a look of genuine confusion.

"Read Appendix C," Dale said. He didn't know if he was right. He was guessing. But he had read every manual, every addendum, every update to the Licensing Code since he entered the system.

He remembered a footnote. A tiny, easily missed line in the 2008 update.

*'Pest' includes any biological or metaphysical entity that causes nuisance, damage, or contamination to licensed property.*

The man's eyes narrowed.

"You are twisting the law."

"I am applying it," Dale said. "If you are the supervisor, you have a Level IX clearance. Do you?"

The man went silent. The air grew cold.

"If you don't," Dale continued, his voice steady, "then you are unlicensed to be here. And I am obligated to issue a citation."

He raised his pen. He pointed it at the man.

**SYSTEM PROMPT: CITATION ISSUED.** **TARGET: UNLICENSED ENTITY.** **PENALTY: REALITY REJECTION.**

The man screamed. It wasn't a sound; it was a tear in the audio spectrum. His form unravelled.

The suit dissolved into code, into binary, into nothingness. The shadows shrieked and scattered.

The black lid above shuddered. It pulled back, just an inch.

Dale stood there, breathing hard. His TP counter was at 1,200. He had spent almost everything.

He looked down at his clipboard. The text had changed again.

**APPEAL ACCEPTED.** **NEW STATUS: AUDITOR CANDIDATE.** **WARNING: YOU ARE NOW ON THE RADAR.**

Dale wiped his hands on his pants. They were shaking. He forced them to stop.

A car door opened behind him. It was the woman from the house. She was holding a flashlight, not a frying pan.

"Did you... did you fix it?" she asked.

Dale looked at the sky. The blackness was retreating, but slowly. The chittering had stopped, but the silence felt heavier than the noise.

"No," Dale said. "I just filed a temporary injunction."

He looked at the field where the man had stood.

There was a mark on the ground. Not a stain. A symbol.

A complex, geometric shape that looked like a stamp, which was a **Notice of Hearing**.

Dale picked it up. It was warm.

**DATE: TOMORROW.** **LOCATION: THE VOID.** **DEFENDANT: DALE KOWALSKI.** **CHARGE: INSUBORDINATION TO COSMIC ORDER.**

He looked at the date. Tomorrow.

He looked at his TP counter. 1,200 points. Not enough to survive a hearing in the Void.

He needed more, and he needed a promotion. He needed Level V.

To get Level V, he needed to pass the next exam. The next exam was usually a practical test. A hunt.

But there was no monster left to hunt. The street was empty.

Except for one thing.

In the center of the street, where the man had been, a single object remained. A briefcase.

Black.

Leather.

Locked, which was pulsing.

Dale walked over to it. He didn't open it. He knelt down and inspected it.

**ITEM IDENTIFIED: CLASSIFIED FILE OF THE MISSING INSPECTOR.** **CONTENTS: UNKNOWN.** **DANGER LEVEL: EXTREME.** **REWARD: +5,000 TP AND IMMEDIATE PROMOTION TO LEVEL V IF DOCUMENTED CORRECTLY.**

If he opened it, he might die. If he didn't, he would fail the audit.

Dale clicked his pen. He wrote one last line on his report.

*Item recovered. Pending review.*

He stood up. He picked up the briefcase. It was heavier than it looked. It felt like it contained the weight of the entire sky.

He turned and walked toward his truck. He had to get home. He had to prepare.

As he drove away, his phone buzzed. A text from an unknown number.

*You signed the wrong form, Dale. The Hearing is tonight.*

Dale slammed on the brakes. The truck skidded on the wet asphalt.

He looked at the briefcase on the passenger seat. The lock was clicking.

The lid popped open.

Inside was not a document, which was a mirror.

And in the mirror, Dale saw himself. But not as he was. He saw himself with four arms. And no face.

The system notification flashed one final time before the screen went black.

**NEW PROTOCOL UNLOCKED: MIMICRY OF THE VOID.** **COST: YOUR IDENTITY.**

Dale stared at the mirror. The reflection smiled.

He didn't, and he threw the briefcase out the window. It shattered on the road, and the shards of glass turned into black insects that scurried away into the darkness.

He was safe. For now.

But his reflection was still smiling in his mind.

And he knew, with cold certainty, that the next time he looked in a mirror, he wouldn't be alone.

He started the engine, knowing what waited at the door.
