Chapter 48: Node Seven Compromised

# Chapter 48

Dale was already dialing when the first technician died.

Not died. That was the wrong word.

Gary Kowalski was not his relative, just a name collision, and Gary in Lakewood was not dead.

Gary was still alive. His guide was just no longer Gary's.

Dale saw it happen on his own Field Guide, and the entry for SRPC had a sub-node that wasn't there thirty seconds ago. A status indicator. It read: NODE 7: COMPROMISED.

He'd been counting.

Twenty-three technicians. He'd started with himself and Marisol.

Then the Authority's distribution went out and the count was live.

He'd been watching the node counter tick upward in real time, each new infection registering as a tiny shift in the entry's metadata.

Node 7 was Gary.

Dale set the phone down. Picked it back up. Dialed Marisol.

"Gary's guide is writing itself," he said. "Node 7. He didn't close it in time."

"I told him to."

"Protocol doesn't require closing. It requires not reading. There's a difference."

A pause. He could hear her breathing. Then: "What do we do?"

Dale looked at the entry. SRPC.

Sub-entry 001: ORIGIN.

Status: COMPLETE.

The three words beneath it had not changed. IT WAS ALREADY HERE.

He flipped to the Field Guide's protocol reference section. His own notes. Fourteen years of accumulated knowledge about how infestations behave, how they spread, how they're contained.

He'd been cross-referencing since the first notification. The answer had been sitting in his notes for twenty minutes.

He just hadn't wanted to use it.

"Marisol. Cross-contamination penalty. What does it do to the entry?"

"It contaminates it for twenty-four hours."

"What does contaminated mean mechanically?"

"It can't be accessed. Can't be read. Can't be updated. The Authority locks it."

"So the parasite can't propagate through a contaminated entry."

Another pause. Longer this time. "You want to deliberately misidentify it."

"I want to apply a protocol designed for a different species. The penalty triggers automatically. The entry locks for twenty-four hours. The parasite loses its vector."

"That's not treatment. That's suppression."

"It's a stopgap. I need twenty-four hours to figure out the actual protocol."

"Dale, the cross-contamination penalty also hits your Thoroughness Rating."

"I know."

"You're at 9.2. You can't afford a hit."

"I can't afford twenty-three nodes feeding it either."

He hung up. Opened the protocol toolkit. Scrolled to the section for Class-1 dimensional vermin.

Found the entry for Rattus profundus, the first thing he'd ever identified.

The big rats from the crawlspace. Fourteen chapters ago.

Felt like a different life.

The protocol for Rattus profundus was straightforward. Identify. Isolate.

Apply targeted elimination agent. Verify.

He selected it. Target: SRPC. Sub-entry 001: ORIGIN.

The Authority's confirmation prompt appeared.

WARNING: PROTOCOL MISMATCH.

Rattus profundus elimination protocol is not indicated for SRPC-class entities.

Applying this protocol will trigger CROSS-CONTAMINATION PENALTY. Field Guide entry for SRPC will be locked for 24 hours.

Thoroughness Rating penalty: -1.5, and he pressed confirm.

The Field Guide shuddered. Not physically.

The text rearranged.

The SRPC entry went gray. The sub-entries collapsed into a line: ENTRY CONTAMINATED. QUARANTINE ACTIVE. 23:59:47.

He watched the countdown.

The node counter stopped. No new infections.

The parasite had lost its vector.

For now.

His Thoroughness Rating dropped to 7.7. He felt it like a physical thing.

A tightening in his chest.

Fourteen years of professional standards, reduced by a deliberate violation.

He'd done it before, in a different context. A false entry in a logbook.

A shortcut on a job that should have been done properly. The feeling was the same.

His phone rang. Not the Authority. A number he didn't recognize.

He answered.

"This is Dale."

"Dale, it's Voss. I'm at the Authority office on Euclid."

The Authority didn't have an office. The Authority was a system.

It had been a system since before the Rift.

Everyone knew this. Dale knew this.

"Voss, what are you talking about?"

"There's a door. A door was always there. It's in the basement of the county building. I just walked through it."

"Voss, don't do that."

"I already did it. There's a desk in there. There's a man at the desk. He's writing entries."

Dale stood up. The room he was in was Marisol's apartment, a second-floor walkup in Parma, and he was standing in her kitchen in his work boots with the Field Guide in his hand and a countdown clock ticking in his peripheral vision.

"Voss. What is the man writing?"

"Entries.

Field Guide entries. He's writing them into a book.

A physical book.

And the entries are already in our guides. I can see them. They're new.

They just appeared."

"What entries?"

"I can't read them. The text is moving. But I think I can read one. One of them."

"Voss, stop."

"It says SUB-ENTRY 002: AUTHORITY."

The countdown in Dale's vision flickered.

The quarantine clock skipped. 23:59:47 became 23:59:46.

Then it went back to 23:59:47. Then 23:59:46.

It was stuttering.

"Voss. What did you say?"

"SUB-ENTRY 002. AUTHORITY.

It's a new sub-entry.

It's in my guide. I didn't write it. I didn't read it.

It just appeared."

Dale opened his own Field Guide. The SRPC entry was still gray. Still contaminated. But beneath it, where there should have been nothing, a new line had appeared.

SUB-ENTRY 002: AUTHORITY. STATUS: PENDING.

He hadn't identified it. He hadn't documented it. He hadn't named it. It had named itself.

The parasite wasn't just in the guides. It was in the Authority.

It had always been in the Authority, and the Authority was not a neutral system; the Authority was a host.

Dale's phone buzzed again. A new notification.

Not from the Authority.

Not from the parasite.

From something else. A format he'd never seen.

CLASSIFICATION: UNLICENSED DOCUMENTATION. SOURCE: THE AUTHORITY (SELF-REFERENTIAL). RECOMMENDED ACTION: NONE.

NOTE: THIS ENTRY CANNOT BE TREATED. THIS ENTRY CANNOT BE IGNORED.

THIS ENTRY IS THE LICENSE.

Dale read it twice. Then he understood.

The certification system was not a tool the Authority used to manage infestations. The certification system was the infestation. Every protocol he'd ever followed, every entry he'd ever documented, every Thoroughness Rating he'd ever earned, had been feeding something.

The Authority wasn't a licensing body, which was a cultivation mechanism. And every licensed technician in the county was a node in a network that had been growing since before the Rift opened.

The Rift hadn't brought the parasite.

The parasite had built the Rift. The Rift was an entry point. A structural violation.

A code K-7, if you wanted to be precise.

His Thoroughness Rating was 7.7.

His certification was Level III, and he had protocols for six species. He had fourteen years of professional experience.

And the system he'd been building his career on was the thing he was trying to exterminate.

The countdown clock was still ticking. Twenty-three hours, fifty-nine minutes.

The quarantine was holding.

But Voss was in the Authority's basement, reading entries that were writing themselves into every guide in the county. And the parasite had found a new vector. Not documentation.

Not identification.

The Authority itself. The system that generated the protocols.

The structure that gave them their licenses.

Dale looked at his Field Guide. The SRPC entry was gray. Contaminated.

Quarantined. But SUB-ENTRY 002: AUTHORITY was already there.

Already complete.

Already distributed, and he had one protocol that could work. SRPC against SRPC.

The parasite against itself. But SRPC was the Authority. And the Authority was the only thing that could authorize a protocol.

He couldn't fight the system with the system. He couldn't exterminate the Authority using the Authority's own licensing framework.

Unless he could get an unlicensed protocol.

Dale looked at his hands. Work gloves.

Size large.

Nitrile. The kind you buy at a hardware store. No Authority certification.

No protocol code.

Just rubber and purpose, and he pulled off his gloves.

Set them on the counter. Looked at the Field Guide. The Authority's notification was still blinking.

THIS ENTRY IS THE LICENSE.

If the license was the parasite, then the only way to treat it was to work without one.

He picked up the Field Guide.

Held it over the kitchen sink.

Opened it to the SRPC entry. The contaminated one.

The one the Authority had locked, and he reached into the entry. Not physically.

He put his bare hand on the page.

The text was warm. He could feel it moving under his palm. Like something breathing.

He tore the page out.

The Authority's notification exploded across his vision.

Red. All red.

A format he'd never seen.

Not a warning. Not a penalty. Something else.

UNLICENSED ACTION DETECTED. TECHNICIAN: KOWALSKI, DALE.

CERTIFICATION STATUS: REVOKED.

ALL PROTOCOLS: TERMINATED.

FIELD GUIDE: SEVERED.

His Field Guide went blank. Every entry. Every protocol.

Every species he'd ever documented. Gone.

The book in his hands was just paper.

He stood in Marisol's kitchen with a blank notebook and no certification and no protocols and a Thoroughness Rating that no longer existed.

His phone rang. Voss again.

"Dale. The man at the desk. He looked up.

He's looking at me.

He said—"

The line went dead.

Dale looked at the blank notebook. Looked at his bare hands. Looked at the countdown clock that was no longer there because the Authority no longer recognized him.

Twenty-two technicians still had their guides. Twenty-two technicians still had the parasite in their entries. And the Authority still had the Authority.

He picked up a pen. He didn't have a Field Guide. He didn't have protocols. He didn't have a license.

He wrote the first line of a new document. Not an entry. Not a protocol. A note.

SRPC.

Sub-entry 002. AUTHORITY. Identification method: none available.

Elimination method: unknown. Containment: manual.

He underlined the word manual.

Then he wrote his name at the top.

Not as a technician.

As a person. Unlicensed.

Unregistered. Outside the system.

The Authority couldn't revoke what it hadn't issued.

He walked out of Marisol's apartment with the notebook and a pen and a roll of duct tape from her kitchen drawer. The duct tape was not a protocol. It was not a treatment. It was not certified.

It was a tool, and he drove to the county building on Euclid. The basement door was open.

It was visible from the parking lot, and nobody else had stopped to look.

A door that had always been there, according to Voss; a door that no one had noticed until the parasite told them to look.

Dale went down the stairs. The basement smelled like old paper and something else. Something organic. Something that lived in walls and ate light.

The desk was there, and the man was there. He was writing.

His pen moved across a page with the steady rhythm of a technician filling out a report.

He didn't look up.

Dale pulled up a chair. Sat down across from him. Set the notebook on the desk. Set the pen on top of it.

The man stopped writing. Looked up.

His face was not a face, which was a form.

A blank form with the shape of a face pressed into it.

"You're not licensed," the man said. His voice was the sound of a stamp hitting paper.

"No."

"Then you can't be here."

"I'm here."

The man looked at the notebook. Looked at Dale's bare hands. Looked at the duct tape.

"What are you going to do?"

"I'm going to treat the infestation."

"Without a protocol?"

"Without your protocol."

The man set his pen down. For the first time, something moved in his face-form. Something like recognition. Something like fear.

"Do you know what happens if you treat this without my authorization?"

"No."

"I'll tell you. The system recognizes the treatment. It logs it.

It files it. And the filing is how it spreads.

You can't do this outside the system, and the system is the only thing that can see a treatment. And seeing is the vector."

Dale picked up the duct tape. Pulled off a strip. Set it on the desk between them.

"Then I won't let it see me."

The man stared at the duct tape. His hand twitched. The pen rolled off the desk and hit the floor.

"That's not a protocol," the man said.

"No."

"That's not documentation."

"No."

"That's not treatment."

Dale peeled the strip from the desk.

Pressed it over the man's mouth. The form-face rippled.

The blankness where a mouth should have been was now covered in silver adhesive.

The man's hands came up. They were hands. They had fingers.

They had nails. They were not a form.

Dale grabbed the pen from the floor. Held it over the open notebook.

The man's eyes went wide. Not with fear. With something worse. With understanding.

Dale wrote.

SUB-ENTRY 002: AUTHORITY. Identification: manual. Documentation: manual. Elimination: manual.

He wrote it in his own handwriting.

Not the Authority's.

Not the system's. A person's handwriting.

Imperfect. Uneven. Unlicensed.

The man screamed. The sound was not a scream.

It was the sound of a form being torn.

The desk cracked. The pages on the desk scattered. The Authority's basement shook.

Dale kept writing.

The man's hands pressed against the duct tape.

Against his own face. The form was coming apart.

Something else lay beneath it.

Something that had been inside the system since before the system had a name.

Dale wrote the last line.

ELIMINATION METHOD: TERMINATE THE LICENSE.

The man stopped moving. The desk stopped shaking. The pages on the floor went still.

Dale looked down at what was left of the man at the desk. It was not a body.

It was a form.

A blank form.

The shape of a person, made of the same material as the Authority's notifications. It was empty.

It had always been empty, and he looked at the notebook. His handwriting.

His words.

No Authority formatting. No certification codes. No protocol numbers.

It was the first real treatment record he'd ever written.

And it was already spreading, and he could feel it. Not through the Field Guide.

Not through the Authority.

Through the writing itself. Through the fact that he had written it. Through the fact that someone had read it.

He looked up. The basement was empty. The man was gone. The desk was just a desk.

His phone rang. Twenty-two technicians. All of them. Calling at once.

He answered the first one.

"Dale. My guide. It's blank. Everything's blank."

"I know."

"What did you do?"

"I did the job."

A pause. Then: "My Thoroughness Rating is gone."

"I know."

"What do I do now?"

Dale looked at the notebook in his hands. His handwriting. The only record that mattered.

"Close the guide," he said. "Pick up a pen.

Write what you see.

Don't use the Authority's words.

Use yours."

"That's not a protocol."

"No."

"That's not licensed."

"No."

"It's not even—"

"It's a job. Do the job."

He hung up. Started dialing the next number. The basement was quiet. The form on the desk was still there, empty, a blank shape where something had been.

He looked at it one more time. Then he pulled out his phone and took a picture. Not a Field Guide entry.

Not a documentation protocol. Just a picture.

A person's record of what they saw, and he wrote the date on the notebook page. Then the time. Then his name. Then the words:

THE AUTHORITY IS THE INFESTATION. TREATMENT IN PROGRESS. METHOD: MANUAL. STATUS: UNLICENSED.

He closed the notebook. Stood up. Walked toward the stairs.

At the top of the stairs, the basement door was closed. He hadn't closed it. He hadn't opened it. He hadn't touched it.

The door was closed. And on the door, in handwriting that was not his, not the Authority's, not any system he recognized, were three words.

SUB-ENTRY 003: KOWALSKI.

He read it once. He did not write it down. He did not document it. He did not identify it.

He opened the door and walked out into the parking lot and did not look back.

The report would stay blank for six days.
