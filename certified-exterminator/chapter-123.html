Chapter 123: A Hand Came Out

# Chapter 123

The figure stepped out of the glass.

It didn't walk. It unfolded.

One moment it was a flat, distorted silhouette pressed against the reflective surface, the next it was three-dimensional, solid, and occupying the hallway with the same unsettling weight as the real Dale Kowalski.

The reflection's hazmat suit was pristine. Not a scuff, not a tear, not a single drop of the black ichor that currently coated Dale's own boots. The clipboard in the doppelganger's hand was white.

Stark, blindingly white.

Dale froze. His breath hitched in his throat, a sharp, involuntary gasp that sounded loud in the buzzing silence of the corridor.

He looked at his own hands. The red stain on his fingers had spread.

It wasn't just on his fingertips anymore.

It was creeping up the side of his thumb, staining the skin of his palm. The pen in his grip felt hot. He dropped it.

The plastic clattered against the linoleum, rolling away into the shadows.

The reflection smiled. It was Dale's smile, but the corners of the mouth were too high, the teeth too numerous.

"Identification confirmed," the reflection said.

The voice was perfect.

It was Dale's voice, stripped of the gravel, the exhaustion, the fourteen years of breathing dust and mold. It was the voice of a man who had never missed a deadline.

"Target: Dale Kowalski. Status: Contaminated. Action: Purge."

Behind the reflection, the other eleven figures stepped forward in unison. Their movement was synchronized, a hive-mind efficiency that made Dale's stomach turn. They held their clipboards like shields. They held their pens like daggers.

Dale's mind raced. He tried to access his Field Guide, tried to pull up the entry for *Mirror Paradox Entity*, but the system interface remained locked. The timer in his peripheral vision ticked down. *23:45:12.*

He couldn't fight them. He couldn't talk to them. They were his own image, corrupted by the unauthorized use of knowledge. They were the embodiment of his own fear: the fear that he wasn't thorough enough, that he had missed a spot, that the infestation would return because he had taken a shortcut.

He looked at Halloway. The younger technician was pressed against the far wall, his face pale, his hands shaking so violently he could barely hold his own flashlight.

Halloway wasn't looking at the reflections.

He was looking at Dale. Not with fear of the monsters, but with a dawning horror that Dale understood too well.

Halloway was seeing the red stain on Dale's hand. He was seeing the blank clipboard. He was seeing a man who had lost his license, his protection, his sanity.

The lead reflection took another step. The glass behind it rippled like water disturbed by a stone.

"Dale," Halloway whispered. The sound was tiny, swallowed by the hum of the sphere. "Your report."

Dale didn't answer.

He couldn't. If he spoke, he acknowledged the threat.

If he acknowledged the threat, he validated the mirror.

This was a psychological trap, a test of mental stability disguised as a combat scenario. The Authority didn't care if he killed the reflections. It cared if he believed they were real.

The reflection raised its pen. It wrote a single word in the air, the ink hanging suspended in the hallway.

*INVALID.*

The word pulsed with a sickly green light. The other eleven reflections wrote the same word. Twelve instances of *INVALID* floating in the air, forming a barrier between Dale and the exit.

Dale backed away. His heel caught on the edge of the dropped pen. He stumbled, catching himself on the wall. The red stain on his hand smeared against the drywall, leaving a long, bloody streak.

The reflection noticed. It tilted its head, mimicking Dale's stumble with a jerky, unnatural grace. It pointed at the smudge.

"Contamination breach," it said. "Protocol violation."

Dale looked at the wall. He looked at the red smear.

It looked like blood.

But it wasn't blood.

It was ink. His own ink, twisted by the Rift's radiation.

He thought about the code. Code K-7, structural entry point. He had written it once, years ago, for a duplex in Columbus.

He had cited the same code today, in the room with Gerald. He had used it to justify the breach.

The mirror wasn't showing him demons. It was showing him his own paperwork. It was showing him the errors he had made, the shortcuts he had taken, the corners he had cut.

The lead reflection moved faster now. It lunged.

Dale didn't dodge. He didn't block. He closed his eyes.

He focused on the protocol. *Identify.

Document. Isolate. Eliminate.

Verify.*

He couldn't identify this. He couldn't document it. He couldn't isolate it without breaking the mirror, and breaking the mirror might break the reality of the hallway.

He needed a third variable. Someone else.

"Look at it," Dale said, his voice low. He didn't open his eyes. He spoke to Halloway.

Halloway didn't move. "Dale, run."

"Look at it," Dale repeated. "Don't look at me. Look at the clipboard."

Halloway's eyes flickered. He looked at the reflection. He looked at the white clipboard.

The reflection paused. Its pen hovered over the white page.

It was waiting for Halloway to react.

The mirror drew its power from the observer. If Halloway believed the reflection was Dale, it remained. If Halloway saw it as an anomaly, it might destabilize.

"Is that you?" Halloway asked, his voice trembling. "Is that really you?"

The reflection smiled wider. "I am the perfect technician.

I have no anxiety.

I have no fear.

I am the ideal you aspire to."

Halloway's eyes widened. He looked at the real Dale, slumped against the wall, covered in red filth, his suit torn, his face gaunt. Then he looked at the reflection.

Pristine. Confident.

Wrong.

"That's not him," Halloway said. The words were quiet, but they were decisive. "Dale doesn't smile like that. Dale doesn't write in white."

The reflection's smile faltered. A crack appeared on its cheek, running from the corner of its mouth to its ear. Not a physical crack, but a visual glitch. The image distorted, pixelating for a split second.

Dale opened his eyes. He saw the glitch. He saw the crack.

It wasn't a monster. It was a rendering error.

He stood up. He walked toward the reflection. The other eleven mirrors watched him.

They didn't attack.

They were waiting for Halloway's confirmation.

"You're wrong," Dale said.

He spoke directly to Halloway, not the reflection. "I'm not perfect. I'm sloppy.

I left the jar lid open. I used the wrong concentration of acid on the beetles.

I wrote the report in red ink because I was tired and I grabbed the wrong pen from Gerald's desk."

He held up his stained hand. The red was vivid against the grey drywall.

"This is the real infestation," Dale said. "Not the mirror. The paperwork."

Halloway stared at the red hand.

He stared at the reflection's white clipboard. The cognitive dissonance was visible on Halloway's face.

He was a junior tech. He had been trained to trust the system, to trust the clean lines of the protocol. But he had also been working with Dale for three weeks.

He knew Dale was messy. He knew Dale was anxious.

He knew Dale made mistakes.

The reflection's eyes narrowed. The black pupils swirled.

"Correction," the reflection said. "Protocol is protocol. Thoroughness is the metric. You failed."

"I failed the exam," Dale said. He walked closer. "I failed the practical.

But I'm still here.

And you're not."

He reached out. His hand, stained red, moved toward the reflection's pristine clipboard.

The reflection didn't pull away.

It couldn't. It was bound by the rules of the mirror. It had to maintain its form as long as it was believed to be real.

Halloway was the observer. Halloway had just expressed doubt.

Dale's fingers brushed the edge of the white clipboard.

The surface rippled.

The red stain on Dale's hand transferred.

It didn't smear.

It *absorbed*. The red ink bled into the white paper of the reflection's clipboard.

The white turned pink. Then purple. Then black.

The reflection screamed. It was a silent scream, a vibration that shattered the glass of the nearby light fixtures.

"System error," the reflection shrieked. "Input corruption detected."

The other eleven reflections flickered. Their clipboards stained as well. The red spread like a virus through the network of mirrors.

Dale grabbed the edge of the mirror frame. It was cold. Ice cold.

"Halloway," Dale said. "The Authority rates thoroughness. They don't rate perfection. They rate *completion*."

He ripped the pen from his pocket. The tip was still wet with the red ink.

He didn't write on his own clipboard. He wrote on the mirror.

He slashed a diagonal line through the reflection's face.

The glass didn't break. The image broke.

The reflection's face split in two. The left half slid down. The right half slid up. The body dissolved into a shower of black shards that turned to dust before they hit the floor.

The other eleven mirrors shattered. Not physically, but informationally. The data they contained—the idealized, perfected versions of Dale—was corrupted by the red ink, the stain of imperfection.

The hallway was silent.

The mirror was just a mirror again. It reflected Dale. Halloway. The empty hallway.

No figures. No smiles.

Dale looked at his hand. The red stain was gone. His fingers were clean.

He looked at his clipboard. The page was blank.

He looked at Halloway.

Halloway was staring at the mirror. His face was unreadable.

"You cheated," Halloway said.

Dale picked up his pen from the floor. It was dry. The ink had vanished.

"I documented the error," Dale said. "I isolated the contamination. I eliminated the anomaly."

He looked at his peripheral vision. The timer was gone. The lockout notification had disappeared.

**SYSTEM ALERT:** **ANOMALY RESOLVED.** **THOROUGHNESS RATING: 4.5** **NOTE: UNCONVENTIONAL METHOD. PENALTY APPLIED.**

Dale stared at the numbers. A 4.5. A failing grade in most contexts. But it was a passing grade for survival.

He had lost points. He had lost credibility. But he had kept his license.

"We need to move," Dale said. He tucked the clipboard under his arm. "The sphere is still active. Gerald is still inside."

Halloway didn't move. He was looking at the spot where the reflection had stood. There was a stain on the floor. A small, perfect circle of red ink.

"Dale," Halloway said. "That wasn't a hallucination. That was a copy. The Authority made a copy of you."

Dale felt a chill crawl up his spine. He looked at the stain. He looked at his clean hands.

"The Authority doesn't make copies," Dale said. "It makes audits."

He walked toward the door at the end of the hall. The door to the containment chamber.

"If they made a copy," Halloway said, following him, "where is it? Did we destroy it?"

Dale stopped. He looked back at the mirror.

In the glass, behind his reflection, there was a shadow. A tiny, almost imperceptible movement in the corner of the frame.

A second mirror. Smaller. Hidden in the reflection of the first.

And in that second mirror, a figure was writing.

It wasn't smiling. It wasn't pristine.

It was wearing Dale's hazmat suit. It was holding Dale's clipboard.

And it was writing in red ink.

The figure looked up. It met Dale's eyes through the layers of glass.

It mouthed a single word.

*Next.*

The door to the containment chamber slid open.

Gerald's face was still pressed against the metal sphere, screaming silently.

But the sphere was no longer dark.

It was glowing.

And the light coming from inside wasn't black.

It was red.

Dale raised his pen.

He didn't look at Halloway. He didn't look at Gerald.

He looked at the red glow.

"Initiate Protocol 7," Dale said. "Secondary Containment."

The sphere vibrated. The hum grew louder, shifting pitch from a buzz to a shriek.

The red light pulsed.

And then, the metal of the sphere liquefied.

It didn't melt. It *grew*. Tendrils of silver metal stretched out from the sphere, seeking the walls, the ceiling, the floor. Seeking them.

Halloway screamed. "Dale! It's breaching containment!"

Dale didn't run. He clicked his pen.

The click echoed in the room.

*Click.*

The sphere stopped vibrating.

The tendrils froze.

Dale stared at the frozen metal. He stared at the red glow.

He had not issued the command. He had not spoken the protocol code.

He had just clicked his pen.

The System had responded to the click.

**SYSTEM ALERT:** **COMMAND INPUT: PEN CLICK.** **ACTION: PAUSE.** **STATUS: HELD.**

Dale looked at the pen in his hand. The click was a mechanical sound. A physical input.

The System responded to physical inputs now?

Or was the System inside the pen?

He looked at Halloway. Halloway was staring at the frozen tendrils, his mouth open, his hands empty.

Halloway had no pen.

Halloway had no way to pause the threat.

Halloway was helpless.

Dale was not.

He realized then that the License wasn't just a status. It was a hardware interface.

He had never questioned why the pen was so heavy. Why the clipboard never got wet. Why the ink never dried.

He looked at the red stain on the floor. The stain from the mirror.

It was still there.

It was wet.

And it was moving.

It was crawling toward his boots.

Dale stepped back.

The red stain reached his toe. It didn't stop. It climbed.

It went through the rubber of his boot.

It went through his socks.

It touched his skin.

And then, his left foot went numb.

Dale looked down. His foot was grey. Like stone. Like glass.

The contamination wasn't just in the paper. It was in him.

The mirror hadn't been trying to kill him.

It had been trying to *update* him.

Halloway grabbed Dale's arm. "Move! We have to move!"

Dale couldn't move his left leg. He dragged it. The grey substance spread up his calf.

The sphere was humming again. The pause was temporary. The tendrils twitched.

Dale looked at his clipboard. The page was blank.

He had no report.

He had no record of what had just happened.

Without the record, the event didn't exist.

Without the record, he couldn't claim the points.

Without the points, he couldn't upgrade the protocol to stop the spread.

He had thirty seconds before the grey reached his knee.

He had sixty seconds before the sphere woke up.

He had zero seconds to write.

Dale looked at Halloway.

"Give me your pen," Dale said.

Halloway shook his head. "I don't—"

"Give me your pen!"

Halloway fumbled in his pocket. He pulled out a cheap plastic ballpoint. Blue ink.

Dale snatched it.

He slammed it against his clipboard. He wrote.

Not a report. Not a protocol.

He wrote his name.

*Dale Kowalski.*

Then he wrote his ID number.

Then he wrote the date.

He wrote quickly, sloppily, the blue ink mixing with the residual red on the page.

**SYSTEM ALERT:** **DOCUMENTATION IN PROGRESS.** **THOROUGHNESS: 1.2** **PENALTY: HIGH.**

It wasn't enough. It was barely a start.

The grey reached his knee.

The sphere shrieked.

The tendrils surged forward, aiming for Halloway.

Dale threw himself forward, placing his body between Halloway and the metal.

He caught a tendril on his chest. It pierced his suit. It pierced his skin.

He didn't flinch.

He kept writing.

*Date. Time. Location. Anomaly.*

The System pinged.

**DOCUMENTATION COMPLETE.** **STATUS: VERIFIED.** **REWARD: TEMPORARY IMMUNITY.**

The grey on his leg receded. The pain vanished.

Dale stood up. He was alive.

But the sphere wasn't stopped.

It was waiting.

And now, it had a name.

Dale Kowalski.

The red glow pulsed in a rhythm.

*Da-le. Da-le. Da-le.*

It was saying his name.

Dale looked at the sphere.

He looked at his clipboard. The page was full.

He looked at Halloway.

"We can't leave him in there," Dale said.

Halloway looked at the sphere. He looked at Gerald's face, still pressed against the metal.

"He's gone, Dale."

"No," Dale said. "He's filed."

The sphere cracked.

A hand came out.

It wasn't Gerald's hand.

It was his own.
