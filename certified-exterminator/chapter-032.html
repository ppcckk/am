Chapter 32: Inadequate Service Coverage

# Chapter 32

Type 8: Inadequate Service Area Coverage.

Dale read it twice. Then a third time, slower.

The definition was narrow. It applied when a licensed Authority office failed to maintain active technician coverage within a designated service radius during an ongoing infestation event.

The violation window lasted forty-eight hours, starting when the coverage gap began.

The NEXUS had opened thirty-one hours ago. They were inside the window. Barely.

He could file a Type 8 against the Authority.

The complaint would trigger a mandatory seventy-two-hour hold on any containment or quarantine decisions pending investigation. It wouldn't stop the NEXUS. It wouldn't close the breach. But it would freeze the Authority's plan to abandon the county.

To file, he needed documentation.

Not just a sighting. A full specimen record.

Species identification, morphological notes, behavioral observations, and a treatment recommendation.

The kind of thing a Level VII technician would produce. He was Class II. The System would flag his documentation as insufficient for a Type 8 filing.

Unless he did it by hand, and he crouched beside the creature's body; the ceramic plates were splitting along the dorsal ridge, revealing the pale tissue beneath.

Cold air made the edges curl like birch bark.

He pulled on his nitrile gloves.

The ones from his kit. Not the Authority-issue ones.

The Authority's gloves were rated for Class I and II specimens. These were standard chemical-resistant nitrile, 8 mil thickness, powder-free. He'd used them for fourteen years on cockroach nests, rodent droppings, and termite galleries.

They didn't care what crawled inside them, and he opened his Field Guide.

The entry for the unregistered species sat there with its blank weakness field, and the System had populated the basic taxonomy: arthropod-vertebrate hybrid, exoskeletal plating, acid-secreting mandibles. Generic.

Useless, and he pulled a pen from his shirt pocket. Not the System's digital annotation.

A real pen.

Ballpoint. Black ink. He'd been carrying it since before the Rift.

He started with the mandibles.

The creature's head was roughly triangular, flattened laterally. The mandibles weren't pincer-shaped like a beetle's.

They were recurved, hollow, and the acid glands sat at the base of each one.

He noted the gland size relative to the head capsule.

In a German cockroach, the equivalent glands were vestigial. In a bombardier beetle, they were oversized and pressurized.

This thing's glands were intermediate. Not pressurized. Drip-fed.

That meant it didn't spray. It smeared.

Close-range contamination, and he wrote that down. The System's Field Guide interface flickered. A small prompt appeared: MANUAL ANNOTATION DETECTED. Would you like to accept?

He tapped yes.

The weakness field stayed blank. No damage bonus. But the text was there.

His words, in his handwriting, scanned into the guide.

The System couldn't reject accurate observation. That was the loophole.

The System rewarded thoroughness.

It didn't care about certification level for documentation. Only for treatment.

He moved to the thorax. Cut the plating along the lateral seam with his utility knife.

The tissue underneath was layered.

Muscle, then a thin membrane, then something that looked like a secondary exoskeleton nested inside the first.

He'd seen this pattern before. Not in monsters.

In his own line of work.

A termite's gut. The worker caste had a nested chamber system for protozoa. The outer body was the shell. The inner structures were the colony.

He pressed his knife deeper, and the secondary exoskeleton had a second set of organs inside it. A smaller set.

Same layout.

Same acid glands, just scaled down. Like a creature built inside a creature.

He cut further, and the third layer was smaller still. And inside that, the beginnings of a fourth.

Dale stopped cutting.

He sat back on his heels. Nitrile gloves slick with pale fluid. The knife hung loose in his grip.

"Voss."

She was twenty feet away, scanning the tree line with a rifle she'd scavenged from a dead National Guardsman. She turned.

"Come look at this."

She walked over. Looked down. Her face didn't change much. It just got harder to read.

"What is it?"

"It's not one animal." Dale pointed at the nested layers.

"It's a colony organism. Each layer is a different caste.

The outer one is a worker.

The inner ones are reproductive. The whole thing is a single organism structured like a termite mound."

"Can you be sure?"

"I've done termite inspections for six years. I've cut open more termite workers than I can count.

This is the same architecture.

Just bigger.

And more layers."

He looked at the body again. The cold was making the tissue contract. The layers were tightening around each other like fists.

"The NEXUS isn't a hole," he said. "It's a body. Everything coming through it is a cell.

A worker. A reproductive unit.

They're not invading.

They're growing."

Voss was quiet for a long time.

"Can you file your complaint with that?"

"The documentation is good.

The morphology is clear. I can write the treatment recommendation myself." He pulled out his phone.

Opened the Authority's filing portal.

Type 8. Service area violation. He started typing.

The portal rejected him.

ERROR: DOCUMENTATION INSUFFICIENT FOR TYPE 8 FILING. REQUIRED: CLASS VII OBSERVATION CERTIFICATION.

He tried again. Same result.

"Remote filing doesn't count," he said. "They'll say I'm not qualified to make the observation."

"So what?"

"I have to file in person. At the Authority office. They can't reject a physical filing if the documentation is on paper and the specimen is present."

"The specimen is a forty-pound hybrid carcass on the side of Route 9."

"I can put it in the truck."

Voss looked at the body. Looked at his truck. A 2019 Ford F-150 with a pest control company logo on the door and a bed full of sprayer tanks.

"You want to put that in your truck."

"I want to put it in a containment bag." He reached into his kit. Pulled out a 55-gallon heavy-duty polyethylene liner.

The kind he used for rodent carcass disposal.

Rated for biological waste.

"I'll bag it. I'll label it.

I'll drive it to the office. They can't reject a physical filing."

He worked fast. This was the part he was good at.

Not the fighting.

Not the System. The physical process. Bag, seal, label, log.

He'd done it a hundred times on dead rats in walls, on dead raccoons in attics, on the occasional dead cat behind a dumpster.

The creature was bigger. The smell was worse.

But the process was the same, and he wrapped the body in the liner, double-bagged it, and taped the seams with duct tape. He wrote on the outside in permanent marker: SPECIMEN 1. UNREGISTERED SPECIES.

COLONY ARCHITECTURE. DATE, TIME, COORDINATES.

His name.

His certification number, and he dragged it to the truck bed.

Voss watched him do it without saying anything. When he was done, she got in the passenger side.

"Four hours," she said.

"Maybe three."

"They'll be looking for you."

"Then we take back roads."

He started the truck. The bed groaned under the weight.

The drive was mostly silence. Dale kept his eyes on the road.

Voss kept hers on the rifle. The radio picked up fragments. A news station, mostly static.

A voice saying "permanent" "zone" and "do not re-enter." Then static again.

They passed a checkpoint on County Road 14, and the barricade was unmanned. Sandbags and a folding table with a clipboard. The clipboard had a form on it.

Dale recognized the format.

Infestation event log. Someone had started filling it out and stopped halfway.

The pen was still on the table, and he pulled over. Got out. Read the form.

It was a Type 5 report. Neglected infestation. Someone had flagged a residential cluster three miles east.

The report was incomplete. The technician who started it had a certification number.

Class I.

The report stopped mid-sentence.

Dale pulled out his pen. Finished the form.

Added his own certification number. Signed it. Tucked it into the clipboard.

"Come on," Voss said.

He got back in the truck, and the Authority office was a squat concrete building behind a strip mall in the next county. Dale had driven past it twice before the Rift.

It looked like a DMV satellite office.

Same beige walls. Same fluorescent lighting visible through the windows.

They parked. The parking lot was empty.

The front door was locked. Dale knocked. Waited. Knocked again.

A voice from inside. Muffled. "Closed."

"Type 8 filing," Dale said. "Physical submission. I have documentation and a specimen."

Silence.

Then the sound of a lock turning. The door opened.

A woman in a gray Authority windbreaker stood in the doorway.

She looked tired. She looked like she hadn't slept in two days.

"You have forty-eight hours to file," she said. "Check the timestamp."

"Thirty-one hours. I'm inside the window."

She looked at him. Looked at his certification badge clipped to his belt.

Class II.

Her expression shifted.

Not dismissal. Something closer to pity.

"You know what a Type 8 does when it's filed against the Authority itself?"

"Seventy-two-hour hold."

"That's the procedural answer. The practical answer is that it triggers a mandatory reassignment protocol. The Authority has to designate a qualified technician to handle the infestation within the hold period. If no technician exists in the region, the protocol escalates to the nearest available certified operator."

Dale said nothing.

"You understand what that means."

"I understand that I'm filing it."

She stepped aside. Let him in.

The office was empty except for a desk, a filing cabinet, and a computer terminal with a green screen. The woman sat down. Pulled a form from a drawer. Slid it across the desk.

"Fill it out."

Dale sat. Wrote his name. His certification number.

The violation type. The service area.

The infestation event ID, and he attached the specimen log; he wrote the treatment recommendation by hand, on a separate sheet, in his own handwriting.

COLONY ARCHITECTURE CONFIRMED.

SINGLE ORGANISM, MULTI-CASTE. TREATMENT REQUIRES NEST-LEVEL ELIMINATION, NOT INDIVIDUAL SPECIMEN KILL.

CURRENT AUTHORITY PROTOCOL (QUARANTINE) INADEQUATE.

RECOMMEND: TARGETED NEST TREATMENT WITHIN HOLD PERIOD.

He signed it. Slid it back.

The woman typed his filing into the terminal. The green screen blinked.

FILING ACCEPTED. TYPE 8: INADEQUATE SERVICE AREA COVERAGE.

Dale exhaled.

SEVENTY-TWO-HOUR HOLD INITIATED.

He felt something loosen in his chest. Seventy-two hours. Not forty. Seventy-two.

MANDATORY REASSIGNMENT PROTOCOL TRIGGERED.

The screen blinked again.

SEARCHING REGIONAL TECHNICIAN DATABASE...

NO LEVEL VII TECHNICIAN FOUND IN SERVICE AREA.

ESCALATING TO NEAREST CERTIFIED OPERATOR...

Dale watched the cursor blink.

NEAREST QUALIFIED OPERATOR: KOWALSKI, DALE R. CERTIFICATION: LEVEL II.

THOROUGHNESS RATING: 9.2.

ASSIGNMENT: CLASS VII INFESTATION EVENT (NEXUS).

STATUS: BINDING.

The woman looked at him. Her face was blank.

"Congratulations," she said. "You just assigned yourself."

Dale stared at the screen. The cursor blinked at him.

BINDING.

Not optional. Not recommended. Binding.

The System had decided that he was the nearest qualified operator. The only qualified operator.

The only person in the county with a Thoroughness Rating high enough to qualify.

He was Class II. The infestation was Class VII.

The hold was seventy-two hours. The assignment was permanent. If he failed to treat the NEXUS within the hold period, the violation would be logged against him.

Not the Authority.

Him.

Voss was in the doorway. She'd read the screen.

"Can you appeal?" she said.

The woman shook her head. "Reassignment protocol is binding on acceptance. He accepted by filing."

Dale looked at the form. His signature at the bottom. His handwriting. His certification number.

He'd done it by hand. The System had recognized it. And the System had decided he was the only one who could do the job.

The screen added one more line.

NEXT APPOINTMENT: WRITTEN EXAMINATION, CLASS VII. SCHEDULED: 14 HOURS. LOCATION: THIS OFFICE.

Fourteen hours. He had fourteen hours to pass a Class VII written exam or the hold would collapse. And even if he passed, he'd be a Level VII technician with no practical protocols, no field-tested abilities, and a dimensional organism growing under a suburban county.

He stood up.

"Where do I study?" he said.

The woman opened the filing cabinet.

Pulled out a binder. Thick. Black cover.

She set it on the desk.

"Class VII reference material," she said. "Everything you need to know about colony-architecture organisms, nest-level treatment protocols, and dimensional biology."

Dale opened the binder, and the first page was blank.

The second page was blank.

Every page was blank.

He looked at the woman.

"The exam isn't written," she said.

"It's a field examination.

You go in. You identify.

You document. You eliminate. You verify.

The exam is the job."

She closed the binder.

"Fourteen hours, Kowalski."
