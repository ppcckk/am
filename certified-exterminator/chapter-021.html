Chapter 21: Aerial Contamination

# Chapter 21

The grain was falling faster now. Not snow.

Not rain.

It hit Dale's clipboard in a fine spray, each particle no larger than a pinhead, and each one was moving. Not drifting with the wind. Moving against it.

Against gravity.

Against everything.

Voss backed up three steps. His hand went to the baton on his belt, the one that pulsed with a low amber light. Class III protocol. Sub-surface containment. Wrong tool for this.

"Don't deploy that," Dale said.

"I have to do something."

"You have a sub-surface protocol.

This is aerial. You'll get the cross-contamination penalty and lose your Field Guide entry for twenty-four hours. That's not doing something.

That's doing nothing slower."

Voss lowered his hand.

His face was doing the thing faces do when they're trying to process a threat they can't name.

Dale had seen it a dozen times in fourteen years. Homeowners finding a wasp nest for the first time.

The same look. The brain running through its catalog of known things and finding nothing that fits.

Dale didn't have that problem. His catalog was larger. And he had a second catalog. The one that listed everything a thing wasn't.

He looked at the grain. Pinhead-sized.

Moving independently.

Not falling. Not drifting. Each particle oriented itself, rotating, testing the air.

He'd seen this before.

Not in a monster. In a report.

A 2019 extension bulletin from the Ohio State Extension office, describing the dispersal behavior of certain aphid species during population pressure events.

The aphids don't fly. They don't have wings. But they climb to the highest point available, extend their bodies, and let the air currents carry them.

They orient themselves using static charge in the atmosphere. They move against gravity because they're not falling.

They're being lifted.

These weren't being lifted by the atmosphere.

But they were orienting.

Dale pulled up his Field Guide. Forty-seven entries. He started crossing things off.

Not an insect.

Insects have six legs, three body segments, and a chitinous exoskeleton that would be visible at this magnification even in grain form. He could see the particles rotating.

No legs. No segmentation.

Not a nematode. Nematodes are cylindrical and move by undulation. These were rotating.

Not a fungal spore. Fungal spores don't self-orient. They don't have any mechanism for it. They're passive.

Not a bacterium. Too large. Pinhead-sized is roughly 0.5 millimeters. Bacteria are measured in microns.

Not a mite. Mites have eight legs and a fused body. Visible under magnification.

Not a protozoan. Too large, and protozoans don't have structural rigidity. These were holding shape.

He kept going. Crossed off arachnids, crustaceans, annelids. Crossed off every entry in his Field Guide that had a taxonomy he could rule out based on size, movement pattern, and visible morphology.

Forty-three entries remained. Then thirty. Then eleven.

His Authority portal flickered. A new notification.

FIELD GUIDE DIFFERENTIAL IDENTIFICATION IN PROGRESS. ELIMINATION COUNT: 36. REMAINING CANDIDATES: 11.

Dale stared at it. He hadn't activated this.

He hadn't even known it existed, and the Field Guide had been tracking his elimination process. Every species he ruled out was being logged. Every species he ruled out was narrowing the search space.

He kept going.

The particles were rotating faster now.

The vibration in his molars was climbing, and he could feel it in his sinuses, in the backs of his eyes.

The grain was thick enough to see individual particles with the naked eye.

They were all oriented the same direction. Up. Toward the sky.

Not toward the sky. Away from the ground. Away from the source.

Dispersal. Not attack.

Not hunting.

Not feeding.

Dispersal. These were leaving something.

They were being shed, and he crossed off the remaining candidates that were predatory. Crossed off the ones that were parasitic. Crossed off anything that would attack a human host directly.

Six remaining.

The Authority notification updated.

FIELD GUIDE DIFFERENTIAL IDENTIFICATION: 6 REMAINING CANDIDATES. PROVISIONAL ENTRY AVAILABLE.

A provisional entry. He'd never seen this before. He'd never seen it mentioned in any of the Authority's training materials. He pulled it up.

PROVISIONAL ENTRY: Species identification incomplete. Confidence threshold not met.

You may deploy any protocol corresponding to the remaining candidate set.

Penalty: If the actual species does not match the deployed protocol, cross-contamination penalty applies. If it does match, full Treatment Points awarded. Provisional entries expire in 90 minutes.

Ninety minutes, and he had ninety minutes to figure out which of six species this was, or he'd be guessing. And guessing meant the cross-contamination penalty.

Which meant his Field Guide entries would go dark for twenty-four hours.

Which meant he couldn't identify anything. Which meant every monster in his service area became unidentifiable. Which meant he couldn't earn points.

Which meant his certification stalled, and he looked at the six candidates.

Entry #12: Chitin mite (Acarus laminaris).

Sub-surface.

Social. Forms colonies in wall voids.

He'd treated this one three weeks ago. Got a 6.9 Thoroughness Rating.

Entry #19: Threadworm (Filum noctis). Sub-surface. Solitary. Nests in drain lines.

Entry #27: Ash beetle (Cineris punctatus). Surface. Solitary. Feeds on dead wood.

Entry #31: Silt crawler (Sedimentum gradus). Sub-surface. Colonial. Found in foundation cracks.

Entry #38: Void tick (Vacuum aranea). Surface. Parasitic. Attaches to hosts.

Entry #44: Dust wasp (Pulveris vespa). Aerial. Colonial. Nests in insulation.

Aerial. Colonial.

Only one match.

Entry #44. Dust wasp.

But dust wasps had wings, and he could see the particles. No wings.

No legs.

No visible anatomy at all.

He eliminated it.

Five remaining, and the grain was thick enough now that he could feel it on his skin. Each particle was warm.

Not hot.

Warm.

Like the heat you feel from a living thing pressed against your forearm.

He eliminated the solitary species. The particles were too numerous to be solitary organisms. Millions were present.

Three remaining.

Chitin mite. Silt crawler. Void tick.

All colonial or parasitic. All sub-surface or surface. None aerial. None of them had a mechanism for flight or dispersal into the atmosphere.

But the particles were in the atmosphere, and he looked up. The grain was coming from above. From the wrong sky.

The thing he'd identified as "wrong" wasn't a sky at all. It was a mass.

A dense aggregation of particles, so thick it blocked the light.

Not clouds. Not a storm. A colony.

A colony in the air, and he eliminated the three remaining candidates. All of them were ground-dwelling. None of them formed aerial colonies. None of them had a dispersal mechanism that would produce particles this size.

Zero remaining.

The Authority notification updated.

FIELD GUIDE DIFFERENTIAL IDENTIFICATION: 0 REMAINING CANDIDATES. SPECIES NOT PRESENT IN FIELD GUIDE. PROVISIONAL ENTRY CANNOT BE CREATED.

He had eliminated everything. The species wasn't in his guide. It wasn't anything he'd seen before. It wasn't anything the Authority had documented before.

And it was getting closer.

Voss was shouting something. Dale couldn't hear him over the vibration. The grain was in his mouth, his eyes, his nostrils. Each particle was warm and moving and oriented upward.

He closed his eyes.

Opened them. Looked at his clipboard.

The word "Unlicensed" was still there.

He'd written it without thinking.

Then he looked at the particles on the clipboard. They were on the surface.

Moving.

But they weren't moving up, and they were moving toward the word.

Toward the ink.

He wiped them off. Looked at the clipboard. The ink was still there.

He wiped again. The particles were gone but the ink was unchanged.

He tried something else, and he wrote a new word. "Treated." The particles on the clipboard moved toward it. He crossed it out.

They stopped.

He wrote "Treated" again. They moved toward it. He crossed it out. They stopped.

He wrote "Treated" and left it. The particles on the clipboard clustered around the word.

All of them.

Every single one. Orienting toward it like it was a light source.

He looked up. The mass in the sky. He raised his clipboard above his head.

The particles in the air shifted. Not all of them.

Not enough to matter.

But he saw it.

A subtle reorientation. A drift toward the clipboard.

He pulled out a marker. The thick kind he used for violation codes. He wrote on the clipboard in letters two inches tall.

TREATED.

Then below it, smaller:

INFESTATION UNDER CONTROL. DO NOT DISPERSAL.

He held it up. The mass above the subdivision shifted. Not much.

Not enough to clear the sky. But enough.

The vibration dropped a half-step.

The grain thinned.

Voss grabbed his arm. "What the hell did you do?"

"I wrote a compliance notice."

"That's not how that works."

"It's how that works for anything with a dispersal response.

You don't kill a colony. You change its signal.

You tell it the territory is occupied.

You tell it the resources are depleted. You tell it there's no reason to be here." He looked at the clipboard. The marker was still wet.

"I just don't know if it's going to read it."

The mass above them held.

The vibration stayed at its new pitch.

The grain thinned further. The particles then started rising slowly.

Away from the subdivision. Away from the ground. Rising back into the wrong sky.

Voss let out a breath that sounded like a tire losing air.

Dale didn't celebrate. He was looking at his Field Guide.

The notification was still there.

SPECIES NOT PRESENT IN FIELD GUIDE. He'd identified what it wasn't. He'd identified how it responded.

He'd identified its behavioral trigger.

But he hadn't identified the species.

And then the Authority sent a second message.

TREATMENT ACTION LOGGED. METHOD: BEHAVIORAL DETERRENCE. NOT ELIMINATION. TREATMENT POINTS: 0.

He knew that.

He'd known that. Killing wasn't the point. Eliminating the nest was the point.

And he hadn't eliminated anything. He'd just told the colony to go somewhere else.

Which meant it was still out there.

Still breeding.

Still growing. Still coming back.

He looked at the notification again. And below it, a second line he hadn't seen before.

NOTE: BEHAVIORAL DETERRENCE IS A VALID TREATMENT METHOD FOR COLONIAL SPECIES WITH KNOWN DISPERSAL TRIGGERS. THIS METHOD HAS NOT BEEN PREVIOUSLY LOGGED BY ANY LICENSED TECHNICIAN IN THE CURRENT SERVICE AREA.

Dale stared at it.

NEW PROTOCOL UNLOCKED: BEHAVIORAL DETERRENCE (PROVISIONAL). THIS PROTOCOL IS NOT CERTIFIED. USE OF THIS PROTOCOL IN COMBAT SITUATIONS WILL BE FLAGGED FOR REVIEW.

He hadn't earned Treatment Points. He hadn't earned a Field Guide entry. But he'd unlocked something.

A protocol that didn't exist before. One that the Authority had never seen anyone use.

And then a third message.

CERTIFICATION REVIEW: PENDING. YOUR USE OF UNCERTIFIED PROTOCOLS TRIGGERS A THOROUGHNESS AUDIT OF ALL PREVIOUSLY TREATED INFESTATIONS IN YOUR SERVICE AREA. AUDIT WINDOW: 72 HOURS.

Dale read it twice. Then he looked at his clipboard. At the forty-three sub-surface notifications still glowing on his screen. At the word "Unlicensed" in his own handwriting.

He thought about the broodmother in the crawlspace.

The 9.2 rating. The thirty-one entries he'd closed. The protocols he'd deployed.

The nests he'd treated.

If the Authority was auditing all of them, they were looking for gaps. Any gap.

Any missed egg.

Any untreated entry point. Any frass trail he'd followed but not fully mapped.

He thought about entry #12. Chitin mite.

A 9.4 rating.

Three weeks ago.

He'd treated the wall void, sealed the entry points, verified the colony was dead. Standard protocol.

He'd verified it.

Had he?

He pulled up the entry. Scrolled through his own notes. The verification step.

He'd checked the wall void with his flashlight. He'd checked for live specimens.

He'd checked for frass.

He'd checked for egg cases.

He'd checked for egg cases.

He'd checked for egg cases of Acarus laminaris. Chitin mites lay flat, translucent eggs in clusters of forty to sixty. He'd looked for those. He'd found none.

But the particles in the sky were pinhead-sized. Warm. Moving. Orienting.

He looked at his notes again. The verification entry. "No egg cases present."

He hadn't written what kind of egg cases. He hadn't specified the species. He'd just written "egg cases" because that's what the protocol said. Check for egg cases.

And the Authority was going to audit this. They were going to look at his notes. And they were going to see that he'd checked for the wrong kind of eggs. Because if the species in the sky was a colonial organism with a dispersal trigger, and if it was related to something he'd treated before, and if the thing he'd treated had laid eggs that didn't match the species profile he'd been looking for...

He had seventy-two hours to find the gap in his own work.

Before the Authority found it for him. And if they found it, his Thoroughness Rating would drop.

And if it dropped below the threshold for his current certification level, he'd be demoted.

Back to Class I. Back to rats and cockroaches. Back to a world where the sky wasn't wrong and the air wasn't full of things that moved against gravity.

He looked at Voss. Voss was watching the sky clear. He was relieved. He thought the problem was over.

Dale pulled out his phone. Opened his job log.

Thirty-one closed entries.

Thirty-one Thoroughness Ratings, and he started reading his own verification notes. Looking for the word "egg." Looking for what he'd missed.

The sky was clearing. The vibration was fading. The subdivision was safe.

And Dale Kowalski was looking for the mistake he'd made three weeks ago, and the one that was probably still alive in someone's wall.

He went back to look.
