Chapter 71: Seventy-Two Hours

# Chapter 71

The door didn't swing open. It was removed from its hinges, and a hand reached through the gap, grabbed the doorframe, and pulled the entire panel free. It hit the floor with a sound like a car door being thrown off a truck.

Three people stepped through. They wore the same grey coveralls Dale had seen on the Authority's dispatch notifications, but the fabric was wrong.

Too clean.

Too new. No stains, no creases, no evidence of anyone having actually crawled through a crawlspace in them.

The lead operative carried a device that looked like a leaf blower welded to a cattle prod. The second carried nothing. The third was Dale's age, maybe a year older, with a clipboard.

Dale looked at the clipboard. He looked at the third operative.

"You brought a clipboard," Dale said.

"Standard kit." The operative flipped it open.

Empty pages. No inspection form.

No entry code.

No certification number at the top.

"That's not standard."

The lead operative raised the leaf blower. "Target confirmed. Class IX. Initiating elimination."

The man against the wall scrambled backward. His hands found the floor and he dragged himself away from the operative's boots. His mouth was moving but no sound came out.

Dale stepped between them.

"Protocol requires isolation before elimination," Dale said. "You haven't isolated."

The lead operative looked at Dale. Looked at the clipboard. Looked back at Dale.

"Step aside, technician."

"What's your certification level?"

The operative's jaw tightened. "That's not your concern."

"It is. If you're Class III or below, you're not authorized to handle Class IX targets.

You'd be deploying a protocol designed for lower-tier infestations against a higher-tier subject.

That's cross-contamination.

Forty percent debuff. And it contaminates the Field Guide entry for twenty-four hours." Dale pointed at the man on the floor.

"You'll lose your ability to re-identify him. You'll lose the ability to eliminate him properly. You'll create a contamination event that I'll have to clean up."

The third operative with the empty clipboard said something. "He's right. We haven't run the isolation sweep."

"Nobody asked you." The lead operative shifted the device's nozzle toward Dale. "Move or I move you."

Dale didn't move. He pulled out his own clipboard.

Flipped to a fresh page.

Wrote: UNAUTHORIZED EXTERMINATION ATTEMPT. NO ISOLATION PROTOCOL. NO DOCUMENTATION.

THREAT TO CERTIFICATION INTEGRITY.

He held it up. "I'm documenting this.

If you proceed, I'm filing a protocol violation against your team.

The Authority will flag it. Your Thoroughness Rating will drop. You'll be demoted to Class II within a week."

The lead operative stared at him. The device hummed. The air smelled like ozone and something chemical, like the inside of a new car left in the sun too long.

"You're a technician," the operative said. "You spray for bugs."

"I spray for everything that crawls. You don't.

That's the difference." Dale wrote another line.

TIME OF VIOLATION.

CERTIFICATION NUMBER OF REPORTING TECHNICIAN. "You want to kill something?

Fine. But you follow the protocol or you don't kill it. That's how the system works.

You know this. It's on the card."

The operative's eyes flicked to the notification still glowing on the wall. EXTERMINATION TEAM DISPATCHED. TARGET: CLASS IX SUBJECT IN CONTAINMENT ZONE.

"He's a person," Dale said.

"He's a Class IX entry."

"He's a person who got a Class IX entry because I identified him as humanoid. That's a classification, not a death sentence. You know the difference."

The third operative stepped forward. "What do you want us to do?"

"Hold the target in place. Don't touch him. I'm running a re-inspection."

"On a Class IX?"

"Protocol allows re-inspection of any entry marked PENDING CLASSIFICATION.

His entry says PENDING. That means the classification isn't final. You can't eliminate a pending entry.

That's a violation on its own."

The lead operative's hand was shaking. Not from fear. From the effort of not pulling the trigger.

"Six hours," the operative said. "The Authority gives you six hours to resolve the classification or the order reactivates. After that, it doesn't matter what your clipboard says."

Dale looked at his own Field Guide. The entry glowed: IDENTIFIED: PENDING CLASSIFICATION. Below it, a timer. 05:59:47. Counting down.

"Then I have six hours," Dale said.

He turned to the man on the floor. The man was crying. Not the silent kind. The ugly kind, with snot and shaking shoulders and the kind of sound that makes you want to leave the room.

Dale crouched. Opened his clipboard to a fresh page.

"I need to inspect you," Dale said. "Full protocol. Measurements, morphology, behavioral assessment, entry point analysis. How did you get here?"

The man shook his head.

"How did you get here?" Dale repeated. "I need to document the vector.

If I can show the Authority you entered through a standard Rift breach and not a targeted dimensional insertion, your classification drops to Class IV at most.

Class IV doesn't get exterminated. Class IV gets treated."

"I don't know."

"You don't know, or you won't say?"

"I don't know." The man wiped his face with the back of his hand. "I was at work.

I was at my desk.

The floor opened.

I fell. I don't remember anything else until I woke up in that room."

Dale wrote it down. Standard Rift displacement. No targeted insertion signature.

No dimensional anchoring. No purposeful crossing.

He pulled out his measuring tape.

"I'm going to take some measurements. Hold still."

The man held still. Dale measured his height, his wingspan, his cranial circumference. He checked his pupils. He checked his skin for dimensional scarring, the telltale silver threading that marked creatures who'd crossed the Rift on their own volition.

Nothing. Clean skin. Normal vitals. The man was scared, but his heart rate was within normal parameters for acute fear response.

Dale checked his teeth.

Human dentition. He checked his hands. Five fingers, no vestigial structures.

He checked his ears.

Normal pinnae, and he checked his shadow.

That was the one that mattered.

Creatures from the Rift cast shadows that didn't match their body shape, and they cast too many, or too few, or the wrong shape. The shadow was the tell.

You couldn't fake it. It was a dimensional signature, like frass is for a nest.

Dale angled his flashlight. The shadow fell on the wall behind the man.

One shadow. Correct shape. Correct proportions.

Human.

Dale wrote: SHADOW ANALYSIS: CONSISTENT WITH HUMAN BASELINE. NO DIMENSIONAL ANCHORING DETECTED. NO VOLUNTARY CROSSING EVIDENCE.

He added: ENTRY POINT: STANDARD RIFT DISPLACEMENT. NO TARGETED INSERTION.

He added: BEHAVIORAL ASSESSMENT: FEAR RESPONSE WITHIN NORMAL PARAMETERS. NO AGGRESSION. NO TERRITORIAL BEHAVIOR. NO HUNTING PATTERN.

He looked at the timer. 05:41:12, and he looked at the entry.

He needed to change it from PENDING CLASSIFICATION to something the Authority would accept.

But what? He couldn't classify the man as non-target.

The system didn't have a category for "human who fell through a Rift." It had classifications for infestations. Everything else was unclassified, which meant unhandled, which meant the entry stayed in the system as a threat until someone resolved it.

Dale flipped through his Field Guide. Page after page of entries.

Dimensional Vermin.

Acid Beetles. Broodmother variants. Class I through Class VII.

All of them infestations.

All of them things that needed to be eliminated, and he stopped at a page he'd never looked at before. Class IX. The page was blank except for a header: CLASS IX: EXISTENTIAL THREATS. And below it, in smaller text that Dale had to squint to read:

NOTE: Class IX classification applies to any entry that cannot be resolved through standard elimination protocol. Resolution requires Authority adjudication. Technician may file reclassification petition with supporting documentation. Petition window: 6 hours from initial classification.

Dale read it twice. Then he looked at the third operative.

"Who files the petition?"

"The technician who made the classification."

"Me."

"Me," the operative confirmed.

Dale looked at his clipboard. At the timer. At the man on the floor who was a person and was going to die in five hours and forty minutes unless Dale could prove he wasn't a monster.

But the petition required supporting documentation. And the documentation Dale had was good. It was thorough. It was protocol-compliant.

It wasn't enough.

Because the system didn't care about shadow analysis or behavioral assessment.

The system cared about one thing: was the entry an infestation? And the entry said Class IX. Class IX meant the entry couldn't be resolved through standard elimination.

Which meant it wasn't an infestation. Which meant it was something else.

Something the system didn't have a category for.

Dale pulled out his pen. He turned to a fresh page. He started writing.

PETITION FOR RECLASSIFICATION. SUBJECT: [MAN'S NAME]. CURRENT CLASSIFICATION: CLASS IX. REQUESTED CLASSIFICATION: [BLANK].

He stared at the blank.

What category did you put a human in?

The system had no category for humans. Humans weren't infestations.

They weren't vermin. They weren't threats. They were the people the system was supposed to protect from infestations.

Unless the system didn't know that. Unless the system had never encountered a human on the other side of a Rift before. Unless the Authority, which predated human civilization, had no framework for "human" because humans hadn't existed when it was built.

Dale looked at the timer. 05:38:03, and he looked at the man. The man was watching him with an expression that had stopped being fear and started being something worse. Something that looked like understanding.

"You're going to die in here," the man said. "You can't classify me. There's no category."

"There's always a category," Dale said. "Everything fits somewhere. That's the whole point of the system."

"Then classify me."

Dale looked at his Field Guide. He looked at the blank page for Class IX.

He looked at the other entries.

German cockroach. Brown-banded cockroach. Rattus profundus.

Acid beetle.

Broodmother.

All infestations. All things that entered a structure and needed to be removed.

The man had entered a structure. He needed to be removed.

Dale wrote: REQUESTED CLASSIFICATION: STRUCTURAL INTRUSION, NON-REPRODUCTIVE, and he added: ENTRY VECTOR: ACCIDENTAL.

NO COLONIZATION INTENT. NO REPRODUCTIVE CAPACITY. NO NESTING BEHAVIOR.

NO RESOURCE EXPLOITATION.

He added: RECOMMENDED TREATMENT: RELOCATION. NOT ELIMINATION.

The page glowed.

The timer stopped.

The notification on the wall changed. PETITION FILED. PENDING ADJUDICATION.

The lead operative lowered the device. The third operative exhaled. The man on the floor put his head in his hands.

Dale capped his pen. He looked at the timer. It wasn't gone. It was replaced with a new one.

ADJUDICATION WINDOW: 72 HOURS.

Three days. If the Authority didn't approve the reclassification in three days, the original order reactivated; this time, there was no petition. No second chance.

Dale looked at the operative. "What happens if they reject it?"

"Then we come back."

"And if I file another petition?"

The operative's face did something complicated. "You can't. One petition per entry. That's the rule."

Dale looked at the man. The man looked at Dale. Neither of them said anything.

Dale turned to his clipboard. He needed to prepare for the adjudication. He needed to build a case. He needed to convince an entity that predated human civilization that a human being was not an infestation.

He started writing.

Then his Field Guide pulsed. A new notification. Not from the Authority. From his own guide.

ENTRY UPDATED: STRUCTURAL INTRUSION, NON-REPRODUCTIVE.

Below it, a second line:

NEW ENTRY DETECTED IN SERVICE AREA. 47 SIGNATURES. ALL MATCH STRUCTURAL INTRUSION, NON-REPRODUCTIVE.

Dale looked up.

Forty-seven people.

All of them classified as structural intrusions.

All of them now in the system. All of them with no petition filed.

All of them facing the same timer.

And Dale was the only technician in the county with a Thoroughness Rating high enough to file petitions.

Forty-seven petitions. Three days each. He had seventy-two hours.

He looked at the man. The man looked at him.

"You're not going to be able to do all of them," the man said.

Dale looked at his clipboard. At the pen. At the blank pages.

"No," Dale said. "I'm not."

He started writing the second petition anyway.
