Chapter 320: Check Your Mirrors

# Chapter 320

The grease pencil snapped.

It wasn't a graceful break. The plastic housing crumbled under the pressure of Dale's thumb, splintering into three jagged pieces against his palm. The graphite core remained intact, a sharp, black nub hovering over the concrete floor of the cavern.

Three seconds.

Dale didn't look at the System notification. He didn't look at the red glow pulsing from the fractured crystal mass.

His eyes were locked on the three-inch square of bare concrete in front of him.

The surface was rough, pitted, and dusty, but it was a substrate. And substrates accepted markings.

The countdown in his peripheral vision ticked down.

*Two seconds.*

Dale dropped the pencil, and he grabbed his clipboard. The metal clip was bent, warped from the earlier sonic blast, but the form on top was blank.

He flipped it over to the back, where the Authority's watermark was printed in faint gray.

He pressed the graphite tip to the paper.

*One second.*

He didn't write a spell. He didn't write a prayer. He wrote a form number.

**FORM 14-B: EMERGENCY STRUCTURAL COMPROMISE & TEMPORARY EXCLUSION ZONE.**

The numbers blurred as his hand shook. He wrote *Date* and *Time*. He wrote *Location: Grid 7, Sub-basement C*. He wrote *Nature of Hazard: Lithic Awakening*.

The world stopped.

The red glow didn't fade. The mountain didn't go back to sleep. But the countdown froze at **0.00**.

A new notification popped up, overlaying the red glow in crisp, indifferent white text.

**APPLICATION RECEIVED.** **PROCESSING...** **HOLDING ACTION: TEMPORARY.** **REASON: REGULATORY REVIEW.** **DEADLINE FOR SUBMISSION OF FULL INCIDENT REPORT: 48 HOURS.**

Dale exhaled. The air in his lungs burned, tasting of copper and ozone.

His fingers were numb, the blackened skin of his palms tingling with pins and needles.

He looked at the clipboard, and the graphite marks were smudged, but legible.

*Regulatory Review.*

It wasn't a save. It was a delay. A bureaucratic injunction against his own execution.

The cavern groaned. The crack in the *Petrosoma* widened. Blue light bled from the fissure, mixing with the angry red.

The sound was no longer a hum. It was a tectonic shifting, a grinding of stone against stone that vibrated through the soles of Dale's boots.

He stood up. His knees popped.

"Unit 7," he said into the radio. His voice was a rasp. "Status."

Static. Then, a voice, distorted and wet.

"Kowalski. Did you kill it?"

"No," Dale said. "I filed a complaint."

"What?"

"The System paused the termination protocol.

I'm in a grace period. I need you to get the containment team to Grid 7. Do not approach the center.

Do not deploy acid.

I repeat, do not deploy acid.

"

He clicked the radio off. The silence that followed was heavier than before. It pressed against his eardrums.

Dale looked at his Field Guide.

The entry for *Petrosoma* was a gray blur. The text swam before his eyes, unreadable. He had lost the weakness.

He had lost the bonus damage. And now, he had lost the clock.

He had forty-eight hours, and he looked at the crystal mass.

The red glow was intensifying.

The rock face was bulging outward, veins of magma glowing beneath the surface. It wasn't dead.

It was molting.

Dale walked to the edge of the cavern floor. He picked up the broken grease pencil. He put it in his pocket. He picked up his clipboard.

Then he started walking toward the exit tunnel, and he didn't run. Running was inefficient. Running wasted calories he would need later. He walked with the measured pace of a man who knew that panic was a liability in the workplace.

The tunnel was narrow. The walls were slick with condensation. The air smelled of sulfur and rotting vegetation. As he moved away from the chamber, the red glow faded, but the vibration remained, a low-frequency thrum that rattled his teeth.

He reached the junction where the tunnel split. One path led up, to the surface. The other led down, deeper into the earth.

The radio crackled again.

"Kowalski. We have a problem."

It was Unit 7. Not the containment team. Unit 7 was the liaison. The handler.

"Dale," the voice said. It was smoother now, less panicked. "The Authority is... concerned."

"The Authority is always concerned," Dale said. He kept walking. He chose the upward path. "Did you get the team?"

"The team is on hold. We have a visitor. A Class V Auditor."

Dale stopped.

The Auditor. He had heard rumors.

The Auditors were not just inspectors, and they were prosecutors. They handled the high-level infractions, the ones that threatened the stability of the Licensing Authority's jurisdiction.

"What's the charge?" Dale asked.

"Unlicensed combat engagement is serious, Dale.

But you know that. The real issue is the cross-contamination.

You used a Sonic protocol against a Lithic entity.

That's not just a mistake. That's a violation of the Inter-Species Segregation Act.

"

"I identified the weakness," Dale said. "The system said Acid. The Auditor said Sonic. I followed the Auditor."

"The Auditor was acting under duress," Unit 7 said. "He is currently being detained.

But the System does not accept duress as a defense for protocol deviation.

Your Field Guide is corrupted.

That means you are operating without a valid identification. Any further engagement with that entity will be classified as Unidentified Hazard Elimination.

Do you understand the penalty for that?

"

"Execution," Dale said.

"Immediate and irreversible."

Dale resumed walking. His boots scraped against the gravel.

"I have forty-eight hours to submit the incident report.

I need to document the nest. I need to map the root system of the *Petrosoma*.

"

"You're not going down there."

"I have to.

If I don't document the extent of the infestation, the report is incomplete. Incomplete reports are rejected.

Rejected reports mean the violation stands.

The violation stands means I'm dead.

"

"And if you go down there, and the *Petrosoma* wakes up fully, you're dead anyway."

Dale paused at a junction. He pulled out a map from his back pocket. It was a hand-drawn schematic of the cavern system, based on seismic readings from the previous week. He traced the line of the tunnel with his finger.

"The *Petrosoma* isn't just one organism," Dale said. "It's a colony.

The crystal mass is the hive, and the blue fluid is the circulatory system.

When I cracked it, I didn't kill it. I exposed the core.

It's going to try to seal the breach. It will send out workers.

"

"Workers?"

"Stone-shapers. Lithic drones.

If they come out, they'll be classified as Class III Vermin.

I can handle Class III. I have protocols for that. I have tools for that.

I have a Field Guide entry for *Lithic Drone*.

"

Dale looked at the radio.

"Unit 7. I need you to pull my previous inspection logs from the quarry in Sector 4. Six months ago."

"Why?"

"Because I saw a similar structure there. A false alarm, I thought.

A geological anomaly.

But now I think it was a dormant *Petrosoma* larva. If I can find the correlation, I can argue that the identification was ambiguous. That my use of Sonic was a reasonable attempt at suppression given the limited data available at the time.

"

Silence on the line.

"That's... creative," Unit 7 said. "But it's risky. The Authority hates precedent. If you argue ambiguity, they might argue incompetence."

"Incompetence is a demotion," Dale said. "Ambiguity is a defense."

"You have twenty-four hours before the report deadline, Dale. Not forty-eight. The System accelerated it. The Auditor's detention triggered a review cycle."

Dale stopped walking.

Twenty-four hours, and he looked at his hands. The burns were spreading.

The blackness had reached his wrists.

He couldn't feel his fingertips anymore.

He clenched his fist. The movement was sluggish, painful.

"Understood," Dale said.

He reached the top of the tunnel. A ladder led up to a hatch. He climbed.

The surface world was raining.

Dale emerged from the grate into a mud puddle. The sky was gray and heavy. The city lights of suburban Ohio flickered in the distance, oblivious to the tectonic nightmare beneath their feet.

He walked to his truck. It was parked on the shoulder, covered in a fine layer of dust. He opened the door. The interior smelled of stale coffee and insecticide.

He didn't drive home. He drove to the Municipal Storage Facility.

The facility was a warehouse on the edge of town, filled with confiscated equipment and archived specimens. It was where Dale went to find things he couldn't afford to buy.

He parked. He got out. He walked into the facility.

The night watchman, a kid named Gary who was more interested in his phone than his job, looked up.

"Kowalski? It's three a.m."

"I need the archive room," Dale said. "I need the quarry files."

Gary hesitated.

"That's restricted. You need a supervisor sign-off."

Dale pulled out his badge. It was Class II. Silver. Shiny.

"Emergency protocol," Dale said. "Section 9, Subsection D. Technicians have access to historical data for active infestation mapping."

Gary looked at the badge. He looked at Dale's blackened hands. He looked at the clipboard tucked under Dale's arm.

"Go ahead," Gary said. "Just don't break anything."

Dale walked into the archive, and the room was cold. Rows of metal cabinets lined the walls. He found the cabinet labeled *Geological Anomalies: Sector 4*. He opened it.

The files were dusty. He pulled out the folder from six months ago. It contained photographs of the quarry.

Rock faces. Seismic charts.

He spread them out on the floor, and he looked at the photos. He looked at the seismic charts.

There.

In the third photograph, a crack in the rock wall.

It looked like a fault line. But Dale saw the pattern. It wasn't a fault.

It was a suture.

A scar from a previous emergence, and he pulled out a marker. He circled the crack.

He pulled out his Field Guide. The entry for *Petrosoma* was still blurred. But he could see the shape of the text. He could see the header.

He closed the Guide. He opened a fresh notebook.

He wrote, and he didn't write about the monster.

He wrote about the environment. He wrote about the humidity levels in the quarry. He wrote about the mineral composition of the rock.

He wrote about the vibrations detected by the seismographs, and he was building a case. Not for the monster. For himself.

If he could prove that the environment dictated the protocol, then his use of Sonic wasn't a mistake. It was an environmental adaptation.

He wrote for hours. The rain hammered against the roof of the warehouse. The lights buzzed.

At 4:00 a.m., his phone rang, which was Unit 7.

"Dale. The Auditor is released."

Dale didn't look up from his writing.

"And?"

"He's pressing charges. He's claiming you sabotaged his investigation. He's alleging you deliberately used the wrong protocol to cover up a personal vendetta."

"Vendetta against what?"

"Against the Authority. He claims you're trying to destabilize the licensing system by exposing its vulnerabilities."

Dale stopped writing. He looked at the radio.

"He's lying," Dale said.

"The System doesn't care about lies, Dale.

It cares about evidence, and the Auditor has submitted a counter-report. He's claiming your Field Guide corruption was intentional.

That you tampered with your own data to create plausible deniability.

"

Dale felt a cold knot in his stomach.

Tampering with Field Guide data was a Class I violation. The penalty wasn't execution.

It was erasure.

Total deletion of his certification. He would be stripped of his license, his status, his protection. He would be just a man in a cave with a monster.

"I didn't," Dale said.

"Then you have to prove it. And you have to do it now. The hearing is in six hours."

Dale looked at his notes, and he had the environmental data. He had the correlation.

But it wasn't enough.

It was circumstantial.

He needed proof. Physical proof.

He looked at the photos of the quarry. The crack.

He looked at his blackened hands, and he looked at the clock.

Six hours.

He needed to go back down. Not to fight. To retrieve.

There was a piece of rock in the cavern. A shard of the *Petrosoma* that had broken off when he applied the sonic blast.

It was small, no bigger than a fist.

But it was still warm.

Still active.

If he could get it, he could analyze it. He could prove its composition matched the quarry sample. He could prove the species was identical. He could prove the weakness *was* Acid, and that his Sonic attempt was a failed experiment, not a deliberate act.

But going back down meant engaging an Active Class IX Hazard.

The System would flag it, and he would be dead.

Unless.

Unless he filed a new permit.

*Permit 14-C: Specimen Retrieval for Forensic Analysis.*

It was a rare permit. It required a supervisor's signature.

He needed Unit 7.

Dale picked up the radio.

"Unit 7. I'm filing a retrieval request. I need your signature."

"You're asking me to sign a death warrant for you."

"I'm asking you to sign a liability waiver for the Authority. If I die, they lose the case. If I live, they keep the Auditor. You know what the Authority prefers."

The line went silent.

Then, a notification appeared in Dale's vision.

**PERMIT REQUEST SUBMITTED.** **AWAITING AUTHORIZATION.**

Dale stood up. He gathered his notes. He put the photos in his bag.

He walked out of the archive.

Gary was asleep at the desk.

Dale walked to his truck. He didn't start it. He sat in the driver's seat.

He opened his Field Guide again. The blur was still there. But he could feel the weight of it. The knowledge was there, just hidden.

He closed the Guide, and he started the engine.

He drove back toward the city. Toward the cavern.

As he drove, a second notification appeared.

**AUTHORIZATION GRANTED.** **PERMIT 14-C APPROVED.** **CONDITION: SOLO OPERATION. NO SUPPORT UNITS.** **NOTE: IF THE SPECIMEN IS DESTROYED, THE PERMIT IS VOIDED.**

Dale gripped the steering wheel. His knuckles turned white.

He wasn't going down to fight. He was going down to pick up a rock.

And if he failed, he wouldn't just lose his license. He would lose his life.

The radio crackled one last time.

"Dale. The Auditor is watching the feed. He's rooting for you to die."

Dale looked at the road ahead. The rain was getting heavier. The windshield wipers beat a frantic rhythm.

He didn't answer, and he turned on the highway. The city lights blurred into streaks of gold and white.

He thought about the crack in the crystal. The red glow. The blue fluid.

He thought about the twenty-four hours, and he thought about the pen in his pocket.

He had one move left. And he had no idea if it would work.

But Dale Kowalski didn't panic. He checked his mirrors. He checked his gas gauge. He checked his clipboard.

He was ready.
