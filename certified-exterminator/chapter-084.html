Chapter 84: Paper Cannot Be Hacked

# Chapter 84

Dale did not sleep. Sleep was a luxury for men who didn’t have a Class VII specimen sitting in a dry-walled conference room with their own contaminated data.

He drove home in silence, the heater blasting against the chill of the Ohio night. The truck smelled of old coffee and the metallic tang of the Authority sedan’s wet upholstery, though he hadn’t been in that car.

He kept checking the rearview mirror.

Nothing followed. The Authority’s surveillance grid was passive, but the mimic was active. It knew he was coming.

It knew he was alone.

At his apartment, Dale didn’t go inside. He went to the garage.

He unlocked the heavy steel cabinet that held his personal gear, bypassing the standard-issue kit.

He pulled out the backup tablet, wiped clean, and a hard-copy Field Guide. Paper. You couldn’t hack paper.

You couldn’t contaminate ink if it was sealed in plastic.

He spent the next four hours rewriting his entries.

Not from memory, but from the physical notes he’d taken on paper during the last three jobs, which he had scanned into the offline storage drive.

He stripped every entry of its metadata tags. No cross-referencing.

No automated suggestions. Just raw observation. The mimic had poisoned the digital network by inserting false data into his personal profile, creating a feedback loop that would cause his augmented vision to misidentify targets or apply the wrong protocols.

But the Authority’s licensing exam wasn’t a test of processing power. It was a test of procedure.

Dale sat at his workbench, a lamp illuminating the spread-out notes, and he stared at the entry for *Mimicry Substrate, Type 4*.

The mimic had copied Rachel’s signature, her speech patterns, even the specific phrasing she used when filing violation reports.

That was the trap. If Dale tried to "eliminate" the mimic by targeting the person it looked like, he would trigger a cross-contamination penalty. His Field Guide would lock up.

The Authority would mark him for unauthorized use of force against a civilian.

He had to find a way to distinguish the copy from the original without using the compromised digital overlay.

He pulled out a pencil. A standard No. 2.

Mechanical pencils jammed.

Pens ran dry. Pencils were reliable.

He drew a diagram of the examination room. Room 3B was small.

Concrete floor.

Fluorescent lighting.

One door. One window, bricked up for security.

The desk was bolted to the floor. The chair was bolted to the desk.

The mimic would be sitting in the chair.

Dale tapped the eraser against the table. If he couldn’t use his digital Field Guide, he had to use his physical one. But the exam rules stated: *using only their personal Field Guide entries.* It didn’t say the entries had to be digital. It didn’t say he couldn’t use the paper copy, provided it was his official, licensed version.

He checked his pocket. His official Field Guide was a laminated, waterproof booklet issued by the Authority.

It was currently synced to his neural link, meaning the contamination was present in the digital layer of the book, too.

But the physical text remained unchanged. The mimic could alter what he *saw*, but it couldn’t alter what was printed in the binding.

Unless.

Dale froze. The rule said: *The candidate is expected to identify, document, isolate, and eliminate the specimen using only their personal Field Guide entries.*

If the Field Guide was contaminated, any identification made *via* the Guide was suspect, and if he identified it *before* he opened the Guide, did it count?

He looked at his hands. They were shaking. Not from fear, but from the sheer, grinding exhaustion of trying to outthink a bureaucratic entity that had been operating since before humanity learned to stack stones.

He needed a loophole. A procedural error on the Authority’s part.

The notification had said the specimen would be present in the examination room; it didn’t say the specimen would be the only thing in the room. It didn’t say Robert Alderman wouldn’t be there. It didn’t say the room was empty.

If the room wasn’t empty, it was a shared workspace.

Dale’s eyes narrowed. He grabbed his clipboard. He started writing the Pre-Occupancy Inspection Report for Room 3B.

***

The County Authority Office was a beige nightmare of linoleum and flickering lights. It looked exactly like a municipal government building from the 1980s, designed to drain the will to live out of anyone who entered. Dale walked in at 8:55 AM, five minutes early.

He wore his Class II uniform, pressed and clean. His boots were polished. He carried his clipboard, his hard-copy Field Guide, and a canister of industrial-strength aerosol that looked suspiciously like standard pest control spray but was actually labeled *Protocol Delta-9: Rapid Desiccant*.

The receptionist, a woman with hair sprayed into a helmet, didn’t look up. "Room 3B. Elevator’s broken. Stairs."

Dale took the stairs. Two flights. He checked his watch. 8:58.

Room 3B was at the end of a long, dim hallway. The door was closed. A sign on the door read: *EXAMINATION IN PROGRESS. DO NOT DISTURB.*

Dale knocked.

The door opened.

Robert Alderman stood there. He was a small man with a mustache that looked like it had been drawn on with a marker.

He wore a gray suit that fit poorly.

Behind him, the room was empty except for the desk and chair.

And the mimic.

It looked like Dale.

Not Dale from today. Dale from five years ago.

Heavier.

Unshaven.

Wearing the old, faded uniform he’d used before he got his Level II certification, and the mimic-Dale sat in the chair, slouching, one leg kicked out, looking bored.

"Mr. Kowalski," Alderman said. His voice was flat, monotone. "Please enter."

Dale stepped inside. He didn’t look at the mimic. He looked at the floor.

"Is the room secure?" Dale asked.

Alderman blinked. "This is a controlled environment. Yes."

"I need to conduct a pre-treatment safety sweep," Dale said. He pulled a small, handheld device from his belt. It wasn’t a weapon. It was a moisture meter.

Alderman frowned. "The exam begins in two minutes. Sit down."

"Protocol 4.1," Dale said, pointing to his clipboard. "Before initiating any elimination protocol in an enclosed space, the technician must verify environmental stability. If humidity exceeds 60%, aerosol dispersion is compromised. I need to check the HVAC."

"This is not a field job. This is a simulation."

"The notification said 'live documentation,'" Dale said. "Live implies physical reality. If the environment is unstable, the protocol fails. I’m not starting until the room is compliant."

Alderman stared at him. For a second, the mimic-Dale smiled. It was a sharp, predatory grin.

Alderman sighed. "Fine. Check the HVAC. But you’re wasting time."

Dale didn’t go to the HVAC vent. He walked to the corner of the room, near the bricked-up window, and held the moisture meter against the wall.

The reading was normal. He moved to the door frame.

Normal, and he walked to the desk. The desk was bolted down. He ran his hand along the underside of the desk surface.

Nothing.

He turned to the chair. The mimic-Dale watched him, eyes tracking his movements with eerie precision.

Dale pulled out his hard-copy Field Guide. He flipped to the page for *Mimicry Substrate, Type 4*.

"Entry 402," Dale said aloud. "Weakness: The mimic struggles to maintain mass density when subjected to rapid thermal fluctuation. It cannot mimic heat sinks."

Alderman raised an eyebrow. "You’re reciting from the book. You’re supposed to use the digital interface."

"The digital interface is compromised," Dale said calmly. "I am using the physical text. The Authority accepts handwritten notes as primary source documentation if the electronic record is flagged for review.

My record is flagged.

See line three of the notification."

Alderman looked at his own tablet. He frowned. He didn’t say anything, but he didn’t stop Dale.

Dale walked to the mimic-Dale. He stopped two feet away.

"Stand up," Dale said.

The mimic-Dale remained seated. "Why?"

"Because you’re blocking my line of sight to the thermal regulator behind the drywall.

And because Protocol 4.1 requires me to isolate the target. You are the target. You are currently occupying the designated workspace.

Please vacate."

The mimic tilted its head. "I am the examiner’s note. I am part of the test."

"No," Dale said. "You’re an infestation. And you’re violating the occupancy limit."

Dale raised his hand. Not with a weapon. With a piece of chalk. He drew a line on the floor between himself and the mimic.

"Isolation zone established," Dale said. He looked at Alderman. "Examiner, please confirm the isolation boundary."

Alderman looked confused. "What are you doing?"

"Isolating the specimen," Dale said.

"The exam requires me to identify, document, isolate, and eliminate.

I have identified it. I have documented it.

Now I am isolating it."

"You can’t just draw a line on the floor."

"I can if I declare it a containment perimeter," Dale said. He pulled a roll of yellow caution tape from his belt.

He slapped it across the chalk line, pressing it into the linoleum with his boot.

"This is a physical barrier. The Authority’s system recognizes physical barriers as valid isolation markers if they are explicitly declared and maintained by the technician."

Alderman’s eyes widened, and he looked at the tablet. The system flickered.

A message appeared: *ISOLATION BOUNDARY DETECTED.

VALID.*

The mimic-Dale stood up. Its movement was too smooth, too fluid. It didn’t have joints.

"You’re cheating," the mimic said. Its voice sounded like Dale’s, but layered with static.

"I’m following procedure," Dale said. "And you’re breaching the boundary."

The mimic took a step forward. It crossed the yellow tape.

"Violation," Dale said. He didn’t spray. He didn’t attack. He pulled out his pencil and wrote on his clipboard.

"Specimen breached isolation perimeter. Protocol failed. Re-attempting isolation."

The mimic lunged. It moved faster than a human could. It was across the room in a blur, its hand extending into a spike of bone and cartilage, aiming for Dale’s throat.

Dale didn’t dodge. He dropped to his knees.

The spike missed him by an inch, shattering the desk behind him. Splinters flew. Alderman hid behind the doorframe, yelling about liability.

Dale was on the floor. He looked up at the mimic.

The creature was frozen, its arm embedded in the wood of the desk.

It couldn’t pull it free.

The density of the oak was too high for its current mass configuration.

Dale pulled out his canister. *Protocol Delta-9.*

He sprayed directly at the mimic’s exposed face.

The spray wasn’t poison. It was a desiccant. It sucked moisture from the air and the target.

The mimic shrieked, a sound like tearing metal. Its skin cracked and peeled, becoming gray and dry.

"Documenting termination," Dale said.

He spoke into his recorder. "Target breached isolation. Forced engagement authorized.

Using Class IV desiccant.

Specimen is losing cohesion."

He sprayed again. And again.

The mimic thrashed, pulling its arm free, leaving chunks of wood behind; it tried to reform, but the desiccant was working. It was drying out its cellular structure. The mimic was running out of mass.

Dale stood up. He walked calmly to the desk. He picked up a shard of wood. He held it like a shiv.

"Final verification," Dale said.

The mimic was on its knees, its form slumping, losing the shape of Dale, and it looked like a pile of wet clay and broken bones.

Dale slammed the wood shard into the center of the mass.

It didn’t pierce deep. But it was enough. The mass collapsed. It spread out across the floor, steaming, shrinking, until it was just a puddle of gray sludge and a few scraps of old fabric.

Dale stepped back. He took a photo with his phone. He wrote the final entry in his Field Guide.

*Specimen neutralized. Cause of death: Rapid dehydration and structural failure due to isolation breach.*

He looked at Alderman. The examiner was pale. He was checking his tablet furiously.

"The exam..." Alderman started.

"I completed the protocol," Dale said.

"Identify. Document. Isolate.

Eliminate. Verify.

All steps logged, and the isolation breach was initiated by the specimen, not the technician.

Therefore, the penalty for crossing the boundary is null and void. The elimination protocol was successful."

Alderman looked at the puddle. He looked at Dale.

"You used a physical tape barrier," Alderman said slowly. "That’s... that’s not standard."

"It’s in the manual," Dale said. "Page 42. Section C.

'Improvised containment measures are acceptable if they prevent cross-contamination.' I prevented cross-contamination. If I had fought it without isolating it, I would have risked contaminating the room.

I didn’t.

The room is still clean."

Alderman typed something. The tablet pinged.

*EXAM RESULT: PASS.* *THOROUGHNESS RATING: 9.8.* *CERTIFICATION: LEVEL V GRANTED.*

Dale exhaled. His hands were still shaking, but he hid them in his pockets.

"Thank you," Dale said. "I’ll need my updated license card."

"One moment," Alderman said. He looked at the puddle of sludge. He looked at Dale. "There’s a problem."

Dale froze. "What problem?"

"The specimen you destroyed," Alderman said. "It wasn’t the only one in the building."

The lights in the hallway flickered. Then died.

Emergency lights kicked on, bathing the room in red.

Dale heard a sound from the stairs.

A wet, slapping sound. Like bare feet on linoleum.

But heavy.

Too heavy for a human.

"The mimic was a scout," Alderman whispered. "It wasn’t trying to kill you, Kowalski. It was trying to see if you could be trusted."

"Trusted with what?"

"With the cleanup crew," Alderman said.

The door to Room 3B burst open. Not by Alderman. Not by Dale.

Something crawled in from the hallway. It looked like the office receptionist, but her limbs were too long. Her head was split down the middle, revealing a mouth full of needle-like teeth. Behind her, the hallway was filled with shadows that moved independently of the light.

Dale looked at his new license card. Level V.

He looked at his clipboard, and he looked at the door.

"Examiner," Dale said, his voice steady. "Do I need to file a workplace safety incident report?"

Alderman pulled a gun from his suit jacket. It wasn’t a pistol. It was a flare gun.

"No," Alderman said. "You need to file a casualty report. And you need to run."

Dale didn’t run. He opened his Field Guide to the last page. He wrote: *Infestation Status: Critical. Containment Failed.*

He looked at the crawling thing in the doorway. It smiled with the receptionist’s face.

"Welcome to Level V, Dale," it said. "Now the real work begins."
