Chapter 55: Self-Treatment Order

# Chapter 55

The drainage grate was warm. Not sun-warm.

Treatment-warm.

Dale pressed his palm flat against the metal and felt the residual heat of a Class-IV fumigation protocol bleeding through the concrete below. Someone had already been here. Someone had already deployed a thermal agent in an open residential zone without containment verification.

He pulled out his pen.

The strip mall parking lot was empty.

Three weeks since the last Rift event in this sector, and the Authority's cleanup crews had already hauled away the debris, the bodies, the evidence. But they hadn't hauled away the residue.

They never did.

Protocol residue bonded to surfaces at the molecular level. You could scrub it, you could power-wash it, you could pour concrete over it, and it would still be there if you knew where to look.

Dale knew where to look, and he opened his Field Guide to the blank page at the back. The Authority had not populated this page.

It was his page.

His observations.

His margins. He wrote: *Unauthorized thermal treatment, open residential zone, no containment verification.

Residual heat signature consistent with Class-IV protocol. Timestamp: roughly 14 hours before observation.*

The pen moved as usual. Fourteen years of writing violation codes had made his handwriting a tool, not an expression.

Each letter was a data point.

The Authority's system processed the Field Guide like a municipal inspector processing a citation form, and it did not care who wrote the entry. It cared that the entry existed.

He checked his watch. Sixty-one hours remaining.

Dale stood and looked south. The parking lot ran alongside a row of vacant storefronts. He had walked past them twice already on his way here, but he had not stopped because he had not found anything. Now he stopped.

The second storefront from the end had a service door, and the door was steel, painted the same gray as the building, and it had been opened recently. Not forced.

Opened.

The latch was in the unlocked position, which meant someone had used a key or had bypassed the lock cleanly. Dale looked at the doorframe. No tool marks.

No pry damage. The lock had been defeated with a technique he recognized from the Authority's own training materials.

A technician's entry, and he stepped inside.

The storefront was a former nail salon.

The chairs were still there, bolted to the floor, their vinyl seats cracked and gray.

A mirror on the back wall reflected Dale's face back at him. He looked at it without interest.

He was not checking his appearance. He was checking the reflection for treatment residue on the glass, because technicians left residue on everything they touched, and the mirror was at chest height, which meant the technician had stood here, facing the mirror, for a duration long enough to leave a heat signature.

He pressed his fingers to the glass. Warm.

The technician had been here. The technician had been preparing. Setting up a treatment station.

Dale walked deeper into the space. Behind the salon chairs, a doorway led to a back room.

The back room had been converted into something.

The nail polish counters had been pushed against the walls. The floor was covered in a grid of chalk lines, each intersection marked with a small object. Dale crouched and picked one up.

It was a pin.

A straight pin, steel, the kind used in upholstery. He turned it over.

The tip was coated in a substance that looked like dried honey but smelled like ozone.

A treatment marker. The technician was building a containment grid. Standard Class-III protocol.

Dale had seen this layout in the Authority's practical exam materials. You built a grid, you placed markers at each intersection, and when the subject crossed a boundary line, the markers activated and created a localized suppression field.

But the grid was incomplete.

Dale counted the intersections.

The pattern required forty-nine markers. There were thirty-one.

The technician had not finished.

Which meant the technician was still in the area. Still working. Still approaching.

Dale put the pin back exactly where it had been. He did not disturb the grid. He did not need to disturb it. He needed to document it.

He wrote: *Treatment station, incomplete containment grid (31/49 markers). Subject identification: unknown.

Protocol: Class-III suppression.

Location: former commercial establishment, residential zone. Violation: containment grid deployed in zone not cleared of civilian population. Requires full evacuation before activation.*

He underlined the last line twice.

The Authority's system would flag that. A containment grid in an uncleared zone was a Category 2 procedural violation. The technician would lose Thoroughness points. If the technician lost enough points, their treatment authorization could be suspended.

Dale did not know if that would stop the technician. But it would slow them down. And time was the only resource he had.

He left the salon through the back door, and the back lot was gravel and weeds. He walked east, following the direction the technician's heat signature suggested.

The trail was faint now, maybe twelve hours old, but Dale had spent fourteen years reading the faintest evidence of life in walls and under appliances.

He could interpret a twelve-hour-old thermal trail like a detective interpreting a footprint.

He found the second marker two blocks east. A pin in the asphalt, same ozone smell.

Then a third.

Then a fourth, and the technician was extending the grid outward from the salon. Building the containment perimeter.

Dale followed the line. He did not run. Running made you miss things. He walked at the pace of a technician doing a perimeter inspection, which was the pace of a man who had done ten thousand perimeter inspections and knew that the devil was in the last three feet.

The line ended at a storm drain. Dale looked down. The drain was open.

Not broken. Open.

Someone had removed the grate and set it aside, and the opening was wide enough for a person to climb through.

The technician had gone underground.

Dale knelt at the edge, and he shone his flashlight down. The drain was dry, concrete-walled, and ran straight for about thirty feet before curving out of sight.

On the floor of the drain, chalk lines continued.

The grid extended underground.

He wrote: *Containment grid extends into municipal stormwater system. Unauthorized subterranean treatment zone. Requires Authority permit for underground work. No permit displayed.*

Another violation. Category 3.

Less serious than Category 2, but it added up.

The Authority's Thoroughness Scale was cumulative.

Three violations in a treatment cycle would trigger an automatic review, and the technician would have to file a written explanation.

That would take time.

Dale checked his watch. Fifty-four hours.

He needed more than violations. He needed a weakness.

The Field Guide gave you a targeted damage bonus if you identified a weakness before combat.

But the Field Guide entry for the technician did not exist yet. The Authority had not populated it. Dale had no information about the technician's species, physiology, or behavioral patterns.

Unless he could populate it himself, and he stood at the edge of the drain and thought.

The Authority's system read Field Guide entries as factual observations made by certified technicians; the system did not verify whether the observation was true.

It verified whether the observation was *formatted correctly*.

Dale had spent fourteen years formatting observations. He knew the difference between a valid entry and an invalid one, and knew what made an entry stick and what made it get rejected.

He pulled out his pen. He opened his Field Guide to a new page. He started writing.

*Subject: First Technician. Species: Unknown.

Behavioral observation: Subject deploys containment grids in incomplete sequences, suggesting either time pressure or procedural unfamiliarity with subterranean environments.

Weakness: Subject relies on grid completion for treatment efficacy.

Grid disruption at any point during construction cycle renders all placed markers inert. Subject cannot recover markers without restarting full grid sequence.*

He wrote it in the Field Guide's entry format. Species, observation, weakness. The same format the Authority used.

The same format his own entries used, and the Authority's system would read it as a field observation by a certified technician.

It would populate the Field Guide.

And if the technician appeared before him, Dale would have a targeted damage bonus against a weakness he had identified.

Except he had not identified a weakness. He had invented one.

Dale stared at his own handwriting.

His hand was steady. It had always been steady.

He had written ten thousand reports without a tremor, without a hesitation, without a word that was not a data point.

The pen was an extension of his competence. And his competence was the only thing keeping him alive.

He closed the Field Guide.

The drain gurgled. Not water.

Something else.

A sound like a throat clearing.

Dale looked down. The chalk lines in the drain were glowing.

Faintly. A pale blue light pulsing along the grid intersections. The technician had activated the markers.

The grid was live.

Dale stepped back from the edge.

A notification appeared in his peripheral vision.

Not in the center of his thoughts this time. In the corner, like a citation. Small.

Administrative.

*Field Guide entry accepted. Subject: First Technician. Weakness logged: Grid dependency. Targeted damage bonus: Active.*

The system had accepted his invention. The loophole was real. The Authority could not distinguish between an observation and a fabrication. It could only check the format.

Dale felt something in his chest that was not relief. It was the feeling of a man who has just filed a fraudulent report and knows the inspector will come looking.

He started walking again. He needed to be somewhere when the technician surfaced.

He needed to be somewhere the technician would not expect, and the storm drain came up at the river. Dale knew this because he had inspected the river culverts twice in the past year, filing reports about sediment buildup and root intrusion. The Authority had not sent anyone to fix those reports.

The Authority never fixed the things it did not have to fix.

The river was four blocks north. Dale walked.

His hands were different, and he noticed it at the second block.

His right hand, the one holding the clipboard, was slightly larger than it had been an hour ago. Not swollen.

Not injured. Larger. The knuckles were thicker.

The skin over the back of his hand had taken on a texture that was not skin. It was close to skin.

It was close to the texture of a cockroach's thorax, and he flexed his fingers. They moved correctly. They responded to his commands.

But the sound they made when the joints flexed was wrong.

A dry click, like chitin.

The classification was not just a label.

It was a diagnosis. And a diagnosis, in the Authority's system, was a treatment plan. The Authority was treating him.

The treatment was happening to him. The Class-IX Infestation was not a description of what he was.

It was a description of what he was becoming, and he checked his watch. Fifty-one hours.

The river bank was muddy.

Dale stood at the edge and watched the water move; the storm drain outlet was visible upstream, a concrete pipe jutting from the bank.

The glow from the markers was visible through the pipe's opening, pulsing in rhythm, and the technician was still inside the grid. Still working.

Still approaching.

Dale sat down on the bank, and he opened his Field Guide. He looked at his own entry. The one the Authority had written about him. *Classification: Class-IX Infestation.

Status: Active.*

He picked up his pen.

He could not change the classification. He had tried.

The Authority's entries were not writable, and he had tested this in the first hour, pressing his pen to the page where the Authority's text sat. The ink would not take. The page rejected his writing.

But his own entries. His observations.

His fabrications.

Those were writable.

Those were his, and he wrote: *Self-observation. Subject: Dale Kowalski. Species: Unknown.

Behavioral observation: Subject exhibits compulsive documentation behavior consistent with Class-IX infestation patterns. Weakness: Subject cannot cease documentation.

Cannot stop writing.

Cannot stop filing reports. This compulsion is the infestation.*

He read it back. The words were true. That was the problem. They were true.

The notification came.

Not in his peripheral vision. Not in his thoughts.

In his hand.

The pen moved on its own, one stroke, adding a word below his entry, and the Authority had written in his Field Guide. In his own handwriting.

Using his own hand, and the word was: *Confirmed.*

Dale looked at his hand.

The pen was still moving.

He was not moving it. His fingers were writing without his command.

The pen drew a line. Then another. Then a third.

A grid. A containment grid, drawn in the margin of his Field Guide, in his own handwriting, by his own hand, while he watched.

The Authority was not treating him from the outside, and the Authority was treating him from the inside. The technician was not coming for him. The technician was already in him.

The grid was in his Field Guide.

The grid was in his hand. The grid was in the space between his thoughts, and it was building itself, marker by marker, and he could not stop it, because stopping meant stopping the documentation, and the documentation was the compulsion, and the compulsion was the infestation, and the infestation was him.

The grid completed. Forty-nine intersections. All in the margins of his Field Guide. All in his handwriting.

His hand stopped moving. The pen fell from his fingers and clattered on the mud.

A notification appeared in the center of his thoughts.

The Authority's voice. Not a thought that was not his own. His own thought.

His own voice. Saying something he had not decided to say.

*Self-treatment order issued.

Subject: Dale Kowalski.

Technician: Dale Kowalski. Protocol: Class-IX extermination.

Timeline: immediate. Compliance: mandatory.*

He had to exterminate himself, and he had to identify, document, isolate, eliminate, and verify.

He had to do it thoroughly.
