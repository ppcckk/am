Chapter 39: Eleven Hours to Breach

# Chapter 39

The galvanized steel plate was vibrating. Unlike the vibration of a door being knocked. Like a pipe shaking from water hammer at the run's end. A steady, rhythmic pulse that Dale could feel in his molars when he pressed his teeth together.

He knelt on the crawlspace hatch, one hand on the expanding foam seal, the other holding his flashlight at a forty-five-degree angle to the substrate channel. The foam was yellowing at the edges. Not the slow, chalky yellowing of UV exposure. The wet, translucent yellowing of something dissolving it from behind.

His clipboard read:

HIST-0041-A-PRIME. Status: SEALED. Integrity: 74%. Projected failure: 11 hours.

Eleven hours. He'd checked it three times. The number hadn't moved.

Dale pulled out his thermal reader. The substrate channel was 2.3 meters deep, running from the crawlspace floor into a void that the Authority's mapping system rendered as a flat gray rectangle labeled CONTAINMENT ZONE.

The temperature gradient at the seal interface was wrong.

It should have been warm on the void side, cold on the crawlspace side. Instead, both sides were the same temperature. That meant the organism wasn't generating heat.

It wasn't metabolizing, which was in stasis.

Or it was dead.

He pressed his ear against the foam.

The vibration was still there. Steady. Rhythmic.

Every four seconds.

A sound like a heartbeat, and he pulled back. Stood. Brushed dirt off his knees. Walked to the front of the house where three other units were already working.

They were Level V.

He could tell by the protocol rigs strapped to their backs: multi-spray harnesses, substrate injectors, and the heavy containment foam cannons that only Level V and above could deploy.

They'd come fast. The ALL UNITS notification meant the Authority had flagged this as a structural event, not a pest event.

Structural events pulled from the county-wide pool.

The lead unit was a woman in her fifties with a shaved head and a Field Guide tablet mounted on her forearm. Her name tag read: R. Vasquez, Level V, Structural Containment. She was already mixing a batch of neutralizing agent in a portable drum.

"You the primary?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Thoroughness rating?"

"9.2."

She looked at him the way a supervisor looks at a new hire who passed the test but hasn't seen a job site yet. "You sealed a containment organism."

"Yes."

"Without a Level V containment protocol."

"Yes."

She set down the mixing paddle. "You sealed it with expanding foam and sheet metal."

"Yes."

"And now something on the other side is trying to get through."

Dale checked his clipboard. "The organism is in stasis. It's not trying to get through. It's trying to heal."

Vasquez stopped. Looked at him. Looked at the house. Looked back.

"Heal."

"The seal is the problem. Not the breach.

The organism is a living barrier.

I sealed it in place. It can't maintain its own integrity because I'm blocking its regenerative cycle, and the vibration is its attempt to re-establish substrate contact.

The foam is preventing that.

If I leave it alone, the foam degrades, the organism resumes maintenance, and the containment holds."

"You're saying the fix is to remove your own seal."

"Yes."

"That's not how containment works. You don't remove seals. You reinforce them."

Dale pulled up his Field Guide entry. Turned the clipboard toward her. "Read the note."

Vasquez read it. Her jaw tightened.

"Do not eliminate."

She looked at Dale. "You identified this before the Authority auto-populated the entry?"

"No. The Authority added it. I didn't know what it was when I sealed it."

"Then you don't have a protocol for it."

"No."

"Neither do I." She said it flat.

No apology. No deflection. Just a fact she was stating.

"I've been doing structural containment for six years. I've never seen a Containment Organism in the field.

I've seen the entries in the reference manual.

I've never handled one."

Dale nodded. He'd been hoping she had.

"Then what do you want me to do?" she asked.

"I want you to stop what you're doing."

Vasquez looked at the drum of neutralizing agent. She looked at the foam cannon on her back. She looked at the two other Level V units who were already unspooling a containment net toward the crawlspace entrance.

"That's not your call," she said.

Dale opened his clipboard. Went to the job routing screen.

His name was listed as PRIMARY UNIT. The other three were listed as SUPPORT, and he found the option he'd seen in the Level IV protocol manual but never used.

The one that felt wrong to use. The one that was designed for situations where a junior technician had to halt a senior unit's work because the senior unit was making a mistake.

STOP ORDER, and he typed it in.

Reason: Primary unit assessment indicates current intervention will accelerate containment failure. Requesting all support units hold position pending primary unit correction of seal.

He hit submit.

Vasquez's forearm tablet buzzed. She looked down. Her expression went through three stages: confusion, recognition, and something that might have been respect if it hadn't been so much like anger.

"You just issued me a stop order."

"Yes."

"I'm Level V."

"I know."

"You're Level IV."

"Yes."

She stared at the tablet, and the stop order was real. The Authority had accepted it. The routing system recognized primary unit authority on containment events.

It was a provision designed for exactly this kind of situation: when the person who caused the problem was the only one who understood the mechanism.

"Fine," she said. "You've got the floor.

But I want you to understand something." She pointed at the house.

"The substrate channel is 2.3 meters deep. That's shallow. The organism is small.

If it dies, the breach isn't a crack. It's a door.

And everything behind that door comes through.

All of it.

At once."

"I understand."

"Do you? Because from where I'm standing, you sealed it with foam. Foam. And now you want to remove the foam and hope it heals itself."

"I want to remove the foam and give it what it needs to heal itself."

"And what's that?"

Dale looked at the crawlspace. He looked at the thermal readings. The organism was in stasis because it had no substrate contact.

It was like a plant in a sealed jar. It wasn't dead.

It was suffocating.

"Contact," he said. "It needs to touch the substrate again. That's how it maintains the barrier.

Substrate contact.

It's not a creature. It's a sealant.

A living sealant.

It works by adhering to the channel walls and maintaining a continuous membrane."

"So you need to remove your foam and let it re-adhere."

"Yes."

"And if it's too stressed to re-adhere?"

"Then we have a problem."

Vasquez was quiet for a moment. Then she walked to her truck.

Came back with a case of substrate adhesive.

Industrial grade.

The kind used for Level V structural repairs.

"You won't have a protocol for this," she said.

"No."

"You won't have a Field Guide entry for the stressed variant."

"No."

"You'll be working blind."

"Yes."

She set the case down. "I'm not going to stop you. But I'm going to be ready."

Dale nodded. He went back to the crawlspace.

The foam was at 71% integrity now. Six hours left. He pulled out his utility knife.

The blade was standard issue, not protocol-rated. It would cut foam but not the galvanized steel.

He'd need to remove the steel first, and he used the pry bar from his kit. Worked the edge under the plate. The screws were galvanized, rusted to the frame.

It took four minutes to get the first one out.

The second one snapped. He used the third and fourth screws as anchor points and levered the plate free in one piece.

Underneath, the foam was worse than he'd expected, and the substrate channel was narrow, maybe thirty centimeters across. The foam had expanded into every crack, every irregularity; it was bonded to the channel walls on all four sides.

He'd sealed the organism against its own substrate.

He started cutting.

Slow.

Deliberate. Working from the edges inward, like he was removing a caulk seal around a window.

You don't pull. You score. You lift.

You let the bond release on its own terms, and the foam came away in chunks.

Each chunk left a residue on the channel walls. The residue was the problem. It was polymer-based.

It was blocking the organism's adhesive contact, and he needed a solvent. Not the neutralizing agent Vasquez was mixing. That was designed to dissolve dimensional substrate, not polymer residue. He needed something that would break down the foam's bond without damaging the biological membrane underneath.

He checked his Field Guide. No entry for the organism. No entry for the stressed variant. No protocol for anything.

He checked his toolkit. Standard Class II kit.

Adhesive remover.

The kind for removing old caulk from bathroom tile. Acetone-based.

It wasn't protocol-rated. It wasn't Authority-approved. It wasn't even close.

But it was what he had, and he poured a small amount onto the residue. Waited.

The foam softened.

The bond released.

He wiped it away with a rag.

Underneath, the channel wall was alive. Not alive like a plant. Alive like a wall that had been painted so many times the paint had become part of the structure.

A thin, translucent membrane that pulsed faintly. The same rhythm as the vibration.

Four seconds, and he worked the full length of the channel. Thirty centimeters wide, two meters deep. It took forty minutes.

His knees ached.

His back ached. The acetone fumes made his eyes water.

When he finished, the membrane was exposed.

Bare. Vulnerable. It was thinner than he expected.

Maybe two millimeters. It looked like the skin on the inside of a wrist.

He stepped back. Watched.

The membrane pulsed. Four seconds. Four seconds. Four seconds.

Then it stopped.

Dale held his breath.

The membrane re-pulsed. Slower. Five seconds. Then six.

It was stressed, and he could see it.

The translucency was uneven. Dark patches marked spots where the membrane thinned more.

Places where his foam had bonded too aggressively and pulled the tissue when he removed it.

His clipboard buzzed.

HIST-0041-A-PRIME. Status: REACTIVATED.

Integrity: 31%.

Note: Stressed variant detected. Regenerative cycle impaired. Estimated recovery: 72-96 hours.

Seventy-two to ninety-six hours. The containment was running on a timer now.

He looked at the sky. The wall-before-it-cracks color was spreading. Slowly. Like a stain on drywall.

Vasquez was at the front of the house, watching. She'd seen the color change. She'd seen the clipboard notification. She knew what it meant.

Dale walked out.

"It's alive," he said. "But it's damaged. It needs three to four days to recover."

"Can it hold that long?"

"I don't know."

She looked at the sky, and the stain was wider now. Maybe ten degrees of arc.

It wasn't moving fast.

But it was moving.

"What do you need?" she asked.

"Time. And a secondary seal that doesn't interfere with its regenerative cycle."

"I don't have a protocol for that."

"Neither do I."

They stood there. Two licensed technicians. One Level V, one Level IV. Staring at a sky that was the wrong color.

Dale's clipboard buzzed again.

NEW NOTIFICATION.

CONTAINMENT INTEGRITY: CRITICAL. ALL UNITS. SECONDARY BREACH DETECTED. SUBSTRATE CHANNEL: HIST-0041-B.

Dale looked at the notification. Then he looked at Vasquez.

"There's another one," he said.

"Another what?"

"Another containment organism. Another seal. And it's already compromised."

He checked the location. It was four miles east. A commercial property. A warehouse district.

His clipboard updated.

PRIMARY UNIT ASSIGNMENT: DALE KOWALSKI.

Of course it was, and he looked at the house. The crawlspace.

The membrane pulsing below.

Thirty-one percent integrity.

Seventy-two to ninety-six hours.

He looked at the notification. Four miles east. A second seal. Already failing.

He couldn't be in two places at once.

Vasquez saw his face. "You can't leave the first one."

"No."

"But you have to go to the second one."

"Yes."

"Then who watches this one?"

Dale looked at the house. At the crawlspace hatch. At the membrane he'd just freed. At the sky that was cracking.

He opened his clipboard. Found the option he'd never used. The one he'd hoped never to use.

DELEGATED RESPONSIBILITY, which was a provision for Level V and above, and it allowed a primary unit to transfer containment oversight to a support unit. It was meant for shift changes.

For exhaustion. For situations where the primary unit was incapacitated.

He wasn't incapacitated. He was splitting himself.

He typed it in.

Delegated unit: R. Vasquez, Level V, Structural Containment.

He hit submit.

Vasquez's tablet buzzed. She read it. Her face went still.

"You're making me responsible for this."

"Yes."

"If it fails, that's on me."

"Yes."

She looked at him, and the anger was back. But it was different now. Sharper.

More specific.

"You're four miles away from the thing you broke."

"Yes."

"And if I fail, the first breach opens while you're dealing with the second one."

"Yes."

She picked up her foam cannon. Checked the charge. Checked the neutralizing agent. Checked her Field Guide tablet.

"Go," she said.

Dale went.
