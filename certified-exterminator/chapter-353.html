Chapter 353: Proving a Point

# Chapter 353

Gravity didn't announce its arrival. It simply pulled.

The municipal water tanker, modified with steel plating and a reinforced roll cage, hit the dirt embankment outside the sewer exit with a sound like a dumpster being crushed by a trash compactor. Suspension rods screamed. Glass shattered. The chassis groaned under the weight of three tons of industrial-grade pesticide and the two men inside.

Gary kept his foot on the gas.

The tires spun in the mud, clawing for purchase. The vehicle fishtailed, sliding sideways toward the tree line.

Dale's forehead knocked against the windshield, leaving a smear of blood on the safety glass.

He didn't wipe it away. He watched the horizon instead.

Behind them, the tunnel mouth erupted.

Not with fire. With geometry.

Blue lattice lines snapped into existence across the sky, forming a grid that overlaid the moon.

Red warning glyphs rotated within the squares. The Authority's containment field.

It was expanding, a dome of bureaucratic order pushing back against the chaos of the rift.

And from the center of that grid, things were falling. Not flying. Falling.

Thousands of chitinous bodies, stripped of their exoskeletons, tumbling through the air like rain.

Dale's hand twitched. His fingers sought the clipboard that wasn't there. The phantom weight of the wood and paper ghosted across his palm. He clenched his fist, knuckles white, and forced his hand to rest on the gearshift lever next to Gary's.

"Drive," Dale said. His voice was flat. Dead. "Keep moving."

Gary's hands shook on the wheel. The old apprentice, the man who had stolen diesel three years ago, looked at Dale with a mixture of pity and terror. He didn't look like a survivor. He looked like a man who had watched the world end and decided the only way to survive it was to break every rule.

"They can't track the truck," Gary said, his voice cracking. "The transponder is off.

The Authority's scanners are looking for licensed operators.

You're suspended, Dale.

You're a ghost.

"

"Is that a fact?" Dale asked.

"Yes. And I'm not licensed. So we're invisible."

Gary grinned again. It was a jagged expression, too wide for his face. He looked like he hadn't slept in a week.

His uniform was torn, stained with sewage and something darker. He smelled of rust and fear.

Dale looked at the rearview mirror. The blue grid was closing in. The falling things were hitting the trees, the houses, the streets of Oakhaven. Screams drifted up from the neighborhood, thin and distant.

"Where is the worst infestation?" Dale asked.

Gary glanced at a crumpled map on the dashboard. It was marked up with red circles. Handwritten notes. *Sector 4: Structural failure. Sector 9: Subterranean breach.*

"The Old Mill," Gary said.

"The Authority quarantined it six months ago. They said the air was toxic. They said the residents had been relocated.

But my dad lived there.

He didn't leave.

"

Dale didn't ask why, and he looked at the fuel gauge. Half a tank. He looked at the pesticide tanks on the back.

Full. He looked at Gary.

"Does the mill have a loading dock?"

"Yeah. Large enough for a truck."

"Then we go in the back door."

Gary hesitated.

"That's suicide.

The infestation is Class 4. Maybe higher.

We don't have a squad. We don't have support.

"

"We have a truck full of Dursban," Dale said. "And we have a driver who knows how to handle a stick shift. That's enough."

Gary swallowed. He shifted gears. The engine roared, a low, guttural sound that vibrated through the floorboards. The truck lurched forward, mud spraying into the headlights.

***

Three miles away, inside the perimeter of the Authority's containment grid, Agent Silas Vane adjusted his visor.

The air inside the bubble was sterile. Filtered.

Regulated.

The smell of ozone masked the rot of the dead things piled against the barrier. Vane watched the thermal scan on his tablet. A single heat signature was moving away from the tunnel, heading north.

It wasn't a monster. The signature was too stable, too low-heat. A vehicle.

"Vane," a voice crackled in his earpiece. "Report."

"Subject is departing the breach zone," Vane said. "Vehicle identification: Municipal Service Truck 44-B. License plate is obscured. Driver is unregistered."

"Is it Dale Kowalski?"

Vane zoomed in on the thermal feed. The figure in the passenger seat was hunched over. The driver was upright. Aggressive posture.

"Positive ID on the passenger. Heat signature matches Kowalski's biometrics. The driver is unknown. Likely unauthorized personnel."

"Kowalski is suspended," the voice said.

Cold. Bureaucratic.

"He has no authority to operate.

If he leaves the grid, he is in violation of Protocol 9.

"

"What are the orders?"

"Neutralize. If he resists, terminate the vehicle.

Do not let him reach the Outer Zones, and he carries data.

He carries the Field Guide entries. If he gets to the Rift's edge, he could trigger a cascade.

"

Vane lowered his tablet. He pulled a standard-issue tranquilizer dart gun from his belt. It was useless against a truck. He needed a containment drone.

He tapped his comms.

"Deploying Drone Unit 7. Tracking target. ETA two minutes."

The drone buzzed out of the containment wall, a swarm of metallic wasps with optical sensors. It soared into the night, following the heat trail.

Vane watched it go. He felt a strange tightness in his chest. Kowalski was a bureaucrat.

A pedant. He wrote reports in triplicate.

He argued about entry points and frass trails, and he wasn't a fighter. He wasn't a hero.

Why was he running?

And why was he taking a truck full of poison to the one place the Authority had sealed off for good?

Vane checked the map.

The Old Mill. A dead zone.

A place where the rules of reality had frayed so badly that the Authority had given up on containment.

It was a hole in the system. A place where documentation was impossible.

If Kowalski went there, he wasn't trying to win. He was trying to disappear.

Or he was trying to die.

Vane didn't like either option. He preferred order. He preferred paperwork.

He tapped his comms again.

"Cancel Drone Unit 7. Switch to direct intervention. I'm going after him myself."

The voice in the ear was silent for a second.

"That is against regulation. You are an administrator."

"The truck is moving fast," Vane said. "And if he reaches the Mill, he breaks the quarantine seal. That's a Class 9 violation. I'm going."

He turned off his comms. He stepped out of the containment bubble, into the dirty, screaming night.

***

The truck hit a pothole, jarring Dale's spine.

Gary was sweating. He wiped his forehead with a rag that smelled of motor oil.

The headlights cut through the darkness, illuminating the suburban sprawl.

Houses stood dark and silent, and the streetlights were dead. The power grid had failed in most sectors, a side effect of the dimensional bleed.

"See that?" Gary pointed to the left.

A house was wrapped in webbing. Not spider silk. Something thicker.

Translucent. Pulsing.

Dale leaned forward. He squinted.

"Entry point," he said.

"What?"

"The webbing.

It's not random. It's structural. Look at the foundation.

The cracks in the concrete.

They match the tension lines in the web.

"

Gary slowed the truck.

"You're analyzing a monster's nest while we're being hunted?"

"I'm analyzing a code violation," Dale said.

"If the foundation is compromised, the infestation is inside the load-bearing walls. That's a K-7. Structural entry point.

It means they're not just living there. They're eating the house.

"

"So what do we do? Stop? Spray it?"

"No," Dale said.

"Keep driving.

If we stop, we become bait. The Authority will catch us.

The monsters will smell the fuel.

"

"The fuel?"

"Dursban is an organophosphate. It smells like almonds and death. Monsters love it. It's a pheromone trigger for some species."

Gary's eyes went wide.

"You didn't say that before."

"I didn't think it was relevant until now."

The truck swerved around the webbed house. Dale watched the structure recede in the mirror.

He felt a pang of something.

Not fear. Regret. He knew he could have saved that house.

He could have sprayed the foundation, and he could have written a report that forced the city to condemn the property before the infestation took hold.

But he had been suspended. He had no license. He had no clipboard.

He was just a man in a truck.

"Up ahead," Gary said. "The Mill."

The silhouette of the old textile factory loomed against the sky. It was a black mass, jagged and broken. No lights. No sound.

But the air was changing.

Dale rolled down the window.

The wind hit his face. It smelled of wet wool and copper. And beneath that, a sweet, rotting scent.

The smell of decay accelerated by time distortion.

"Turn off the headlights," Dale said.

"Why?"

"Light attracts them. And we're already visible from the drone."

Gary flipped the switch. Darkness swallowed them.

The truck crept forward, engine idling.

Dale listened, and he heard the creak of metal. The settle of wood.

And then, a sound like dry leaves skittering across pavement.

They were here.

Not in the ground. Not in the sky. In the walls.

Dale opened the glove compartment. It was empty.

No spare tire.

No first aid kit. Just a crumpled receipt from a hardware store. *Purchase: 12 cans of Dursban. Date: Tuesday.*

He picked up the receipt. He held it like a shield.

"Gary," Dale said. "Do you have a pen?"

Gary blinked. "What?"

"A pen. Do you have one?"

Gary reached into his pocket and pulled out a cheap ballpoint. He handed it over.

Dale took it. He looked at the receipt. He turned it over.

On the back, in shaky handwriting, he wrote: *Field Guide Entry: Class 4 Infestation.

Location: Old Mill. Species: Unknown.

Entry Point: Foundation.

Status: Untreated.*

He underlined *Untreated*.

"What are you doing?" Gary whispered.

"Documenting," Dale said.

"We're about to die."

"Then let's die properly," Dale said. "Protocol requires identification before elimination.

If we go in blind, we contaminate the site.

If we contaminate the site, the Authority can't clean it up.

The Rift spreads.

"

Gary stared at him.

"You're serious."

"I'm always serious."

Dale opened the door.

The darkness rushed in. Cold. Wet.

"Stay in the truck," Dale said. "Keep the engine running. If I don't come back in five minutes, drive away. Don't wait for me."

"Dale—"

"Go."

Dale stepped out, and the ground was soft. Mud. He squelched through it, his boots sinking to the ankles.

He walked toward the loading dock of the Mill. The doors were chained shut.

Rust eaten through the metal.

He reached into his belt. He still had his multi-tool. The one they hadn't confiscated because it wasn't a weapon. Just pliers and a knife.

He used the pliers to twist the chain. It snapped. The links fell to the ground with a clatter.

He pulled the doors open.

Darkness.

And then, movement.

Not one thing. Thousands.

They spilled out of the shadows. Not rats. Not beetles.

Something else.

Something that looked like hair. Thousands of thick, black strands, writhing independently, each one tipped with a small, barbed hook.

Hairworms.

Dale froze, and he knew this species.

He had seen it in a textbook once. A rare urban parasite that fed on static electricity. It lived in power lines.

It lived in old buildings with bad wiring.

But these were massive. The size of snakes. And they were hungry.

They surged toward him.

Dale didn't run. He didn't scream.

He reached for the handle of the pesticide sprayer mounted on the side of the truck. He grabbed the nozzle. He pointed it at the tide of black hair.

"Protocol: Eliminate," he muttered.

He pulled the trigger.

The jet of chemical hit the front of the swarm. The worms recoiled, their barbs curling inward. They shrieked, a high-pitched frequency that made Dale's teeth ache.

But they didn't stop. They poured over the dead ones, climbing up the chemical barrier.

Dale sprayed again. And again. The tank hissed. Pressure dropping.

Then, a voice.

"Stop."

Dale turned.

Agent Silas Vane stood on the loading dock. He wore the grey suit of the Authority. His face was pale. His hands were empty.

"You're contaminating the site," Vane said. "You're using a Class 2 protocol on a Class 4 organism. That's a violation."

Dale looked at the worms. They were circling. Waiting.

"I'm exterminating," Dale said.

"You're failing," Vane said. "Your Thoroughness Rating will drop to zero. You'll be stripped of your certification. Permanently."

Dale looked at the receipt in his hand. He looked at Vane.

"Then tell me," Dale said. "What's the correct protocol?"

Vane paused. He looked at the worms. He looked at Dale.

"There isn't one," Vane said.

"This species doesn't exist in our database.

We've never seen it. It's a mutation.

A glitch."

Dale's eyes narrowed.

"A glitch," Dale said.

"Yes."

Dale looked at the worms. They were moving faster now. Coiling.

If the Authority didn't know what it was, they couldn't kill it. They could only contain it. Or burn it down.

And if they burned it down, the evidence went with it. The data. The weakness.

Dale had spent his life finding weak points. Sealing entry points. Writing the report that mattered.

He looked at Gary, who was staring out the truck window, terrified.

He looked at Vane, who was afraid of the unknown.

Dale stepped back. He dropped the sprayer.

"You're right," Dale said. "I don't know the protocol."

Vane relaxed, slightly.

"But I know where they live."

Dale pointed to the ceiling of the warehouse.

Above them, thick bundles of black hair hung like chandeliers. The source.

"They're not an infestation," Dale said. "They're a colony."

Vane frowned. "That doesn't help you."

"It helps me," Dale said. "Because if they're a colony, they have a queen."

He walked toward the stairs leading up into the dark.

"Where are you going?" Vane shouted.

"To find the nest," Dale said.

"That's suicide."

"No," Dale said. "It's documentation."

He disappeared into the shadows.

Behind him, the worms surged forward, sensing his movement. Vane drew a flare gun. He aimed at the stairs.

But he didn't fire, and he watched Dale walk into the dark.

And for the first time in his career, Silas Vane didn't write a report. He just waited.

Because he knew, with a sinking feeling in his gut, that Dale Kowalski wasn't trying to survive.

He was trying to prove a point.

And if he failed, the world would end.
