Chapter 50: Four Seconds to Lock

# Chapter 50

The pen touched paper. Dale watched the nib press down and knew that once the stroke was complete, once the species name settled into the Field Guide's binding, identification would lock.

Protocol would advance.

Isolation would follow. He had maybe four seconds.

He didn't run. Running was frass. Running was a trail. A Level VI operator would read a sprint like a technician reads a droppings line along a baseboard, and the direction would tell them everything.

Instead, Dale walked.

Slow. Deliberate.

He crossed the parking lot toward the commercial strip's rear service alley, keeping his shoulders square, his pace even.

You walked like you were doing a routine perimeter check where nothing warranted alarm.

The technician's pen moved. The nib dragged. The entry formed.

Dale felt it land. A pressure behind his eyes, like the pop you get when cabin altitude changes.

His own Field Guide, the notebook in his jacket pocket, vibrated against his ribs.

He didn't look, and he kept walking.

He turned the corner into the alley. Dumpsters. Grease trap.

A torn bag of ice spilling onto cracked asphalt. The technician followed.

He could hear the clipboard's edge tapping against their thigh, a rhythmic sound, like a metronome set to the tempo of someone who'd done this a hundred times.

Dale stopped at the grease trap. He crouched. Pried the lid up with his boot.

The smell hit him, rancid and sweet, the particular rot of accumulated neglect.

He breathed through his mouth. Professional.

The technician appeared at the alley's mouth.

Clipboard raised. Field Guide open to a fresh page. Their pen was already moving again, filling in behavioral observations.

Posture. Gait.

Environmental interaction.

They built the entry from the outside in, identifying an unseen species by elimination and pattern.

Dale knew this process. He'd done it a thousand times.

You observe. You compare. You eliminate.

You confirm, and he also knew the one thing that breaks it.

"You're using a provisional framework," Dale said.

He didn't turn around. He was scraping grease off the trap's lip with his thumbnail, a habit, a compulsion, his hands doing what they always did when the environment was out of spec. "You haven't collected a specimen.

You're working from behavioral inference alone."

The pen didn't stop.

"Provisional entries don't support Level VI protocols," Dale continued. "You know this.

Section 4.2, subsection C.

A provisional identification can only support containment. Not elimination. You're going to need a confirmed entry before you can deploy the treatment suite."

The pen paused. One second. Two. Then resumed.

Dale stood. Turned.

The technician was mid-thirties, maybe, wearing a standard Authority field jacket, the kind with too many pockets.

Their face was neutral.

Not hostile. Not friendly.

The face of someone reading a work order.

"Your classification is provisional," the technician said. "That's accurate. I'm building toward confirmation."

"Then you need a specimen."

"I have a specimen."

Dale looked at the notebook in his own pocket. The one with the weakness notation. The one he'd written in. The one that now had his handwriting on it, his behavioral data, his confirmed stress response to protocol violation.

The notation wasn't information. It was bait.

He understood it like finding a snap trap behind the stove. Someone had placed it.

Someone had set it in a location where he'd naturally go, in a format he'd naturally read, with content that would naturally trigger a response.

His response was the specimen. His acknowledgment of the weakness was the behavioral confirmation.

He'd walked into his own monitoring station.

Dale pulled the notebook from his pocket. The technician's pen stopped. This time.

"Don't," the technician said.

"I'm not destroying it." Dale held the notebook up. Open. Visible. "I'm annotating it."

He flipped to the page with the weakness notation. Below the technician's handwriting, in his own cramped script, he wrote: Behavioral response confirmed. Specimen self-documenting. Cross-reference: practitioner.

The technician's Field Guide flickered. The entry they'd been building shuddered, the ink rearranging itself, the species name smearing into something provisional again.

"That's not how it works," the technician said.

Their voice had changed. Not angry.

Puzzled.

He sounded like a technician finding a nest that didn't match the species profile.

"It's exactly how it works." Dale kept writing. "You identified me as an infestation.

I've identified myself as a practitioner.

Two competing classifications, and the Authority has to reconcile them before it can proceed. You know the reconciliation process.

It takes time."

"It takes an hour."

"Then I have an hour."

The technician closed their Field Guide. Opened it again.

The entry was gone, and the page was blank. The Authority had pulled it pending reconciliation.

"You've made this harder," the technician said. "Not better. Harder."

"I've made it accurate."

"You've made it ambiguous. Ambiguity doesn't protect you. It just delays the treatment and increases the classification tier when it resolves."

Dale knew that. He knew that unsealed entry points lead to infestations returning within six weeks, with populations harder to control than the original. Ambiguity was a gap in the seal. It would let more through.

But he needed the hour.

The technician's phone buzzed, and they checked it. Their expression shifted, the first real change in their face.

They looked at Dale differently now.

Not as a specimen. As a variable.

"The Authority is escalating," the technician said. "They're pulling your file from the local branch. Routing it to regional."

"Good."

"Good? Regional doesn't do provisional. Regional does confirmed. They'll send a second operator."

"Then there'll be two identifications."

"Yes."

"And if the two identifications conflict?"

The technician was quiet for three seconds. "Then the Authority creates a new species entry. A reconciliation entry. Something that fits both classifications."

Dale wrote that down. In his notebook. The act of writing it made it real, made it part of the documentation trail, made it something the Authority would have to account for when it reconciled.

He knew what reconciliation entries looked like. He'd seen them in the Authority's public database, the ones that were released for educational purposes.

They were rare.

A creature that defied two existing classifications got its own entry, its own protocol, its own treatment tier.

Usually higher. Always more specific.

The technician was typing on their phone. Calling the second operator. Dale could hear the clipped exchange, the transfer of case data, the confirmation of jurisdiction.

He had maybe twenty minutes before the second operator arrived. Twenty minutes to make his own documentation airtight. To make his practitioner classification so complete, so thorough, so undeniable that the reconciliation couldn't simply discard it.

He opened his notebook to a fresh page. Started writing. Not his own entry. The technician's.

He documented them as he would any practitioner observed in the field. License number (visible on their jacket, standard Authority issue). Certification level (VI, confirmed by the Authority's call).

Jurisdiction (regional, per the escalation). Behavioral markers (calm, procedural, protocol-adherent).

Environmental interaction (clipboard, Field Guide, phone, all tools of the trade).

He was building a second file. A parallel documentation. If the Authority had two complete files, one for Dale-as-infestation and one for Dale-as-practitioner, and a third for the technician-as-practitioner, the reconciliation would have to account for all three.

The technician watched him write. They didn't stop him. They couldn't.

Stopping a practitioner from documenting is itself a protocol violation, and they knew this. Dale saw it in their tightened grip on the clipboard and set jaw.

"You're building a case," the technician said.

"I'm building a file. There's a difference."

"What's the difference?"

"A case argues. A file states."

The technician's phone buzzed again, and they answered. Dale couldn't hear the other end, but he could read the technician's face. The confirmation.

The arrival time. The operator was five minutes out.

Dale finished the technician's entry. Tucked the notebook into his jacket. Looked at the technician.

"What's your name?" he asked.

"Tanaka."

"Yuki Tanaka."

"That's my name."

"Tanaka, you identified me using a monitoring tag. That's a Level VI technique. It requires a valid monitoring station license. Do you have one?"

Tanaka's expression didn't change, but their hand moved to their jacket pocket. Checked something. Came back empty.

"I had one," Tanaka said.

"You had one."

"It was suspended. Forty-eight hours ago. A documentation irregularity in a previous case."

Dale nodded.

Wrote this in his notebook.

Not for the Authority. For himself.

For the file he was building. A suspended monitoring license meant the bait station was unlicensed. Unlicensed monitoring data is inadmissible.

The specimen was tainted.

Tanaka saw him write it. Saw the implications land. Their professional composure cracked, just slightly, a hairline fracture along the jaw.

"That doesn't invalidate the identification," Tanaka said. "It just means the evidence chain has a gap."

"An evidence chain with a gap is an incomplete file. An incomplete file can't support a Level VI protocol. You know this."

"I know this."

"Then you know what happens when the second operator arrives and finds that the primary identification is built on inadmissible data."

Tanaka was quiet. The alley smelled like old grease and the chemical tang of the dumpster behind them. Somewhere above, the commercial strip's security light buzzed and flickered, its ballad dying.

"They'll reclassify," Tanaka said. "They'll pull the entry. They'll start over."

"They'll start over," Dale agreed.

"And when they start over, they'll have two practitioners in the field. Me and you. Two files.

Two identifications.

Both valid. Both incomplete."

"That's not how the Authority works."

"It's exactly how the Authority works. It treats what it can identify. It can't identify either of us cleanly. So it does what it always does with ambiguous data."

Tanaka's face went still. They knew. They'd seen it before, maybe. Two practitioners' files overlapped so thoroughly that the Authority couldn't distinguish one from the other.

"A reconciliation entry," Tanaka said.

"Both of us."

"That's not a treatment. That's a containment."

"It's the only protocol the Authority has for two entities that can't be distinguished from each other."

The sound of a vehicle on the road behind them. Tires on wet asphalt. The second operator.

Dale pulled his notebook out.

Tore the page with his own entry from the front. Tore the page with Tanaka's entry from the back. Held them together.

Two pages. Two files.

Two people performing identical work, documenting identically, and responding to identical stimuli.

The Authority's system couldn't tell them apart.

Dale taped the two pages together with a strip of duct tape he pulled from his jacket.

Standard practice. You bind related documentation.

You keep it in one file. You treat related findings as a case.

The technician's Field Guide opened on its own. The Authority, reaching in.

Reading.

The blank page where Dale's entry had been was filling now. Not with Dale's name. Not with Tanaka's name.

With something else.

A species name neither of them had seen before.

Dale read it. The ink was still wet, still forming, the letters settling into place like a diagnosis arriving after too long a wait.

Self-treating entity, and the Authority had a new species. And it was both of them.

Tanaka's phone rang. They answered. Listened. Their face went white.

"They're not sending a second operator to treat me," Tanaka said. "They're sending one to treat both of us. Same protocol. Same file."

"Of course they are."

"One operator. Two infestations. Same species."

"Same file," Dale corrected. "You and I are one file now. One entry. One treatment plan."

Tanaka looked at the duct-taped pages in Dale's hand. Looked at the Field Guide, where the new species name pulsed with Authority ink. Looked at the headlights rounding the corner of the commercial strip.

The vehicle stopped.

The door opened. A figure stepped out. Clipboard in hand.

Field Guide open, and they walked toward them both.
