Chapter 323: Final Assessment

# Chapter 323

The rain didn't fall straight. It curved around the perimeter of the crater like iron filings drawn to a magnet, slicking the asphalt with a sheen that looked too oily for water.

Officer Miller adjusted his radio, his knuckles white inside the tactical gloves. He stared at the figure standing in the center of the exclusion zone.

The man wasn't holding a weapon, and he wasn't crouching in a combat stance. He was holding a clipboard.

"Command, this is Unit 4-Bravo," Miller whispered into his comms, his voice cracking.

"Suspect is... stationary. No visible threat posture.

Repeat, no visible threat posture.

But the air smells like burnt sugar and old blood.

"

Static hissed back.

Miller squinted through the rain-streaked visor. The suspect's truck sat idling ten yards away, its hazard lights blinking a rhythmic orange pulse that seemed to sync with the throbbing pain in Miller's left temple.

Inside the vehicle, silhouettes moved.

Not men.

Something else. Something that shifted when Miller blinked.

"Command, do you have eyes on the Core?" Miller asked.

"Negative. Sensors are down. You are on your own, 4-Bravo. Maintain distance."

Distance. Miller knew the number. Fifty yards.

Standard protocol for unverified Class-4 anomalies. He was at fifteen.

The suspect turned.

Miller flinched, his hand dropping to the pistol at his hip, and the movement was automatic, trained, useless. The man—Dale Kowalski, according to the Authority's public alert feed—didn't raise a hand. He just tilted his head.

The motion was jerky, mechanical, like a doll whose strings had been cut and re-tied by someone who had never seen a human walk before.

Miller's breath hitched. He saw the eyes. Or rather, he saw the absence of them. The sockets were dark pits, reflecting the red glow of the flare still smoking in the sky above.

But it was the clipboard that froze Miller's blood.

It wasn't wood. It wasn't plastic.

It was a slab of something translucent, pulsing with faint, geometric veins of light.

And on it, words were forming. Not written. *Growing*. Like mold on bread, but fast.

Miller stepped back. His boot slipped on the wet pavement. He caught himself, heart hammering against his ribs.

*Don't look at the text,* a voice in the back of his mind warned. *Focus on the threat. Focus on the gun.*

He raised the pistol. The sight picture blurred.

The rain made everything soft, indistinct.

But Dale Kowalski remained sharp.

Too sharp. Every line of his silhouette was absolute.

"Step away from the anomaly!" Miller shouted. His voice sounded thin, swallowed by the rain. "Drop the device!"

Dale didn't drop the device. He clicked his pen.

The sound was loud. A mechanical *click-clack* that echoed across the empty street, louder than the thunder rolling over the city limits.

Miller's radio crackled.

"4-Bravo, we are detecting a massive spike in dimensional energy. It's not coming from the suspect. It's coming from *under* him."

Miller looked down, and the asphalt was bubbling.

Small, black bubbles rose to the surface, popping with a sound like wet footsteps. From each pop, a tiny, six-legged thing emerged. They weren't rats.

They weren't insects. They were shards of glass shaped like ants, each one no bigger than a grain of rice, but vibrating with a frequency that made Miller's teeth ache.

*Shard-Ant Infestation*, Miller's mind supplied. A new entry. He hadn't seen it in the briefing.

The ants didn't attack Miller, and they swarmed toward Dale. They crawled up his pants, over his boots, into his pockets. He didn't brush them off.

He didn't scream.

He just watched them, his pen hovering over the clipboard.

Miller lowered his gun slightly. Confusion replaced fear. Why wasn't he shooting? Why wasn't he running?

Then Dale spoke.

"Kowalski. Level IX," Dale said. His voice was flat, devoid of accent, devoid of emotion. It sounded like a recording.

Miller blinked.

"What?"

Dale looked up, and the rain ran down his face, pooling in the hollows of his cheeks.

"You're contaminating the site, Officer. You're standing in a Class-3 bio-hazard zone without a respirator.

That's a violation of Code R-4.

"

Miller looked at his mask. It was intact.

"My mask is on."

"Your filter is clogged.

See the particulate buildup on the intake valve?

" Dale pointed with his pen.

"I documented it three minutes ago. You're breathing in spores.

You have approximately forty minutes before you start hallucinating that the sky is made of teeth.

"

Miller touched his mask. He felt something gritty on the intake. He hadn't noticed. He had been too busy watching the monster.

"I'm calling for backup," Miller said, his voice trembling.

"Backup is irrelevant," Dale said. He turned back to the swirling mass of glass ants.

"You're not qualified to handle this.

You're just standing there. Which makes you a hazard.

"

A glass ant crawled onto Miller's hand.

It bit him. It didn't hurt.

It felt cold.

Ice-cold.

Miller yanked his hand back, shaking it violently. The bite mark didn't bleed. It turned black. The black spread up his wrist, tracing the veins.

"Command!" Miller screamed. "I've been bitten! I need med-evac!"

"Stay calm, 4-Bravo. Administering stimulant. Hold position."

"His veins are turning black!"

"Copy. Hold position."

Miller looked at Dale. Dale was writing. Fast. The pen moved in a blur, scratching against the translucent surface. *Scratch-scratch-scratch.*

Miller's vision swimmed. The rain seemed to stop falling.

The puddles on the ground rose up, forming shapes.

Faces.

Screaming faces.

*The sky is made of teeth.*

Miller shook his head. He blinked hard. The faces vanished.

But the cold was spreading. His legs felt heavy.

Dale looked up again, and he reached into his truck's open bed and pulled out a spray can. It wasn't a standard pesticide. It was labeled with symbols Miller didn't recognize.

Geometric shapes.

Sharp angles.

"Clear the area," Dale said. "I'm initiating Protocol Zeta. Cross-contamination risk is high."

"I can't move," Miller slurred. His tongue felt thick. "My legs..."

"Then I'm treating you as part of the infestation."

Dale aimed the can at Miller.

Miller's finger tightened on the trigger. He wasn't going to let this man kill him. He was going to shoot. He had to.

But the gun was heavy. Too heavy. It felt like it was made of lead. He couldn't lift it.

Dale fired.

A mist erupted from the nozzle.

It wasn't white. It was a deep, bruised purple. It hit Miller in the chest.

It didn't burn. It froze.

Miller gasped.

The air in his lungs turned to slush, and he couldn't breathe. He couldn't move.

He was a statue. A statue made of ice and terror.

Dale walked toward him. Step by step.

His boots crunched on the glass ants, and he didn't look at Miller's face. He looked at the bite mark.

He clicked his pen again.

"Symptoms: rapid necrosis, sensory distortion, motor control loss," Dale muttered. "Cause: *Crystalline Parasite, Sub-species Alpha*. Vector: Officer Miller."

Miller tried to scream. No sound came out.

Dale leaned in close. His breath smelled like antiseptic and old paper.

"You're not infected," Dale said.

"You're infested. There's a difference.

Infection is passive.

Infestation is active. You have a nest. In your blood.

"

Miller's eyes widened. He could feel them.

Tiny things.

Moving.

Under his skin.

Dale raised his clipboard. The translucent slab glowed brighter.

"Field Guide Update," Dale said. "Target identified. Weakness: High-frequency sonic resonance. Specific pitch: 440 Hz."

Dale reached into his pocket and pulled out a small, silver device. A tuning fork.

He struck it against his boot, and the sound was pure. Perfect. A single, clear note that cut through the rain, through the thunder, through the static in Miller's ears.

The glass ants on Miller's skin shattered.

Miller screamed then. A raw, ragged sound that tore at his throat. He fell to his knees, clutching his arm.

The black veins retreated. The cold faded.

The faces in the puddles vanished, and he looked up, gasping for air.

Dale stood over him. The tuning fork was silent. The glass ants were dust.

"Cleanup complete," Dale said. "Documentation filed. Thoroughness Rating: 10.0."

Miller scrambled to his feet, his legs shaking so badly he almost fell again. He pointed his gun at Dale. His hands were steady now. The adrenaline was back.

"You... you did that on purpose," Miller wheezed.

"I treated the problem," Dale said. "The problem was you. You were standing in the wrong place, wearing the wrong gear, and failing to identify the vector.

That's a liability.

I corrected it.

"

"You bit me!"

"You exposed yourself to the vector. I removed it. I saved your life. You're welcome."

Miller lowered the gun. He didn't know why. It felt wrong to shoot a man who had just saved him. But it also felt wrong to trust him.

Dale turned back to the street. The Rift was still open above. The thing with the clipboard was still there. Watching.

"Officer," Dale said. "Leave. Now."

"Where am I supposed to go?" Miller asked.

"Away from the Core. The Authority is summoning me. You're not invited."

"The Authority?"

"The Licensing Authority. They handle the paperwork. They handle the extermination. You're just noise."

Miller looked at the sky.

The thing with the clipboard was descending. Slowly. Its form was shifting.

It looked less like a monster and more like a bureaucrat. A large, angry bureaucrat.

"I'm staying," Miller said. "I have a duty."

Dale didn't argue, and he didn't even look at him.

He just walked to his truck. He opened the driver's side door.

He sat inside. He started the engine.

The truck roared. It sounded like a beast waking up.

Miller backed away. He didn't want to be near that truck. The hazard lights were blinking faster now. The orange pulse was frantic.

Dale looked out the window. His eyes were normal again.

Brown.

Tired. Human.

"Drive away," Dale said through the speaker system. "If you stay, you'll be logged as collateral damage. And I hate messy paperwork."

Miller got in his police car. He didn't start it. He just sat there, watching.

Dale drove.

The truck didn't drive down the street. It drove *into* the crater.

The asphalt parted.

The earth opened. The truck disappeared into the darkness below.

Miller's radio blared.

"4-Bravo! Status report! What happened to the suspect?"

Miller looked at the empty street. The rain was falling straight now. Normal rain.

"Target acquired," Miller whispered. "Or... released."

"Released to where?"

Miller looked at his wrist. The bite mark was gone.

But there was a new mark.

A small, black symbol.

A square, and he touched it. It was raised. Hard.

"Core," Miller said.

"Say again?"

"Core," Miller said. "He went to the Core."

The radio went silent.

Miller looked at the symbol on his wrist. It glowed faintly. A tiny, pulsing light.

*Status: Pending Review.*

Miller felt a chill run down his spine. Not the cold of the parasite. The cold of realization.

He wasn't just a witness. He was part of the file now.

And the file was incomplete.

Miller started his car. He didn't drive home. He drove toward the crater.

He had to know. He had to see.

As he approached the edge of the hole, his radio crackled one last time.

"4-Bravo, this is Central. We are detecting a new signal. It's coming from your location."

"Where?" Miller asked.

"It's coming from *inside* you."

Miller slammed on the brakes, and he looked at his wrist. The symbol was expanding. Growing.

Spreading up his arm.

And then, he heard it.

A voice. Not in his ear. In his mind.

*Inspection required.*

Miller looked at his hands. They were turning translucent.

He opened the door. He stepped out. He looked down at the crater.

The truck was there. Dale was standing next to it. He was holding a new clipboard.

And this time, the clipboard was covered in Miller's name.

Dale looked up. He smiled.

It wasn't a nice smile, which was a professional smile. The kind he gave to a client who didn't understand why their bill was so high.

"Time for your final assessment, Officer," Dale said.

Miller tried to run. But his legs were gone. His body was turning into glass.

He was becoming an entry in the Field Guide.

Dale clicked his pen.

"Next case," Dale said.
