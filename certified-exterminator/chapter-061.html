Chapter 61: Documentation or Death

# Chapter 61

Dale tested the deadbolt. His shoulder hit the door at the same angle he used on residential exterior units when a tenant claimed they'd locked up at night but the bolt was still in the catch position. The metal gave slightly. The frame didn't.

He stepped back. Looked at the hinges. Three-barrel, commercial grade, non-removable pin. Someone who knew what they were doing had locked him in.

He looked at the windows. Third floor.

The glass was laminated.

He'd know from the sound if he hit it, but he didn't need to hit it. He'd seen the frame construction from the inside. Security film on the exterior face.

Probably rated for forced entry, and he pulled out his clipboard. Checked the time. 4:51.

The Authority notification said twelve minutes.

That put the intervention team at 5:03. He had eleven minutes to stop being a target.

He walked back to the desk. The logbook was still open.

He stared at the entry.

His handwriting.

His name. His sign-off.

The date was correct. The time stamp was 4:47, which was when he'd actually finished documenting the basement. So the entry was accurate in every way except one.

He hadn't written it, and he pulled out his phone.

Checked his Field Guide. The entry for the filing mimic was there, but the corruption marker pulsed in the corner like a warning light on a piece of equipment. The Authority had flagged his guide.

That meant any identification he made using that guide would carry a 40% debuff.

It also meant the Authority considered his knowledge base unreliable.

Which was exactly what the mimic wanted.

Dale sat down in the chair behind the desk. He put his clipboard on his lap. He started writing.

Not the logbook.

Not the Field Guide. He wrote on a fresh page. A new document.

Unconnected to anything the Authority had already flagged, and he wrote: Observation.

The logbook entry at 4:47 matches my physical presence at the basement level but does not match my physical presence at this desk.

I was not at this desk at 4:47. I was below.

The entry was created in my absence, and he underlined that. Then he wrote: Chain of custody violation, and the document was created without technician presence, without technician authorization, and without technician knowledge. Under Authority Protocol 7.4, a document lacking chain of custody is inadmissible as evidence of technician compromise.

He paused. Protocol 7.4 was for evidence handling during infestation surveys.

It was meant for scenarios where a technician collected a sample and the chain of custody was broken during transport.

It wasn't meant for this. But the language was broad enough. A document lacking chain of custody is inadmissible as evidence.

The threat assessment was built on that document.

If the document was inadmissible, the threat assessment had no basis.

He needed to file the violation. But the Authority notification had already been issued. He couldn't unsend it. He could only contest it.

He flipped to the back of his clipboard.

Found the blank forms he always carried. Filed copies. Three-part carbonless paper.

He'd had them since his first job at twenty-four, and the Authority had never updated the filing system to account for paper forms.

It still accepted them, and he wrote the violation form.

Date. Time.

Location. Document in question. Nature of violation.

He checked the box for Chain of Custody Breach. He wrote his name.

His certification number.

His license class, and he signed it.

Then he looked at the logbook again.

The entry was still there. Still his handwriting.

Still his name.

And now he'd filed a form that said it wasn't his. Both documents existed. Both were in the system.

The Authority would have to reconcile them.

The Authority didn't reconcile things quickly, which was a bureaucracy.

It had been a bureaucracy since before humans invented bureaucracy. It had rules for rules.

It had procedures for procedures. And it had a backlog.

Dale checked his watch. 4:56. Seven minutes.

He needed to be outside when the team arrived. He needed to be visible. He had to hold the violation form so it was instantly legible. He needed to look like a technician who had identified a procedural error, not a compromised asset.

He walked to the windows. The security film meant he couldn't break through quickly.

He needed a tool, and he looked around the office. A filing cabinet. A stapler.

A pair of scissors on the desk.

He took the scissors. He went to the window.

He cut the film in a four-inch square, then peeled it back.

The glass underneath was standard tempered. He hit it with the filing cabinet's metal leg. Once.

Twice. Three times.

The glass spider-webbed but held, and he hit it again. Harder. The glass gave.

He cleared the shards with his boot.

Stepped onto the sill. Looked down.

Third floor. The ground was grass. A dumpster sat four feet from the wall.

He dropped, and he landed on the dumpster's lid. It dented.

He slid off and hit the grass on the other side.

His left ankle twisted. He ignored it. He stood up.

He was outside, and he was visible. He was holding the clipboard.

He walked to the front of the building. He stood under the streetlight. He held the clipboard at chest height, form facing outward.

He waited.

At 5:01, a black van pulled up.

Two doors opened. Three people got out. They wore Authority-issue coveralls, the kind with the reflective certification stripes on the sleeves.

The lead technician was tall. Female.

Her coveralls read CLASS IV on the chest, and she had a case in her hand.

Not a clipboard. A case.

She looked at Dale. She looked at the building. She looked at the broken window on the third floor.

"Dale Kowalski?" she said.

"That's me."

"Show me your Field Guide."

He held up the clipboard. The Field Guide was digital, projected from his phone, but the physical clipboard had the violation form clipped to it. He held both up.

She walked toward him. She looked at the form. She looked at his phone. She looked at the building.

"You filed a chain of custody violation," she said.

"I did."

"On yourself."

"On the document that generated the threat assessment. I didn't write the logbook entry. Something else did. That's a chain of custody breach."

She studied the form. She pulled a scanner from her belt. Scanned the form.

The scanner beeped. She frowned.

"The Authority accepted your filing," she said.

"Of course it did. It's a valid form. I filled it out correctly."

"The Authority accepted it, but it hasn't processed it. Your threat assessment is still active until the filing is adjudicated."

"How long does adjudication take?"

She checked her scanner. "Standard queue is four to six weeks."

"Then I'm still a target."

"You're still a target."

Dale looked at the building.

The basement door. The filing system. Forty-nine thousand feeding points.

A mimic that didn't eat people.

It ate documentation. It ate records.

It ate the paper trail that connected a technician to their work.

And it had eaten his name.

"Can you confirm the threat assessment is based on the logbook entry?" he asked.

"It's based on the logbook entry, yes."

"Then if the logbook entry is inadmissible, the threat assessment should be void."

"That's not how it works. The assessment is already issued. You need the adjudication."

"Can you expedite?"

"No."

Dale looked at the van. The case the technician was carrying.

He knew what was in it.

He'd seen the Class IV protocol list in the Authority reference manual he'd memorized during his exam prep.

Containment unit. Neutralization spray.

A restraint harness rated for dimensional entities.

"You're here to contain me," he said.

"I'm here to contain the threat."

"The threat is the mimic. Not me."

"The Authority's assessment says the threat is you."

"The Authority's assessment is based on a document that was created without my presence. I've filed the violation. The violation is valid. You know it's valid."

The technician looked at her scanner again. She looked at Dale. She looked at the building.

"I'll give you a window," she said. "I can hold containment for twenty minutes while I file a secondary review. But if the mimic is still inside that building, and it's still generating documents under your name, the secondary review will just confirm the original assessment."

"Then I need to go back inside."

She looked at him. "You just came out of there."

"I know."

"You're not licensed for Class IV containment. You're Class III."

"I'm not going in to contain it. I'm going in to document it. There's a difference."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she opened the case.

She pulled out a small device.

A recorder. Authority-issued. She handed it to him.

"Voice-activated.

Continuous. If you're inside the building and you speak, it records.

If you're compromised, the recording will show it.

If you're not, it'll show that too."

Dale took the recorder. He clipped it to his shirt.

"Twenty minutes," she said.

"Twenty minutes."

He walked back toward the building. His ankle ached. The grass was wet. The streetlight buzzed.

He went through the side door. The one he'd used to enter the building originally.

It was unlocked, and he went down the stairs.

First floor. Basement door.

The door was closed. The lock was engaged.

He put his hand on the handle. He turned it. It turned.

The lock disengaged. He hadn't touched it.

He went down, and the basement was dark.

He turned on his flashlight. The filing cabinets were where he'd left them. The drawers were open.

The documentation was everywhere, and he walked to the far wall. The cabinet he'd photographed last. The one with the most feeding points. He opened the drawer.

Empty.

He opened the next one. Empty.

Every drawer he'd documented was now empty. Forty-nine thousand feeding points. Gone.

He looked at the floor. The frass.

The droppings.

The evidence trail that every pest control technician knew to look for. It was still there. The mimic had moved.

It had taken its documentation with it, and he looked at the wall.

The spot where the logbook entry had been created.

A mark was present. A smudge.

Not on paper. On the concrete. Like something had pressed a document against the wall, leaving an impression.

He took out his phone. Photographed it.

The Authority notification came.

FIELD GUIDE CORRUPTION DETECTED. CAUSE: RECURSIVE DOCUMENTATION LOOP.

THREAT ASSESSMENT: TECHNICIAN IS COMPROMISED.

DISPATCHING CLASS IV INTERVENTION TEAM. ETA: 12 MINUTES.

He looked at the timestamp. 5:04, and the team was already outside.

He was already inside. The notification said they were being dispatched.

Again.

As though the first dispatch never occurred. As though time looped.

He looked at the recorder on his shirt. It was still running. He spoke into it.

"This is Dale Kowalski. Certification Level III.

I am in the basement of the municipal records building, and the infestation has relocated.

The documentation it feeds on has been consumed. I am documenting the absence of documentation.

This is my fourth entry today. I have not been compromised. I am not the vector.

I am the technician."

The recorder beeped. Stored.

He looked at the wall.

The smudge. He leaned closer. The impression in the concrete was a document.

He could read it, which was his Field Guide entry. The one for the filing mimic.

The entry he'd written himself.

But the text was different. The weakness he'd identified was wrong. The weakness he'd written down was a lie.

He'd written it down himself. He remembered writing it. But the words on the wall said something else. Something that made the mimic stronger instead of weaker.

He'd been feeding it. Not the mimic.

Him.

His own documentation.

His own Field Guide. He'd been writing the mimic's strength into his own notes for weeks.

The recorder beeped. A new notification.

FIELD GUIDE CORRUPTION: SEVERITY UPGRADED. CAUSE: SELF-GENERATED CONTAMINATION. TECHNICIAN HAS BEEN FEEDING THE INFESTATION SINCE CERTIFICATION LEVEL II. ALL FIELD GUIDE ENTRIES POST-LEVEL-II ARE CONSIDERED CONTAMINATED.

He looked at his phone. His Field Guide. Dozens of entries.

Dozens of weaknesses he'd identified. Dozens of targeted damage bonuses he'd used in the field.

All of them wrong. All of them feeding the mimic.

The stairs above him. Footsteps. The Class IV team. They were coming down.

He looked at his clipboard. He looked at the wall. He looked at the recorder.

He had one choice.

Destroy his Field Guide. All of it. Every entry since Level II.

Every identification.

Every weakness. Every protocol he'd ever used.

Or keep it. And become the mimic's documentation.

The footsteps reached the basement door. The light from above hit his face.

He looked at the clipboard, and he looked at the stairs.

He made his choice.
