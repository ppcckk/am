Chapter 283: Twelve Flights of Static

# Chapter 283

The traffic light flickered, not with electricity, but with static.

Officer Miller watched the man in the soiled khaki uniform stop at the crosswalk. He didn't look at the officer. He didn't look at the sky, which had turned the color of bruised plums. He looked at his own hand, where a black marker was currently carving into his skin.

Miller lowered his clipboard. The paper felt heavy, damp. Not from rain, but from a sudden drop in atmospheric pressure that made his ears pop.

"Hey!" Miller called out, his voice cracking. "Sir, you can't be on the roadway. Civilian safety protocol requires—"

The man in the khakis didn't hear him. Or didn't care. Miller stepped forward, hand resting on the holster at his hip, a gesture that felt absurd given that his pistol was currently vibrating with a low, mournful hum.

The cars in the intersection weren't moving. They were arranged.

A sedan, a delivery truck, and a police cruiser formed a perfect equilateral triangle around the man.

Their headlights were on, but the beams didn't cast light. They projected shadows that moved independently of the objects casting them.

Miller squinted. The shadows were wrong. They stretched toward the man, not away from the sun, which was currently obscured by a swirling vortex of geometric shapes.

The man raised his hand. The marker tip touched his forearm.

Miller shouted again, closer this time.

"Sir! Step away from the intersection!"

The man ignored the command. He ignored the reality distortion field rippling through the asphalt. He ignored the way the delivery truck's grille was turning into a mouth, teeth forming from the chrome radiator bars.

He was writing.

Miller froze. The man wasn't scribbling. He was filling out a form. On his own skin.

The officer felt a cold prickle at the base of his neck. He had seen this before, in the files he wasn't supposed to read. The old cases. The ones where the paperwork came first, and the monster came second.

The man in the khakis finished the first line. The air around him snapped, a sound like a dry branch breaking.

**SYSTEM ALERT: LOCAL STATUTE 4.1.2 (NO LOITERING IN TRANSIT ZONES) CITED.** **STATUS: VALID.** **REALITY COHERENCE: 12% RESTORED IN LOCAL SECTOR.**

Miller blinked. The words hung in the air, projected in a stark, bureaucratic font that looked like it had been printed on a receipt.

The man looked up. His eyes were bloodshot, dark circles carved deep into his skin. He looked exhausted. He looked like a man who had been working a double shift for a week.

"Who are you?" Miller asked, his voice smaller now.

The man didn't answer immediately. He uncapped the marker again. The cap rolled across the asphalt, hitting the bumper of the police cruiser.

"Citation," the man said.

His voice was flat, devoid of fear or awe.

"You're obstructing a documented infestation site.

That's a violation of Municipal Code 88-B.

I'm clearing the workspace.

"

Miller looked at the cars. The delivery truck's mouth was closing, the chrome teeth grinding together with the sound of metal shearing. The man walked toward it.

Miller followed, instinct overriding training.

"You can't just walk into that.

It's a Class-IV Reality Breach.

We need the National Guard.

We need—"

"We need a permit," the man interrupted. He didn't turn around. "And you don't have one.

That's why it's getting worse. You're treating a structural violation like a combat scenario.

You're trying to shoot the mold instead of fixing the leak.

"

Miller stopped. The logic was insane. It was also, uncomfortably, true.

The chaos in the square wasn't random, which was reactive. The more they tried to 'fix' it with force, the more the laws of physics seemed to recoil, creating gaps where monsters poured in.

The man reached the front of the delivery truck. The vehicle shuddered. A limb, made of twisted rebar and wet concrete, punched through the windshield.

Miller raised his gun.

The man slapped the gun barrel aside. Hard.

"Put that away," the technician said.

"You're contaminating the field. If you fire now, you'll create a kinetic paradox. The Authority doesn't rate violence.

They rate compliance.

"

"Compliance with what?" Miller yelled, the panic finally rising in his throat.

"With reality," the technician said. He pulled a small, yellow notepad from his pocket. It looked like standard issue from a hardware store. He tore off a sheet.

He held it up.

"Sign here," he said, pointing to the bottom of the page.

Miller stared at the paper. The text on it was moving, rearranging itself as he looked at it.

**WAIVER OF LIABILITY FOR UNLICENSED REALITY MANIPULATION.** **SIGNATORY: OFFICER J. MILLER.** **ACKNOWLEDGMENT: CITIZEN KOWALSKI, DALE, CLASS II LICENSING.**

"I'm not signing that," Miller said.

"Then you're obstructing a licensed professional," the technician said. He turned back to the truck. The concrete arm swiped at him.

The technician didn't dodge. He sidestepped, a precise, economical movement. He stepped into the blind spot of the limb. From his pocket, he pulled out a roll of heavy-duty duct tape.

He didn't attack the monster. He attacked the ground beneath it.

He slapped the tape onto the asphalt, right where the truck's wheels were fused to the road. The truck shrieked, a sound of grinding gears and screaming metal. The limb froze mid-swing.

**SYSTEM ALERT: PHYSICAL ANCHORING PROTOCOL INITIATED.** **TARGET: VEHICLE CHASSIS.** **METHOD: NON-PERMANENT FIXATION.** **STATUS: STABILIZED.**

The green hue of the sky flickered. For a second, it turned blue. Then back to green.

Miller watched, mouth agape. The technician was taping the ground. He was using hardware store supplies to fight a dimensional rift.

"It's not magic," the technician muttered, more to himself than to Miller. "It's just code. And code has bugs. You just have to patch them."

He moved to the police cruiser. He pulled out a marker. He drew a circle on the hood of the car. Inside the circle, he wrote a number. *14.*

The car's engine died. The lights went out. The car became inert metal.

Miller rushed forward.

"What did you do?"

"Grounded the electrical charge," the technician said.

"The rift is feeding on the ambient electromagnetic noise.

Your radios, your phones, your sirens. It's all fuel.

You're feeding the fire with your equipment.

"

He looked at Miller.

"Turn it off. All of it. Now."

Miller hesitated.

"But the backup—"

"Will cause a cascade failure," the technician said. "Do it."

Miller pulled his radio. He switched off his flashlight. He signaled the other officers, who were frozen in confusion, to power down their gear.

The silence was deafening.

Without the hum of electronics, the air in the square seemed to thin. The geometric shapes in the sky slowed their rotation. The cars stopped vibrating.

The technician went back to the delivery truck. He climbed onto the hood. He opened the glove compartment.

Miller expected a weapon. Or a bomb.

The technician pulled out a clipboard, and he held it up to the monster. The concrete arm froze again. The chrome teeth stopped grinding.

**SYSTEM ALERT: DOCUMENTATION SUBMISSION REQUIRED.** **ENTRY: VEHICLE INFESTATION (MODEL: 2018 CHEVY EXPRESS, MODIFIED BY RIFT).** **ACTION: FILE COMPLAINT.**

Miller watched in horror as the technician wrote. He was filling out a repair order.

*Make: Chevrolet.* *Model: Express.* *Symptoms: Sentient chassis, aggressive behavior, unauthorized dimensional integration.* *Diagnosis: Contaminated by non-local entities.* *Recommended Treatment: Immediate decommissioning and hazardous waste disposal.*

The technician signed the bottom of the form. He ripped the carbon copy off and slapped it onto the truck's windshield.

The truck groaned. The concrete arm retracted.

The chrome teeth dissolved into rust.

The vehicle slumped, becoming just a car again. A dead, broken car.

The technician hopped down. He looked at Miller.

"You're good now," he said.

"For about twenty minutes. The Authority will review the citation.

If they reject it, the rift reopens.

If they accept it, we move to the next violation.

"

Miller slumped against his own cruiser. He felt dizzy.

"Who are you?"

"I'm the exterminator," the technician said. He looked at his hand, where the marker had carved the laws of reality into his flesh. The ink was fading, but the scars remained. "And we're way behind schedule."

He walked away, toward City Hall. Toward the center of the glitch.

Miller looked at his own hands. They were shaking. He looked at the radio in his hand. The static was gone.

He looked at the technician's back. The man was walking with a limp. His left leg seemed shorter than his right. Or maybe the ground was tilting under him.

Miller followed. He had no choice. The system had accepted the technician's authority, even if Miller didn't understand it.

They passed the fountain in the center of the square. The water was frozen, suspended in mid-air. Droplets hung like glass beads.

The technician stopped. He looked up.

A woman stood on the roof of City Hall. She was holding a microphone. She was singing.

The song wasn't a song. It was a series of tones that made Miller's teeth ache. The air rippled around her.

**SYSTEM ALERT: VOCAL INFESTATION DETECTED.** **SEVERITY: CLASS V.** **PROTOCOL: SILENCE ORDER.**

The technician sighed. He pulled a pair of industrial earmuffs from his belt. He put them on.

He looked at Miller. He pointed to his own ears.

Miller put his hands over his ears.

The technician walked toward City Hall. He didn't run. He didn't charge. He walked with the steady, weary pace of a man going to work.

He reached the doors. They were locked.

He pulled out his marker. He wrote on the door.

*Notice: Maintenance Required. Do Not Enter.*

The lock clicked. The doors swung open.

Inside, the lobby was dark. But there was light coming from the stairwell. A blue, pulsing light.

The technician stepped inside.

Miller stayed at the threshold. He couldn't go in. The air inside felt thick, like syrup.

The technician turned back. He looked at Miller. He held out his hand.

"Come on," he mouthed.

Miller shook his head.

The technician shrugged. He went up the stairs.

Miller watched him go. The man disappeared into the blue light.

Then, the System Alert flashed in Miller's vision, bright and red.

**SYSTEM ALERT: CITATION INCOMPLETE.** **ERROR: LACK OF WITNESS SIGNATURE.** **PENALTY: LOCAL REALITY ERASURE IN T-MINUS 60 SECONDS.**

Miller looked at the door. He looked at the sky, which was beginning to crack. Shards of glass-like reality were falling from the clouds.

He had to sign. He had to go inside.

He looked at his radio. It was off.

He looked at his gun. It was useless.

He looked at the door, and he took a breath. He stepped forward.

The threshold felt cold.

As soon as his foot crossed the line, the air changed. The syrupy thickness vanished. The blue light intensified.

He saw the technician halfway up the stairs. The technician was writing on the wall.

Miller realized then what he had to do, and he pulled out his notebook. He had carried it for years. It was empty.

He uncapped his pen.

He looked at the wall. The technician had written a single word.

*LISTEN.*

Miller listened, and he heard the singing. But he also heard something else. A rhythm. A pattern.

The singing was a code. A transmission.

The technician turned. He smiled. It was a grim, tight smile.

"Now you're getting it," the technician said.

He pointed to the next floor up.

"Write it down," he said. "All of it. Before the chorus hits."

Miller looked at the stairs. There were twelve flights.

He looked at his pen, and he climbed.
