Chapter 28

The corn was dead in a spiral.

Dale saw it from the truck bed. Webb had hot-wired a flatbed from a farm equipment lot two miles back, and Dale had ridden standing up because sitting meant thinking, and thinking meant the countdown. Four hours, eleven minutes. He'd stopped watching it. The numbers didn't change whether he looked or not.

The spiral was wrong. Not the kind of wrong that meant disease or weather. The kind of wrong that meant something had been feeding from the center outward, leaving a ring of dead stalks that expanded in a perfect circle. Dale had seen this pattern before. Not in corn. In drywall. When a powderpost beetle colony expands, it eats outward from the nest in concentric rings because the outermost larvae are newest and hungriest.

"Pull over," Dale said.

Webb killed the engine. The flatbed rolled to a stop at the edge of the field. Dale jumped down. His boots hit gravel, then dirt, then the first dead stalks. He walked to the nearest one and pulled it free. The root system was gone. Not chewed. Dissolved. A black residue coated the remaining stub, and it smelled like ammonia mixed with something organic and old.

He crouched. Poked the residue with his gloved finger. It was still wet.

"Fresh," he said. "Under an hour."

Webb climbed down. "From what?"

Dale didn't answer. He walked the spiral's outer edge, counting. Twenty-two dead stalks in the first ring. Forty-one in the second. The ratio was wrong for powderpost beetles. Too many in the outer ring. That meant the feeding wasn't expanding. It was contracting. Something was pulling inward.

He found the entry point on the third ring. A hole in the soil, six inches wide, edges smooth and slightly charred. He dropped to his knees and shined his flashlight into it. The beam hit a wall of black chitin three feet down. Not a creature. A structure. A tunnel lining.

"Someone's already been here," Dale said.

He stood and scanned the field. Kill marks. He could see them now that he knew where to look. Scorch patterns where a protocol had been deployed. Three of them, roughly equidistant from the center. Each one had a dead organism at its base. Dale walked to the nearest and identified it from his Field Guide without needing to consult the entry. Class-1 dimensional arthropod. A worker caste. Maybe two feet long, six legs, mandibles designed for boring through calcium-rich material.

He turned it over. The kill was clean. The protocol had targeted the thorax, which was the correct weak point for this species. But the head was intact. The brain case was intact. And the abdomen was intact, which meant the egg sac was intact.

"Amateur," Dale said. Not cruel. Clinical.

He walked to the second kill mark. Same thing. Correct thoracic strike, no decapitation, no abdominal destruction. The worker was dead, but its reproductive organs were preserved. If any of those eggs were viable, they'd hatch in six to eight weeks. And the parent organism, which was clearly the thing that had built the tunnel lining below, was still alive. Still feeding. Still expanding.

The third kill mark was different. No scorch pattern. No protocol residue. Just a dead organism with a clean cut through the neck. Someone had used a blade.

Dale knelt beside it. The cut was precise. Surgical, almost. Whoever had done this had anatomical knowledge. They'd aimed for the cervical ganglion, which would kill the organism instantly. But they'd left the rest of the body. The legs were still twitching. The spiracles were still cycling.

"Post-mortem neural activity," Dale said. "The peripheral nervous system keeps firing for about ninety seconds after central death. You'd know that if you'd ever worked with cockroaches."

He stood. Looked at the spiral again. The contracting pattern made sense now. The parent organism was pulling its workers inward because the outer perimeter was being killed. It was retreating. Consolidating. Protecting its core.

And it was doing it fast.

"Webb. How long since we left the storm drain?"

"Maybe two hours."

"Then this parent is moving underground toward the coordinates. It's not hiding. It's fleeing toward something." Dale pulled out his clipboard. Started writing. "The kill marks are all within a quarter mile of the center. Whoever did this was working the outer ring. They didn't go inward. They didn't find the nest."

"Because they were killing, not treating," Webb said.

"Because they don't know the difference." Dale tore the page out. Handed it to Webb. "Field Guide entry: Dimensional Arthropod, worker caste. Cervical ganglion kill confirmed. Thoracic protocol kill confirmed. Reproductive organs not destroyed in either case. Nest structure identified at depth of three feet. Parent organism active, contracting pattern, moving northeast."

Webb took the paper. "You're documenting this for the Authority?"

"I'm documenting this for whoever comes next. The audit doesn't care about the audit. The audit cares about the infestation. That's how this system works. You can't audit a job that hasn't been completed."

He was still writing when the notification appeared. Not the countdown. Something else.

MONITORING AGENT DEPLOYED. AUDIT PROTOCOL ACTIVE. OBSERVE.

Dale looked up. The air above the field shimmered. Not heat haze. Something else. A distortion that moved with purpose. He tracked it with his eyes. It was small. Maybe the size of a fist. Dark. It hovered above the dead corn at head height, drifting in a slow circle.

It had eight legs. They were thin, translucent, and they moved independently of each other. The body was segmented, tick-like, with a single large eye on the dorsal surface. The eye was scanning. He could see the iris rotating, catching light from different angles.

Dale didn't move. Didn't breathe. He watched it circle the kill marks. When it reached the first one, it stopped. Its legs retracted. The eye focused on the dead worker. A thin filament extended from the creature's ventral side and touched the thoracic wound. It withdrew the filament. Moved to the next kill mark.

It was inspecting the kills. Assessing them. Rating them.

Dale opened his Field Guide. Flipped to a blank page. Started writing.

*Unidentified organism. Eight legs, translucent, segmented body, single dorsal eye. Size: approximately 12 cm. Behavior: approaches kill sites, extends ventral filament to contact wound tissue, withdraws. No feeding behavior observed. No locomotion toward nest structure. Appears to assess kill quality.*

*Hypothesis: Authority monitoring agent. Function: audit enforcement. Method: physical inspection of completed kills.*

He underlined the last line.

The notification pulsed.

DOCUMENTATION IN PROGRESS. FIELD GUIDE ENTRY PENDING CONFIRMATION.

Dale kept writing. He'd done this fourteen years. You don't confirm an identification until you've ruled out look-alikes. He described the leg joint structure. The eye placement relative to the body axis. The filament length and insertion point. He noted the absence of mouthparts. The absence of spiracles. The absence of anything that would indicate this was a living organism in the biological sense.

It was a tool. Not a creature. The Authority had built it.

He finished the entry. Wrote the species name he'd assigned: *Observatio auctoris*. Authority's Eye.

The notification changed.

FIELD GUIDE ENTRY CONFIRMED. NEW SPECIES LOGGED. NO TREATMENT POINTS AWARDED. NO WEAKNESS IDENTIFIED. NO COMBAT APPLICATION.

That made sense. It wasn't a threat. It was a measuring instrument. You don't exterminate a thermometer.

But Dale wasn't done. He started a new entry. A violation report.

*Unlicensed biological monitoring agent deployed within active service area. Agent not registered in Field Guide prior to deployment. Agent not disclosed to licensed technician. Agent interacts with kill sites without technician authorization. Agent modifies audit conditions without consent.*

*Classification: UNREGISTERED MONITORING DEVICE. Violation Code: M-11. Unauthorized introduction of biological agent into treated zone.*

He held the clipboard up. The notification pulsed.

VIOLATION REPORT FILED. PENDING REVIEW.

The monitoring agent stopped moving. Its eye turned. Not toward Dale. Toward the kill marks. Specifically, toward the blade cut. The one that wasn't a protocol kill.

It moved to that kill mark. Extended its filament. Touched the cervical cut. Withdrew. Then it did something new. It opened its ventral segment and released a second filament. Thinner. Longer. It threaded the filament into the wound and began probing deeper. Into the egg sac.

Dale watched it extract a cluster of translucent eggs. The eggs were viable. Alive. The agent held them in its filament and carried them toward the tunnel entrance. Toward the parent organism.

It wasn't auditing the kills. It was auditing the infestation. And it was feeding the nest.

"That's not an audit," Dale said. "That's a stress test."

He looked at the countdown. Three hours, forty-two minutes.

Then he looked at the tunnel entrance. The parent organism was still down there. Still contracting. Still consolidating. And now the Authority was feeding it. Deliberately. Because a technician who could handle a stressed nest would score higher on the Thoroughness Scale than one who could only handle a complacent one.

Dale understood. He'd seen this in municipal work. The city would seed a building with cockroach eggs before an inspection to test whether the contractor had actually treated the problem or just killed what was visible. It was a trap. A legitimate, legal, documented trap.

The Authority was running a trap on him.

"Webb." Dale's voice was flat. "Get the truck."

Webb moved. Dale didn't. He pulled out his spray can. Not the one from the duplex. A different one. He'd been carrying it since Chapter 12, when he'd found it in an abandoned hardware store. It wasn't pesticide. It was a Class-3 treatment compound the Authority had given him as a certification reward. It was designed for fungal organisms. It would do nothing to a dimensional arthropod.

He sprayed it into the tunnel entrance anyway.

The notification appeared immediately.

CROSS-CONTAMINATION WARNING. PROTOCOL MISMATCH. 40% DEBUFF APPLIED. FIELD GUIDE ENTRY CONTAMINATED FOR 24 HOURS.

He'd known that would happen. He didn't care. The point wasn't the treatment. The point was the contamination. The Field Guide entry for the parent organism was now contaminated. That meant the Authority couldn't use it. Couldn't reference it. Couldn't audit against it.

The countdown changed. Three hours, forty-two minutes became four hours, fifteen minutes.

The audit timer had increased.

Dale stared at the new number. He hadn't expected that. He'd expected the contamination to pause the audit. Not extend it.

Then the notification appeared.

AUDIT PARAMETERS UPDATED. CONTAMINATED ENTRY REMOVED FROM SCOPE. REMAINING SCOPE: MONITORING AGENT VIOLATION. TIME EXTENDED TO PERMIT INVESTIGATION.

He hadn't just slowed the audit. He'd redirected it. The Authority was now investigating its own violation. The monitoring agent was now the subject. The infestation was secondary.

Dale allowed himself one breath. Then he looked at the tunnel. The parent organism was still there. Still feeding. Still building toward something at the coordinates.

He hadn't solved the problem. He'd just bought time.

"Webb. Drive."

The flatbed lurched. Dale climbed in. They headed northeast. The corn gave way to soybeans, then to bare dirt, then to a stretch of cracked asphalt that hadn't been repaved since the Rift. The road narrowed. The sky ahead changed color. Not the gray of storm clouds. A deeper shade. The kind of gray that meant the air itself was denser.

Webb slowed. "That's the coordinates."

Dale looked. The road ended at a fence. Beyond the fence, the ground was black. Not scorched. Black. Like something had been pulled out of the earth and the earth hadn't healed. The soil was raw and wet and steaming faintly in the cold air.

And standing at the fence was a woman. She wore a hard hat with a Certification Level IV badge. She held a clipboard. She was writing.

She looked up when they approached. Her eyes went to Dale's clipboard. Then to his truck. Then to his face.

"You're the relay," she said. Not a question.

Dale stepped off the truck. "You're the one who made the kill marks."

"I'm the one who made the kill marks." She held up her clipboard. Her Field Guide entry was visible. The same species. The same worker caste. Her documentation was thin. One line per kill. No nest assessment. No reproductive analysis.

"You left the egg sacs intact," Dale said.

"I killed them. They're dead."

"The organisms are dead. The eggs are not. You treated the symptom. You didn't treat the infestation."

She looked at him the way a person looks at a code inspector who's just walked into their restaurant. "And you're going to lecture me about Thoroughness Ratings?"

"I'm going to show you what's under that fence." Dale pointed at the black soil. "The parent organism is contracting. It's moving toward the coordinates. You killed its outer perimeter and it retreated. Now it's consolidating. You made it stronger."

Her expression changed. Not defensive. Calculating. She looked at the black ground. Then at her clipboard. Then at Dale.

"How long do we have?" she asked.

"Three hours, fifty minutes."

She uncapped her pen. Started a new entry. Dale watched her write. She was documenting. Actually documenting. Not just listing kills. She was writing the nest assessment. The reproductive analysis. The contraction pattern.

She finished. Tore the page. Handed it to him.

"File it under my name," she said. "I don't have Authority interface access. I'm Level IV. I don't get the notifications."

Dale looked at her. Level IV. She'd been working this territory for weeks without the System's feedback loop. Without the countdown. Without the audit. She'd been doing it blind.

"Why?" he asked.

"Because the Rift started here. Because the Authority won't send anyone else. And because if I stop killing them, they reach the town." She looked at the black soil. "I've been here nine days."

Dale looked at the fence. At the black ground. At the steam rising from it. The countdown ticked down.

Then his Field Guide pulsed. A new entry. Not one he'd written. Not one the Authority had confirmed.

A new species. Populated from the monitoring agent's own documentation. The agent was feeding the Authority's database. And what it had logged was not a creature.

It was a location.

NEXUS. Dimensional anchor point. Status: ACTIVE. Classification: INFESTATION SOURCE. Thoroughness required: 10.0.

Dale read it twice. The Authority had never given him a Thoroughness requirement above 9.2. He'd never seen 10.0. It wasn't a rating scale. It was a threshold. And it was for the NEXUS itself.

He looked at the woman. "What's your name?"

"Voss."

"Voss. The Authority is rating the Rift. Not the creatures. The Rift itself. And it wants a ten."

She stared at him. "That's not possible. The Thoroughness Scale tops out at ten. Nobody's ever hit ten."

Dale looked at the black ground. At the steam. At the fence. At the place where the world had been pulled open and never closed.

"No," he said. "Nobody's ever tried to document it."

He walked toward the fence. The countdown read three hours, forty-seven minutes. His Field Guide was open. His clipboard was ready. The Authority's monitoring agent hovered above his shoulder, watching.

He reached the fence. Looked down at the black soil. It was moving. Not settling. Moving. Like something beneath it was breathing.

He started writing.
