Chapter 280: Slick with Intent

# Chapter 280

The water was cold. That was the first distinct fact Dale registered, overriding the panic that had seized his throat. The second was the pressure. It pressed against his chest, compressing his lungs, forcing the air from his nostrils in a thin, useless stream of bubbles.

Darkness swallowed everything. No light. No reference points. Just the heavy, stagnant weight of the flooded substation and the rising tide of something that was not water.

Dale did not struggle. Struggling wasted oxygen.

In a confined space with limited visibility, movement created turbulence, and turbulence stirred up the debris of the nest.

He knew this from the crawlspace inspections of his youth, the ones where the mud sucked at your ankles and the air tasted like old copper. You moved like a surgeon. You moved with intention.

His gloved hand found the breaker box, and the plastic casing was slick, coated in a film of slime that smelled of ozone and rotting kelp. He didn't look for the switch.

He knew where it was.

He had memorized the layout of the panel during the initial inspection, before the swarm descended, before the Authority's notification blared in his peripheral vision.

The switch was down.

He had pulled it.

Now, the silence was absolute. The pumps had stopped. The grid was dead. The Authority's hum, that constant, low-frequency vibration that had lived in the back of his mind for months, was gone.

A new sound emerged. A slithering. Wet, heavy, and deliberate. It came from the floor, from the walls, from the pipes that lined the concrete.

Dale kept his hand on the breaker. His fingers traced the label. *MAIN FEED.*

The Authority had lied. Or perhaps it had simply omitted the context.

Cutting the power didn't kill the entity.

It freed it.

Without the grid's electrical containment field, the parasitic system could expand unchecked. The

"catastrophic failure" warning hadn't been a threat to the infrastructure.

It had been a warning to Dale, and he had failed the practical exam. He had triggered a systemic collapse.

A cold realization settled in his gut, heavier than the water. The Authority wanted him to fail. It wanted him to see the cost of independence. It wanted him to beg for the system to return.

But Dale didn't beg. He checked his watch.

The screen was dark, but he knew the time by the rhythm of his own pulse.

Four minutes since the switch. Four minutes of darkness.

The slithering stopped.

Then, a voice. Not in his ears. In his head. It was dry, papery, like old parchment rubbing against bone.

**"VIOLATION DETECTED: UNAUTHORIZED TERMINATION OF CONTAINMENT PROTOCOL. PENALTY: REVOCATION OF CERTIFICATION LEVEL IV. FIELD GUIDE LOCKED."**

Dale's breath hitched. His Field Guide locked. The data he had spent months compiling, the weaknesses he had documented, the behavioral patterns of the Rift-born creatures, all of it was inaccessible. The blue interface that usually hovered over his vision was grayed out, static.

He was blind. Not just visually, but informationally. He was back to being a man with a flashlight and a can of spray, fighting in the dark.

But he still had his memory, and he still knew that *Rattus profundus*, the dimensional vermin, navigated by heat and vibration.

He still knew that the Authority's entity, which he had come to call *The Auditor*, was a soft-bodied organism that required high-voltage electricity to maintain its structural integrity. Without the current, it was vulnerable.

It was fluid.

It was vulnerable to physical disruption.

The slithering started again. Closer. It was moving up his leg.

Dale didn't try to kick. Kicking in water was ineffective; it created a vacuum that pulled the attacker closer.

Instead, he reached into his belt pouch.

His fingers brushed against the cold metal of his utility knife, which was a small tool, meant for cutting tape and scraping putty, not for fighting monsters. But Dale was a technician.

He understood leverage. He understood weak points.

The thing wrapped around his calf. It was warm, pulsing. It felt less like flesh and more like wet silk.

He twisted his body, turning away from the breaker box. If he stayed here, he was trapped. He needed to move. He needed to get to the ladder.

He kicked out, not to break the limb, but to break the suction. The seal popped with a wet *schlick*. The water churned.

His boots found the bottom of the ladder. He pulled himself up, hand over hand, muscles screaming in protest.

The water rose with him, clinging to his legs, dragging him down, and the weight was immense. It felt like wearing a suit of lead armor.

At the top, the hatch was open. A shaft of moonlight cut through the darkness. It was faint, filtered through the rain and the broken concrete, but it was there.

Dale hauled himself onto the concrete floor of the substation yard. He rolled onto his back, gasping for air. His lungs burned. The taste of the water was metallic, sharp.

He coughed, spitting up black fluid. It wasn't blood. It was ink.

The Authority's ink.

**"STATUS REPORT: SUBJECT SURVIVED EXAMINATION FAILURE. THOROUGHNESS RATING: 2.1. NOTE: INEFFICIENCY DETECTED. SUBJECT EXHIBITED UNNECESSARY DELAY IN ESCAPE."**

Dale wiped his mouth with his sleeve.

The ink smeared, staining the fabric black. He looked at the substation.

The swarm was still there, and they hadn't left. They were hovering around the open hatch, waiting.

They were waiting for him to go back down.

He stood up. His legs shook.

He grabbed his clipboard, which was soggy, the paper warped, but the metal clip held.

He pulled out his pen. The ink was running, but he could still write.

He wrote: *Field Note 47-B. Subject: The Auditor. Hypothesis: Entity is dependent on external power source for cohesion. Without power, entity becomes amorphous and vulnerable to physical separation.*

He underlined *vulnerable*.

A new notification flashed. Red. Angry.

**"WARNING: FIELD GUIDE LOCKED. ANALYSIS INVALID. PENALTY: -10 TP."**

Dale ignored it. He walked to the edge of the substation. The rain was falling hard now. It washed the ink off his suit, but not off his mind.

He wasn't just an exterminator anymore. He was a saboteur. And the Authority knew it.

***

The drive to the safehouse was a blur of wet asphalt and wiper blades that couldn't keep up. Dale didn't turn on the headlights. He drove by the glow of the streetlamps, which were flickering wildly.

The power outage had spread. The entire county was dark.

He parked behind the abandoned textile factory on 4th Street, and the building was a shell, its windows shattered, its roof caved in. It was one of the few places in the county that the Authority's grid didn't reach. No power meant no signal.

No signal meant no oversight.

Dale walked into the main hall. His boots squelched on the concrete.

A figure was waiting for him in the center of the room.

It wasn't a monster. It was a man.

Or at least, it had been a man once, and he was tall, gaunt, wearing a suit that was too clean for the apocalypse. His skin was pale, translucent, and beneath it, veins of black ink pulsed in rhythm with a heartbeat that shouldn't exist.

This was Gregor. He had been a Level IX Technician.

A legend.

The first person Dale had ever met who had reached the top of the certification ladder.

Gregor had disappeared six months ago, rumored to have been promoted to a

"Special Assignment" by the Authority.

Dale stopped. He kept his distance. Ten feet. Standard protocol for unknown entities.

"Gregor," Dale said. His voice was rough, scraped raw by the substation air.

Gregor tilted his head. His eyes were gone. In their place were two smooth, black spheres, like marble.

"Dale Kowalski," Gregor said. His voice was layered, a chorus of whispers. "You broke the circuit."

"I terminated the infestation," Dale corrected. He held up his clipboard. "I documented the vulnerability."

Gregor smiled. It was a horrible expression, stretching too wide, revealing too many teeth.

"You think you're the first to try that? You think you're the first to see the strings?"

Dale didn't answer. He scanned the room. His Field Guide was still locked, but his eyes worked.

He saw the scuff marks on the floor. The drag marks.

The smell of ozone.

Gregor had been waiting here. He had been watching.

"You're not an Authority agent," Dale said slowly. "You're a host."

Gregor's smile faltered.

The ink in his veins pulsed faster.

"I am a vessel.

Like you.

We all are."

"I refused the upgrade," Dale said. "I pulled the switch."

"And you failed," Gregor snapped. The temperature in the room dropped.

Frost formed on Dale's suit.

"You failed the exam.

You lost your certification. You are unlicensed.

Unregistered. To the Authority, you are no longer a technician. You are an infestation.

"

Dale felt a chill that had nothing to do with the weather. He checked his status.

The interface was gone.

No TP count. No level. Just a blank, white void.

"If I'm an infestation," Dale said, "then why are you talking to me? Why not just kill me?"

Gregor stepped forward.

The floorboards creaked.

"Because you are a unique mutation.

Your Thoroughness Rating, even before it dropped, was statistically impossible.

You don't just clean nests. You map them. You find the root.

And you, Dale, found the root of the Authority.

"

Dale's grip on his clipboard tightened.

"What do you want?"

"I want your field notes," Gregor said.

"The ones you wrote in the dark.

The hypothesis about the power dependency. I need to know if it's true.

If it is, there is a way to kill the Authority. Not just disable it. Kill it.

"

Dale looked at the soggy paper in his hand. *Field Note 47-B.* He tore the page off. The paper was ruined, half of the words smeared by the ink in his mouth, but the core message was legible.

*Vulnerable to physical separation without power.*

He held the paper out.

Gregor lunged.

Dale didn't pull his hand back. He thrust it forward.

The paper hit Gregor's chest. As soon as it touched his skin, the black ink beneath the fabric sizzled. Gregor screamed, a sound like tearing metal. He clawed at his chest, trying to remove the paper, but it stuck to him, burning into his flesh.

The ink veins in Gregor's arms turned gray. The black spheres in his eyes cracked.

"It's not enough," Gregor gasped, falling to his knees. "It's just a catalyst. You need a carrier. A conduit."

Dale stepped back. He didn't understand.

"What conduit?"

"The clipboard," Gregor wheezed. "The Authority tracks you through your tools.

Through your documentation.

If you destroy the record, you destroy the link. But if you give it to the Authority... you become the bridge.

"

Gregor looked up. His face was sloughing off, revealing raw, gray muscle beneath.

"You handed it to me, Dale. You handed the proof to the vessel."

Dale froze. He looked at the paper, now embedded in Gregor's chest. It was glowing. A faint, pulsing light.

The Authority wasn't just watching. It was using Gregor. And Gregor was using Dale.

By handing over the evidence, Dale had completed the chain. He had identified the weakness, but he had also delivered the key to exploit it. The Authority didn't want to stop the sabotage. It wanted to control it.

"Gregor," Dale said, his voice flat. "Who else is in the room?"

Gregor's head snapped up.

"No one. Just us."

But Dale heard it.

A hum. Low.

Familiar.

Coming from the shadows in the corner of the room.

From behind a stack of rotting pallets, a figure emerged. It was small, chitinous, and covered in the same black ink that flowed from Gregor's wounds. It had six legs and mandibles that clicked like a typewriter.

It was a Drone. A Class-1 Vermin, but modified. Its eyes were white, blank, and it wore a tiny badge on its thorax.

The badge said: *INTERIM MANAGER.*

Gregor laughed, a gurgling, wet sound.

"You didn't just break the grid, Dale. You created a vacancy. And the Authority always fills vacancies."

The Drone stepped forward. It held a clipboard. A real one. Pristine.

It extended a clawed limb toward Dale.

Dale looked at the clipboard. Then he looked at Gregor, who was dissolving into a puddle of ink. Then he looked at his own hands, stained black.

He realized the trap. He hadn't escaped the system.

He had just changed jobs, and he was no longer an exterminator.

He was being recruited.

If he took the clipboard, he accepted the authority. If he refused, he would be terminated. Not fired. Terminated.

Dale's hand hovered over the Drone's clipboard. His fingers twitched. The contamination anxiety spiked, a physical ache in his chest.

The clipboard was clean. Too clean.

It was an invitation to a new order, a new set of rules, a new set of horrors.

He looked at the Drone. It waited. Patient. Efficient.

Dale made his choice.
