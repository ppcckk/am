Chapter 42: Workspace Prepared

# Chapter 42

The examiner's pen was a click-pen. The kind Dale used to buy in bulk from the office supply store on Route 9. Click-click-click. She was marking something on a form he couldn't see.

The room smelled like toner and old carpet. Fluorescent panels.

A dropped ceiling tile with a water stain shaped like Ohio.

Everything about it was aggressively mundane, which made the fact that the walls occasionally breathed less noticeable. Dale noticed. He noted the pattern in his head.

Three-second intervals.

Slight expansion. Slight contraction.

Not breathing.

Pressure differential. HVAC system with a membrane-sealed return duct. He'd seen it before in crawlspaces where the contractor had used vapor barrier instead of proper sealing.

"You understand the finding," the examiner said. Not a question.

"I understand the finding."

"Residual disruption agents migrated through substrate over a period of eleven days. Degraded membrane integrity at a secondary site. Result: TISB, Critical classification. Your liability."

"I understand."

The examiner looked up. She was maybe fifty.

Gray hair pulled back.

Reading glasses on a chain.

The kind of woman who ran a municipal code enforcement office and had been doing it long enough to know that nobody read their own reports.

"Then explain to me why I shouldn't suspend your certification pending remediation."

Dale set his clipboard on the table. Opened it. The Field Guide was populated to page forty-three. He flipped to a page and slid the clipboard across.

"Migration pattern," he said.

She looked at it. Her click-pen stopped clicking.

"You documented the residue migration in real time."

"I flagged it on day three. Noted substrate conductivity changes. Logged the degradation rate."

"And you didn't report it."

"I didn't have a protocol for reporting it. The Field Guide didn't have an entry for treatment-induced membrane degradation. I flagged it as an anomaly and continued monitoring."

"Monitoring." She set the clipboard down. "You monitored a membrane breach for eleven days."

"I documented it. There's a difference."

The examiner made a sound that might have been a laugh, and she turned to the other chair in the room. The one Dale hadn't been looking at.

He'd been looking at the examiner. He'd been looking at the clipboard.

He'd been looking at the door behind her that had no handle, and the other technician was a woman. Younger than Dale. Maybe thirty.

She had a clipboard too, but it was thinner.

The Field Guide looked like it had maybe eight pages. She was chewing gum with her mouth open.

"You know why she's here," the examiner said.

"I know why I'm here."

"She's here because her violation is the control case."

The gum woman spoke.

"I've got a Class-3 infestation at the Millbrook site. Didn't document the nest. Didn't verify elimination.

Just sprayed and left. Got a Thoroughness of 2.1."

"And your violation?" the examiner asked.

"Reinfestation within two weeks. Three civilians dead."

The room went quiet. The fluorescent panels hummed. The dropped ceiling tile expanded once, slowly, like a lung filling.

"Her violation," the examiner said, "is a standard failure to complete protocol. Reinfestation. Preventable. The penalty is a certification hold and mandatory retraining."

She turned back to Dale. "Your violation is that you completed protocol too well."

Dale said nothing, and he was looking at the gum woman's clipboard.

Eight pages. He could see the Field Guide entries from here.

Generic. One-line descriptions. No behavioral notes.

No substrate interaction data. She hadn't even logged the treatment agent concentration.

She'd treated a Class-3 infestation like a Class-1.

Same mistake the whole county had been making for months.

"The Authority's position," the examiner continued, "is that your over-application of disruption agents created a substrate pathway that something else used. You made the door easier to open. You didn't know. But you did it."

"I documented the pathway."

"You documented it after it was formed."

"I documented it as it was forming.

That's the point. I saw the substrate change.

I logged the rate.

I didn't have a protocol to stop it because the Authority didn't have a protocol for this violation type. TISB is new. You created it to describe what I did."

The examiner clicked her pen. Once. Then stopped.

"Fair point," she said.

The gum woman said, "Wait. He's getting a fair point? I've got three dead civilians."

"Your violation is a failure of thoroughness," the examiner said. "His violation is an excess of thoroughness. The Authority is currently determining whether excess thoroughness constitutes a liability or a preventive action."

"Those are the same thing," the gum woman said.

"They're not." The examiner pulled a second form from a folder. "Kowalski.

Your Field Guide entry on page thirty-one.

The substrate conductivity notes.

You flagged that the migration was heading northeast."

"Yes."

"Northeast of the primary site. Toward the Kovac property."

"Yes."

"Which you didn't know about at the time."

"No."

"But you flagged the direction."

"I flagged directionality of migration. It's standard for residue tracking. If you don't log the vector, you can't model the spread."

The examiner slid the second form across. It was a map. Dale recognized the geography.

Route 9. The duplex.

The culvert system.

And northeast, past the culvert system, past a property line he'd never inspected, was a third site.

The Authority Office had marked it.

"Site 74-C," the examiner said. "Residential. Three occupants. A family."

Dale looked at the map, and he looked at his Field Guide entry. The vector arrow.

The substrate conductivity readings.

The eleven-day migration window. He did the math in his head. Conductivity rate.

Degradation velocity. Distance to 74-C.

"You used my documentation to treat site 74-C."

"We pre-treated it. Preventive disruption barrier. Based on your vector data."

"How many people?"

"Three adults. Two children."

"Alive?"

"Alive."

The gum woman stopped chewing. "You're giving him credit for that?"

"I'm evaluating it," the examiner said. "The question before the Authority is whether the TISB at Kovac's site was a net harm or a net benefit, given that the same substrate pathway was intercepted before it reached a residential zone."

She looked at Dale. "Your Thoroughness Rating at the primary site was 9.2. Your documentation of the migration vector was the only thing that made the preventive treatment at 74-C possible. Without it, the migration would have continued northeast at the same rate and reached 74-C approximately four days after the Kovac breach."

Dale sat with that. His hands were on the table. His face was doing the thing his face did.

"So what's the finding?" he asked.

The examiner clicked her pen. Click-click.

"The finding is that your liability is reduced by a factor of five. You are not suspended. You are not demoted. You are required to file a TISB report within seventy-two hours, including full substrate migration documentation, and you are required to attend a follow-up review at the Columbus office in thirty days."

"That's it?"

"That's it."

The gum woman stood up.

Her chair scraped against the linoleum.

"Three people are dead because I didn't document. He's getting a reduced penalty because he documented too much.

What's the actual lesson here?"

The examiner didn't look at her. "The lesson is that the system is learning what thoroughness costs.

It didn't know that a 9.2 could cause a secondary breach.

It knows now. The next time someone documents a migration vector, they'll have a protocol to report it."

"And the next time someone sprays and leaves, they'll have a protocol to not do that?"

The examiner clicked her pen. "That protocol has existed since Certification Level I."

The gum woman left. The door behind the examiner had a handle now. Or maybe it always had one and Dale had been looking at the wrong side.

Dale stayed seated, and he checked his gear. Spray canister, full.

Flashlight, charged.

Duct tape, half-used. Clipboard, current. Everything in its place.

Everything accounted for.

"You said the examiner would not be redacted," he said. "And I would not be alone in the room."

"Yes."

"The gum woman was the not alone. You were the not redacted."

"Correct."

"Why show me her case?"

The examiner set her pen down. She folded her hands. The fluorescent panels flickered once. The ceiling tile breathed.

"Because you need to understand the comparison.

She is what the system was built for.

Volume. Speed.

Minimum viable treatment. It keeps people alive in the short term. It doesn't solve anything."

"And me?"

"You are what the system is becoming. Something the Authority didn't design. Something that documents so thoroughly it changes the substrate. Something that creates pathways the Authority didn't predict."

She opened her folder. Pulled out a third form.

This one was different.

It wasn't a violation report. It wasn't a certification form. It was a Field Guide entry.

Pre-filled, and the species name was blank. The classification was blank.

But the behavior notes were filled in.

Dale read them.

MIMICS TREATMENT PROTOCOLS. ADAPTS TO DISRUPTION AGENTS. LEARNS FROM DOCUMENTATION PATTERNS. TARGETS LICENSING HOLDERS WHO EXHIBIT HIGH THOROUGHNESS RATINGS.

His throat tightened. He read the entry again. Then a third time.

He looked at the blank species name. The blank classification.

The Authority had filled in the behavior notes but left the identification fields empty, which meant they hadn't identified it yet.

They'd observed it. They'd seen it act.

But they hadn't named it.

In fourteen years of pest control, Dale had learned one thing that no amount of training could teach: an unnamed species was a species you were already losing to.

"What is this?" he asked.

"We don't know yet. We're calling it a Counter-Protocol entity.

It's not a monster.

It's not vermin. It's something that has been observing your work through the membrane and adapting to it."

Dale looked at the entry. The blank fields. The filled-in behavior notes.

"It's in my Field Guide," he said.

"It will populate when you encounter it. You will need to identify it before it can identify you."

"When?"

The examiner picked up her pen. Clicked it. Click-click-click.

"Your Field Guide entry for the substrate migration at the Kovac site. You logged it. You documented it. You flagged the vector."

"Yes."

"That documentation was transmitted through the substrate. It was visible. Readable."

Dale stared at her. The room went still. The fluorescent panels appeared to cease humming.

"You're saying the thing that came through Kovac's breach read my notes."

"I'm saying it read your notes."

The clipboard buzzed.

NOTIFICATION: NEW FIELD GUIDE ENTRY PENDING.

SPECIES: UNKNOWN. CLASSIFICATION: UNKNOWN.

NOTE: FIRST CONTACT PROBABILITY: HIGH.

NOTE: ENTITY HAS OBSERVED YOUR TREATMENT HISTORY. NOTE: IT IS NOT AFRAID OF YOUR PROTOCOLS. NOTE: IT HAS ALREADY ADAPTED TO THEM.

Dale looked at the examiner.

"What do I do?"

The examiner stood. She picked up her folder.

She walked to the door, and she opened it.

The hallway beyond was linoleum and fluorescent light and the smell of old coffee. Normal.

Mundane. Aggressively so.

"You do what you've always done," she said. "You document it.

You identify its weakness.

You eliminate it. You verify."

"And if it's already adapted to my protocols?"

She looked at him. Her face was tired. Not the tired of a long day. The tired of a long career.

"Then you'll need a new protocol."

She left.

The door closed. Dale sat alone in the room.

The ceiling tile breathed.

His clipboard buzzed again.

NOTIFICATION: TREATMENT POINTS RECALCULATED. NET ADJUSTMENT: +47.

NOTE: PREVENTIVE DOCUMENTATION CREDIT APPLIED.

NOTE: YOUR THOROUGHNESS RATING HAS BEEN RECLASSIFIED.

NEW RATING: 9.2 (PREVENTIVE). NOTE: THIS CLASSIFICATION HAS NO PRECEDENT.

NOTE: EXAMINER RECOMMENDS YOU NOT LEAVE THE BUILDING UNTIL YOUR FIELD GUIDE POPULATES. NOTE: IT WILL POPULATE SOON. NOTE: IT IS ALREADY HERE.

Dale looked up. The dropped ceiling tile had a water stain shaped like Ohio.

The stain was moving.

Slowly. Like a

a stain should—spreading, but wrong.

Wrong direction. Wrong speed.

It was climbing.

Against gravity. Up the tile's surface like something being absorbed from the other side.

Dale's clipboard buzzed.

FIELD GUIDE ENTRY POPULATED. SPECIES: Rattus profundus (dimensional vermin).

WEAKNESS: Unresponsive to all Class I and Class II protocols.

NOTE: THIS ENTRY WAS WRITTEN BY THE SUBJECT.

NOTE: THE SUBJECT HAS EDITED YOUR FIELD GUIDE. NOTE: THE SUBJECT HAS ADDED ITSELF TO YOUR FIELD GUIDE.

NOTE: IT IS LISTING YOU AS A PEST.

Dale read it twice. Then he looked at the ceiling tile again.

The stain had reached the edge of the tile and was spreading into the next one.

Then the next. A line of Ohio-shaped stains marching across the ceiling grid like a contamination trail. Frass trail.

He knew what a frass trail looked like, which was a frass trail. Something had been moving through this ceiling.

Moving through the building's infrastructure.

Moving through the walls and the ductwork and the space between the tiles, and it had been doing it for long enough that the stains had set.

He stood. His knees didn't want to. He stood anyway.

The hallway outside was linoleum and fluorescent light and old coffee. In the room's corner, the examiner's chair remained angled as she had left it. The folder was gone. Her handprint on the doorknob was gone.

Dale walked to the door. He didn't open it.

He looked through the small window in the door first.

Protocol.

Assess before entry, and the hallway was empty. The hallway was the hallway. The linoleum was the same beige as the linoleum in every hallway in every municipal building in every county in Ohio.

The fluorescent panels were the same panels. The smell was the same smell.

Except the smell was wrong.

It was too old, which was the smell of coffee that had been sitting for days. Weeks. The smell of a building that had been empty for a long time.

The smell of a place where no one had been alive to make coffee.

Dale looked back at the ceiling. The stains had stopped moving.

They were still.

Patient. Like they'd finished their route and were waiting for him to walk it.

His clipboard buzzed one more time.

NOTIFICATION: FIELD GUIDE ENTRY UPDATED BY SUBJECT. YOUR ENTRY: "Dale Kowalski.

Class II.

Vulnerable to: contamination anxiety, procedural rigidity, need for documentation." WEAKNESS IDENTIFIED: WILL NOT ENGAGE UNTIL WORKSPACE MEETS PROTOCOL STANDARDS.

NOTE: WORKSPACE HAS BEEN PREPARED. NOTE: IT IS WAITING FOR YOU TO ENTER.

Dale looked at the hallway. The door was open. He didn't remember opening it.
