Chapter 126: Static and Wire

# Chapter 126

The searchlights cut the fog into sharp, white ribbons. Dale didn't blink. He adjusted the strap on his respirator, checked the seal on his glove cuffs, and walked toward the warehouse doors with the steady, metronomic pace of a man walking to a break room.

Halloway trailed three steps behind, his boots scuffing against the cracked asphalt.

"Dale, wait. The text said *come now*. It didn't say *walk calmly into a kill zone*."

"Panicking increases heart rate," Dale said, not turning around. "Elevated cortisol attracts pheromones. If there's anything still active in that building, running is a vector."

"It's not pheromones, it's a SWAT team."

"Then don't run. Make them work for it."

The warehouse was a corrugated metal box, stained with rust and chemical runoff. The doors were chained shut, but the lock had been picked recently.

Fresh scratches on the shackle.

Amateurish. Dale noted it on his mental clipboard. *Entry point compromised. Low security clearance.*

Two men stepped out from the shadows of a loading dock. They wore dark tactical gear, but no insignia. No Authority sigils. Just matte black polymer and suppressed rifles pointed at Dale's chest.

"Step away from the door," the taller one said. His voice was gravel dragged over concrete. "Hands where I can see them."

Dale stopped. He raised his hands slowly, palms open. His clipboard remained strapped to his left forearm, a shield of cardboard and plastic.

"Identify yourself," Dale said. His voice was flat, procedural. "I am Dale Kowalski, Licensed Pest Control Technician, Class IV. I am responding to a summons regarding a potential regulatory infraction."

The shorter man laughed, which was a dry, barking sound.

"Regulatory infraction?

You just burned down a Rift node with industrial-grade acid.

That's a felony. That's terrorism.

"

"It was a contained biological hazard," Dale corrected. "Protocol 4-B. Chemical neutralization of a Class-3 dimensional breach. I have the logs."

"Logs don't matter," the tall man said. He took a step forward.

The rifle didn't waver.

"Authority jurisdiction ends at the county line.

This is state soil. State law says you don't use unlicensed toxins on public infrastructure.

"

"It's not public infrastructure," Dale said. "It's a nest. The concrete was frass.

The steel beams were egg sacs. If you'd done your inspection, you would have known.

"

"We did the inspection," the short man said.

He pulled out a tablet.

"And we found out you've been off-book. You're not just a tech, Kowalski.

You're a ghost.

Your certification history has gaps. Big ones.

"

"I had a gap in employment," Dale said. "I was suspended for six weeks while my license was reviewed. Standard procedure."

"Standard procedure doesn't include sealing a rift without filing a Form 12-A," the tall man said. He gestured with his chin. "Open the bag."

Dale hesitated.

The bag contained his emergency kit. Spare canisters. Backup samples.

Evidence.

"Why?" Dale asked.

"Because we're taking you into custody," the tall man said. "And because we want to know what you found in there before the Authority scrubbed the site."

"I didn't find anything," Dale lied.

The tall man narrowed his eyes.

"Don't lie to me, technician.

The scan picked up resonance. You took something.

A sample?"

Dale's hand drifted toward his pocket. The folded violation report. The X-99 code.

"If I hand over my evidence, I violate chain of custody," Dale said. "You'll lose admissibility.

The Authority will void your case.

You'll be demoted. Maybe decertified.

"

The short man lowered his tablet. He looked at his partner.

"He's good at the paperwork game."

"He's a bureaucrat," the tall man spat. "Bureaucrats don't kill things. They just file reports while people die."

"Actually," Dale said, tapping his clipboard against his thigh, "I kill things very thoroughly.

It's the documentation that keeps me alive. You're standing on contaminated soil.

Your boots are tracking spores.

If you arrest me, you contaminate your vehicle. Your precinct. Your home.

You have no decon protocol. You have no Field Guide entry for *Rattus profundus* variants.

You're exposing your family to a Class-2 pathogen because you're too busy aiming guns to read the manual.

"

Silence stretched across the driveway. The searchlights swept over them, blindingly bright.

The short man looked down at his boots. He shifted his weight.

"What kind of pathogen?"

"Neurotoxic.

Slow acting. Causes hallucinations, then paralysis, then systemic organ failure.

It mimics stress. You'd think it was fatigue. Then you'd collapse in your sleep.

"

The tall man's finger tightened on the trigger.

"You're bluffing."

"Look at your partner's left hand," Dale said.

The short man looked down. His fingers were trembling. A fine tremor, almost imperceptible.

"Early stage," Dale said. "You've been here ten minutes.

The spores are airborne near the vents.

You need to decontaminate. Now. Or you'll be dead by morning, and your report will say you died in your sleep.

"

The short man stepped back.

"Marcus, back up."

"It's a trick," Marcus growled.

"Is it?" Dale asked.

"Check your Field Guide. Did you log this location?

Did you check the air quality?

If you didn't, you're exposed. If you did, you should know. You don't know.

Which means you didn't do it. Which means you're negligent.

And if you're negligent, you can't arrest me for negligence.

You can only arrest me for unauthorized use of protocol.

And if you take my bag, you take the proof that I followed protocol. So you destroy your own case.

"

Marcus stared at him. The rifle wavered, just an inch.

"Who told you about the spores?" Halloway whispered from behind Dale.

"I inferred it," Dale said, not moving his head. "The soil was dark. Moist.

The smell was sweet. Rotting fruit and copper.

Classic signs of anaerobic decay in a confined space.

If there was a rift, there was a nest. If there was a nest, there was waste. If there was waste, there were vectors.

"

"You're guessing," Marcus said.

"I'm calculating," Dale said.

"You're shooting. Which is faster?

Thinking or shooting?

Usually, thinking prevents the need to shoot.

"

Marcus raised the rifle again.

"Last warning. Drop the bag."

Dale didn't drop the bag. He opened it.

Slowly. Deliberately.

He pulled out a small glass vial. It was empty.

Just a label. *Sample 44-B.

Dimensional Vermin.

Acidic Residue.*

"This is the residue from the rift seal," Dale said. "It's not dangerous anymore. The acid neutralized the spores.

That's why the air is clear. That's why you're not hallucinating yet.

Because I cleaned the room before you walked in.

"

Marcus lowered the gun. He looked confused.

"You cleaned it?"

"I exterminated it," Dale said. "There's a difference. Extermination involves cleanup.

You just killed the bug.

I removed the nest.

"

The short man, the one with the trembling hand, stepped forward. He pulled a scanner from his belt. It was an old model, bulky and scratched. He pointed it at the ground.

The scanner beeped. A low, steady tone.

"Negative," the short man said. "Air quality is nominal. No spores detected."

Marcus looked at his partner.

"How?"

"He used acid," the short man said, looking at Dale with new eyes. "He dissolved the spores. He chemically sterilized the site."

"Efficient," Dale said. "Waste reduction is a key metric in my job. Less mess, fewer complaints."

Marcus holstered his rifle. He walked up to Dale. He was six inches shorter than the technician, but he carried himself like he owned the pavement.

"You're a problem, Kowalski."

"I'm a resource," Dale corrected. "You're looking for someone to clean up the mess in the suburbs. I'm the only one who knows how to do it without burning down the block. Or getting arrested."

"You're not licensed for rift sealing," Marcus said.

"I'm licensed for structural pest control," Dale said. "A rift is a structural failure. The Authority's definition of 'pest' includes anything that compromises the habitability of a dwelling. I followed the code."

"Which code?"

"Section 4, Paragraph C: 'If the entry point is dimensional, seal it. If the entry point is biological, eliminate it.' I did both."

Marcus rubbed his face. He looked tired. His eyes were bloodshot. He hadn't slept.

"Who else is out there?" Dale asked.

Marcus didn't answer immediately. He looked at the fading vortex in the sky. The stars were returning, cold and distant.

"There are others," Marcus said.

"People who don't write reports. People who don't file forms. They're calling themselves the Unbound.

They think the System is a cage. They break the rules because it feels powerful.

"

"And you're hunting them?"

"We're trying to stop them," Marcus said.

"But they're messy.

They leave bodies. They leave spores.

They don't document. They don't isolate. They just kill.

And when they kill, the infestation spreads. The Authority can't sanction what it can't see.

So they go free.

Until they make a mistake.

"

"What kind of mistake?"

"Like you did," Marcus said. "Sealing a rift without filing the paperwork."

"I filed the paperwork in my head," Dale said.

"That doesn't count."

"It counts for me," Dale said. "It counts for the Thoroughness Rating."

Marcus flinched. The rating. The Authority's hidden metric.

"Your rating is high," Marcus said quietly.

"Too high. That's why they're watching you.

High ratings attract attention.

From the Authority. From the Unbound. From us.

"

"Why?"

"Because a high rating means you're good," Marcus said. "And good exterminators are expensive to hire. And expensive to keep."

"Are you offering me a job?"

Marcus laughed. It wasn't funny.

"I'm offering you a stay of execution.

We need you to clean up a site in Toledo.

A Class-5 infestation. Something big.

Something that's been growing for months. We can't touch it. The Authority won't let us near it.

They say it's 'under observation.'

"

"Observation is passive," Dale said. "If it's growing, it's active. You need to treat it."

"We don't have the clearance," Marcus said.

"You do. Your Thoroughness Rating is 9.4. That unlocks Tier-3 access.

You can walk into that site and do what needs to be done.

We provide the transport. You provide the license.

"

"And if I refuse?"

Marcus looked at his partner. The short man had stopped trembling. He was writing on his tablet.

"Then we arrest you for felony environmental damage," Marcus said. "And we let the Unbound find you. They hate bureaucrats. They'll make an example out of you."

Dale looked at the warehouse. He looked at the sealed glass floor. He looked at the sky.

He thought about the skeleton made of wire and glass. *Internal Affairs.* The audit. The background check.

He thought about the text message. *They know you sealed the rift.*

He thought about the trembling hand of the short man. The power of documentation. The safety of procedure.

But the skeleton had pointed at the sky. At the stars. At something bigger than Toledo.

"If I go," Dale said, "I need full autonomy. I choose the protocol. I choose the tools. I choose the timeline."

Marcus nodded slowly.

"That's standard."

"No," Dale said.

"It's not. Standard is you telling me what to do. Standard is a chain of command.

I want a chain of custody. I want the right to document everything.

Even if it makes you look bad.

"

Marcus hesitated.

"Even if it exposes the Authority?"

"Especially then," Dale said. "Transparency prevents contamination."

Marcus sighed. He pulled a key from his pocket. It was silver, heavy, and etched with a code.

"Here," Marcus said.

"This is a temporary override. Level II Clearance.

It gets you into the Toledo site. But it expires in twenty-four hours. If you're not out by then, the Authority locks you in.

And they don't care if you're licensed.

"

Dale took the key. It was cold. He felt the weight of it in his palm.

He looked at Halloway.

"Get in the truck."

Halloway stared at him.

"Dale, this is a trap."

"It's an audit," Dale said. "Audits are always traps. That's how they find the problems."

He turned to Marcus.

"I'll need a list of entry points. And the last three inspection reports for the building."

"We don't have reports," Marcus said. "The Authority blocked them."

"Then I'll write them," Dale said. "I'm going to create the paper trail. And if there's nothing there, I'm coming back. And I'm filing a complaint against this unit for wasting my time."

Marcus smiled. It was a thin, sharp thing.

"You're a stubborn man, Kowalski."

"I'm thorough," Dale said.

He turned and walked toward the idling truck. The searchlights faded. The fog rolled in, thick and gray.

As he climbed into the passenger seat, Dale looked at his phone. The text message was still there. But now, a new notification blinked in his peripheral vision.

**SYSTEM ALERT: NEW PROTOCOL UNLOCKED.**

**PROTOCOL: JURISDICTIONAL OVERWRITE.**

**DESCRIPTION: Allows user to temporarily bypass Authority restrictions on evidence collection within designated conflict zones. Cost: 50 Treatment Points. Duration: 1 Hour.**

**WARNING: Use of this protocol will flag user as 'Rogue' to Internal Affairs. Repeated use will result in immediate license revocation.**

Dale stared at the text. He hadn't used it. He hadn't even seen it until now. Why had it unlocked?

He looked at Marcus, who was now getting into the driver's seat. Marcus didn't seem to know about the new protocol.

But Dale did.

And somewhere, in the back of his mind, the skeleton's voice echoed. *Point at the sky.*

The truck engine roared. The tires spun on the wet asphalt.

Dale closed his eyes. He opened his Field Guide. He searched for the entry for *Internal Affairs*.

It wasn't there.

Instead, there was a new entry.

**Creature: Auditor.** **Class: IX (Apex).** **Weakness: Unknown.** **Note: They do not exist on the physical plane. They exist in the paperwork.**

Dale opened his eyes. The warehouse was gone. The lights were gone.

Only the road ahead remained. And the key in his pocket, burning cold against his hip.

He had one hour, and he had one site.

And he had no idea if he was cleaning an infestation, or starting one.

"Where in Toledo?" Dale asked.

Marcus didn't look at him. He kept his eyes on the road.

"The Library," Marcus said.

Dale's stomach dropped.

"The public library?"

"The closed one," Marcus said. "The one they sealed after the 'incident'."

"I know that place," Dale said. "I did a termite inspection there five years ago."

Marcus glanced at him.

"You were there?"

"Yes," Dale said.

"I found something. In the basement. I wrote it up.

I told them to seal the door.

"

"And did they?"

"No," Dale said. "They fired me. Said I was hallucinating."

Marcus gripped the steering wheel. His knuckles were white.

"Then you already know what's down there," Marcus said.

Dale looked out the window. The city lights blurred into streaks of neon.

"I know what I saw," Dale said. "But I don't know what it's become."

The truck accelerated.

And in the rearview mirror, Dale saw a shadow detach itself from the darkness of the warehouse.

It wasn't Marcus. It wasn't Halloway.

It was a figure made of static and black wire.

It raised a hand.

And waved.
