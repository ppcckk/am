Chapter 88: Writing in the Dark

# Chapter 88

The fall was not metaphorical. It was a structural failure of the floor plan.

Dale hit the ground hard, the impact jarring his teeth in his skull. His shoulder screamed.

The pen in his hand snapped, the plastic casing splitting along the seam.

He didn't check for broken bones. He checked his surroundings.

He was in a basement. Or a cavity. The walls were not stone, but compressed layers of dried, blackened organic matter, resembling the frass trails of a massive wood-boring beetle, scaled up to architectural proportions. The air smelled of formaldehyde and old copper.

Above him, the hole in the ceiling was already closing, and the edges curled inward like healing flesh, knitting together with wet, sucking sounds. The light from the forest vanished. Darkness rushed in, heavy and suffocating.

Dale turned on his headlamp. The beam cut through the dust motes, illuminating a vast, cathedral-like space, and there, lined up in neat rows, were cages.

Hundreds of them.

Each cage was a cube of glass, suspended by chains that vanished into the ceiling.

Inside each cube sat a creature. Not monsters.

Not demons.

Pests. But *wrong* pests. Cockroaches the size of dogs, their carapaces gleaming with iridescence that shifted color when the light hit it.

Termites with mandibles like industrial shears. Centipedes with legs that had too many joints, twitching in rhythmic, hypnotic patterns.

They were all dormant. Sleeping. Waiting for the inspection.

And standing in the center of the room, holding a clipboard that seemed to be made of the same black matter as the walls, was the Supervisor.

The mirror face reflected Dale’s headlamp beam, blinding him for a second. Then the light adjusted. The Supervisor tilted its head. The skin-suit rustled, a sound like dry leaves scraping against pavement.

"Section 4, Paragraph B," the Supervisor said.

Its voice didn’t come from a mouth.

It vibrated from the clipboard. "Proper storage of dormant specimens requires climate control.

The humidity in this chamber is sixty-five percent. Recommended maximum is forty. You are in violation."

Dale rubbed his shoulder. He stood up.

He brushed black dust off his jacket, and he didn't look at the Supervisor. He looked at the cages.

"Who authorized this facility?" Dale asked.

His voice was steady. It had to be.

If he showed fear, the protocol would shift.

If he showed anger, the audit would escalate to combat. He needed to stay procedural.

"The Authority," the Supervisor said. "I am the authority."

"No," Dale said. He pointed at the nearest cage.

"You’re a localized manifestation.

The Authority is the system.

You’re a middle manager. And you’re out of code."

The Supervisor stepped closer. The ground beneath its feet didn't ripple; it hardened. A circle of black glass expanded outward from its heels.

"Contamination Score at 10.0 indicates high risk of systemic infection. Protocol dictates immediate termination of the source.

You are the source, Mr.

Kowalski. Your thoroughness created a path. Now we seal it."

The Supervisor raised a hand.

From its sleeve, a tool slid out. It wasn't a weapon.

It was a stylus.

A long, needle-like instrument that glowed with white light.

"Pre-elimination sanitization," the Supervisor said.

Dale didn't run. Running would trigger a pursuit mechanic.

The Field Guide would lock, and he had to engage the environment.

He walked toward the first cage.

"Violation logged," Dale said. He pulled a small, battered notebook from his back pocket. The pages were stained with coffee and blood, but the ink was still legible.

He flipped to a fresh page. "Subject: Regional Supervisor, Class Unclassified.

Behavior: Unlawful detention and unauthorized sterilization attempt.

Environmental Hazards: Improper humidity, lack of ventilation, overcrowding of dormant stock."

The Supervisor paused. The stylus hovered inches from Dale’s chest.

"You are writing?" the Supervisor asked. The mirror face showed confusion. A crack appeared in the reflection.

"I'm documenting," Dale said.

"You can't terminate a violation that hasn't been formally cited. Section 12, Subsection A.

Due process requires a paper trail.

I’m creating the trail."

The Supervisor lowered the stylus. The white light dimmed.

"This is a delay tactic," the Supervisor said.

"It’s a protocol requirement," Dale said. He walked to the cage. He didn't look at the giant cockroach inside. He looked at the label on the glass.

The label had been removed.

Dale frowned. He leaned in, pressing his face close to the glass.

The insect inside twitched.

One antenna tapped the glass. *Tap.

Tap. Tap.*

"Unlabeled stock," Dale said. He wrote in his notebook. *Violation: Failure to identify species. Violation: Failure to provide treatment instructions for dormant state. Violation: Potential cross-contamination risk due to unlabeled proximity.*

He turned to the next cage. Termite. No label.

Next. Beetle. No label.

Next. A spider with a human face. No label.

"This is a storage facility," Dale said, his voice rising. "But it’s not a licensed facility. It’s a dumping ground.

You’re hoarding infestations without clearing them. That’s a Class 5 Administrative Error."

The Supervisor’s suit tightened. The skin-stitched seams pulled apart, revealing glowing red veins beneath. "You are attempting to confuse the audit with bureaucratic noise."

"I am identifying the scope of the problem," Dale said.

He pulled out his flashlight. He shone it on the ceiling. The chains holding the cages were rusted.

Corroded.

One chain, directly above the human-faced spider, was snapped in half, hanging by a link.

"Structural integrity failure," Dale said.

He pointed at the chain. "That cage is unstable. If that link breaks, the subject falls.

If the subject falls, it wakes up. If it wakes up, it attacks the nearest unsecured entity."

Dale looked at the Supervisor. "That’s you."

The Supervisor looked up at the chain. The mirror face rippled violently. For a second, the reflection showed not Dale, but a void. A hole in the world.

Then the Supervisor looked back at Dale. "You are threatening the stability of the realm."

"I’m pointing out a safety hazard," Dale said.

"OSHA 1910.176.

Safe clearance and stack heights. You have stacked these cages too high.

You have blocked the egress routes. If this thing falls, you can’t get out. You’re trapped in a box of your own making."

The Supervisor’s stylus ignited. White light flooded the room. "Termination override initiated."

The air pressure dropped. Dale felt his ears pop.

The dormant creatures in the cages stirred.

The cockroach tapped its legs. The termite clicked its mandibles. The spider’s eyes opened.

All of them.

Hundreds of eyes. All looking at Dale.

Not at the Supervisor. At Dale.

They recognized the clipboard. They recognized the pen. They recognized the intent to classify.

To a pest, a classifier is the most dangerous thing in the world. Because a classifier doesn't just kill you. A classifier *knows* you.

Dale realized then that the Supervisor wasn't trying to kill him, and the Supervisor was trying to *erase* him. To wipe the knowledge from his head so the infestation could continue unobserved. Unthorough.

Unchecked.

If the Supervisor succeeded, the rift wouldn't close. It would just get quieter.

"Stop the audit," Dale said.

"I cannot," the Supervisor said. "The file is open."

"Then close it," Dale said. "File it under 'Pending Inspection.' That’s the only way to freeze the timeline. That’s the only way to stop the elimination protocol from engaging."

The Supervisor hesitated. The stylus flickered.

"If I file you as pending," the Supervisor said, "I must assign a handler. I must wait for the next cycle. The backlog will grow."

"Good," Dale said.

"The backlog grows.

The problem is documented. That’s progress.

That’s the system working."

The Supervisor looked at the cages. The creatures were pressing against the glass now. A low hum filled the room. It sounded like a choir of screaming insects.

"You are manipulating the system," the Supervisor said.

"I’m using the system," Dale said. "You’re trying to break it. There’s a difference."

The Supervisor raised the stylus again. But it didn't strike.

It hovered, and the mirror face was cracked now, a spiderweb of fractures running across the surface. Through the cracks, Dale saw something moving. Something dark.

Something that wasn't part of the room.

The Supervisor was afraid.

Not of Dale. Of what was behind him.

"You don't want to be here," Dale said softly. "You’re a construct.

A program running on a dying server.

If you eliminate me, the data is lost. If you document me, the data persists. You’re an archivist, not an executioner.

Act like one."

The Supervisor’s hand trembled. The stylus dropped an inch. Then another.

The ground shook. The ceiling groaned. The single link holding the spider cage above Dale snapped.

The cage fell.

Glass shattered.

The spider, the size of a minivan, dropped into the dust.

It didn't attack.

It scuttled to the side, chittering, its human face contorted in pain. It was injured.

Broken.

Dale didn't run. He didn't scream.

He dropped to his knees, and he pulled a sample jar from his belt. He pulled a forceps. He reached for the spider.

The Supervisor lunged. "Do not engage! It is not approved for contact!"

Dale ignored it, and he grabbed the spider’s leg. He injected a sedative from a vial he hadn't had ten seconds ago—a loophole in his inventory that appeared the moment he identified the threat. *Field Guide Entry: Arachnida humanoidis.

Weakness: Sedative resistance low in juvenile stages.

This is not a juvenile. This is a survivor.*

He didn't care about the species name. He cared about the biology.

The spider went limp.

Dale sealed the leg in the jar. He stood up. He held the jar up to the Supervisor.

"Evidence," Dale said. "Collected. Documented. Isolated."

He looked at the Supervisor. The mirror face was still cracked. The red veins pulsed slower now.

"Your audit is incomplete," Dale said. "You failed to document the environmental collapse. You failed to secure the specimens. You are a hazard, not a supervisor."

The Supervisor lowered the stylus. The white light died.

"What happens now?" the Supervisor asked. Its voice was smaller. Fragile.

"Now," Dale said, "you’re on suspension. I’m taking the file. And I’m going to finish the job."

Dale turned to the open door of the cavity. The hole in the ceiling had reopened.

Light from the stone forest poured in.

But it wasn't the light of the forest, which was harsh, fluorescent, industrial.

Above, in the rift, something was descending. Not the Supervisor. Something larger.

Something that wore a suit of stitched skin but had no mirror face; it had a thousand eyes, each one a different certification level.

Dale looked at the jar in his hand. The spider leg twitched. A message scrolled across his vision.

**NEW ENTRY: CLASS IX OVERSEER.** **THREAT LEVEL: CRITICAL.** **ACTION REQUIRED: IMMEDIATE ESCALATION.**

Dale looked at the Supervisor. The Supervisor was dissolving, its suit slumping into a pile of dry skin on the floor. The clipboard clattered to the ground.

As the pile of skin, Dale saw a object remain. Not the clipboard. A key. A small, brass key with a tag that read: *Level 9 Access.*

Dale picked it up.

The descending thing in the rift opened its mouth. It didn't scream. It laughed. A sound like a thousand clipboards slamming shut.

**SYSTEM ALERT: CROSS-CONTAMINATION DETECTED.** **PROTOCOL: ERASE OPERATOR.**

Dale looked at the key. He looked at the descending horror. He looked at his notebook.

He had one line left on the page, and he wrote: *Violation: Unauthorized access to Level 9.*

He jammed the key into the air. The air ripped open. Not a door. A wound.

Dale stepped through.

The wound closed behind him, and he was in an office.

A real office. Fluorescent lights. Beige carpet.

A cubicle.

And sitting at the desk was a woman. She looked exactly like his ex-wife.

But she was dead.

Her skin was gray. Her eyes were empty.

She looked up.

"Mr. Kowalski," she said. "You’re late for your shift."

She slid a form across the desk. It was a termination notice. But the signature line was already filled in.

In his own handwriting.

Dale looked at the form. He looked at her. He looked at the door. It was locked.

**CERTIFICATION LEVEL IX UNLOCKED.** **PENALTY: PERMANENT LOSS OF REALITY ANCHOR.**

Dale didn't panic. He picked up the pen on the desk.

"Where's your ID badge?" Dale asked.

The dead woman smiled. Her jaw unhinged.

"Show me the work," she whispered.

The lights went out.

But Dale was already writing.
