Chapter 95: Protocol Surrender

# Chapter 95

The rearview mirror showed nothing but black asphalt and the taillights of his truck fading into the distance. Dale didn’t look back.

He knew what was back there.

The Core hadn’t chased him because it didn’t need to, and it had simply waited for the paperwork to catch up.

The sky was still wrong. The cloud formations had dissipated, but the air tasted like ozone and wet copper. Dale’s hands were shaking so badly he had to grip the steering wheel with both hands to keep the truck from drifting into the ditch. His knuckles were white, bone-deep pale against the cracked vinyl.

*You didn’t finish the job.*

The text message burned in his pocket. He hadn’t read it again, and didn’t want to know if the Authority could track his heartbeat through the phone.

He took the next left, then another right, aiming for the industrial park on the edge of town.

It was the only place with heavy-duty sanitation facilities and a supply closet that hadn’t been looted by the looters who thought "survival" meant stealing canned beans and ignoring the mold growing in the pantry. He needed supplies.

Not weapons.

Not swords. He needed a Class-IV Neutralizing Agent, a secondary containment field, and a fresh stack of Form 88-B.

The truck’s engine whined, a high-pitched complaint of worn bearings. The fuel gauge was hovering near empty.

He had maybe three gallons left.

If he ran out, he’d have to walk.

And walking meant being visible. Being visible meant being audited.

The Authority’s inspectors were coming. He could feel it. The air pressure had dropped, a subtle shift that made his ears pop.

It wasn’t weather. It was jurisdictional enforcement.

Dale checked his Field Guide. The holographic projection flickered in the corner of his vision, transparent and urgent.

**Entry 405: Core Entity.** **Status: Unresolved.** **Thoroughness Penalty: -4.2 Points.** **Current Rating: 4.98.**

The number landed, and then it kept landing.

A drop from 9.2 to 4.98. He was no longer the top technician in the county. He was barely holding onto his Level II license.

If he dropped below 4.0, he would be demoted to Class I.

He would lose access to the heavy protocols. He would be relegated to spraying ants and sealing cracks in drywall while things the size of buses ate the rest of the world.

He couldn’t let that happen. Not yet. He had to fix this. He had to find a way to complete the protocol retroactively.

The industrial park was a graveyard of rusted shipping containers and half-finished warehouses. Most of the lights were out. The silence was heavy, broken only by the distant, rhythmic thumping of something large moving through the nearby woods.

Dale parked the truck behind a stack of pallets, far from the main road.

He grabbed his kit bag. It was light. Too light.

He’d used up most of his neutralizers on the broodmother’s nest earlier that night. He checked his pockets.

One spare canister of aerosol sealant.

A utility knife with a dull blade.

His clipboard, held together with duct tape, and he stepped out of the truck. The air was cold. Frost was forming on the metal surfaces, but it wasn’t natural frost.

It was crystalline, purple-tinged. Residue from the Rift.

He moved quickly, sticking to the shadows.

His eyes scanned the ground. No footprints. No drag marks.

The area was clean.

Too clean. That was the problem.

In pest control, a clean room meant nothing was wrong.

In monster hunting, a clean room meant something had cleaned it. And predators don’t clean up after themselves. They hide their tracks.

He reached the supply building. The door was chained shut. He pulled his utility knife and cut the chain. It snapped easily, the metal fatigued by the same dimensional corrosion that was eating the rest of the town.

Inside, the smell hit him first. Bleach.

Ammonia.

And something else.

Something sweet and rotting, like fruit left too long in the sun.

He clicked on his flashlight. The beam cut through the dust motes. Shelves lined the walls, stocked with buckets, mops, and industrial-grade chemicals. He walked down the aisle, scanning the labels.

*Hydrogen Peroxide. Industrial Strength.* *Ammonia Hydroxide.* *Quaternary Ammonium Compounds.*

None of these would work on a Core Entity. It was a metaphysical infestation. It fed on belief, on fear, on the structural integrity of reality.

You couldn’t poison it with bleach. You had to starve it.

You had to deny it the narrative it needed to sustain itself, which was the loophole. The Authority’s system rewarded documentation. It rewarded understanding. If Dale could prove that the Core Entity was not an infestation but a manifestation of unresolved bureaucratic trauma, he might be able to close the ticket without killing it.

It was a risky play. It required him to file a "Non-Infestation Report," a form that most technicians never even saw. It required him to argue that the monster wasn’t a bug.

It was a ghost.

And you don’t exterminate ghosts. You evict them.

He found a box of Form 88-B in the back, and the paper was brittle, yellowed with age. He grabbed a pen. His hands had stopped shaking.

The procedural mindset was kicking in. The anxiety was still there, a low hum in the back of his skull, but it was manageable.

It was just noise.

He ignored it and started writing.

**Subject: Core Entity (Unidentified). Location: Sector 7, Residential Zone.** **Observation: Entity does not exhibit biological markers.

No waste products. No feeding trails. No territorial markings.** **Hypothesis: Entity is a psychosomatic manifestation of collective anxiety regarding the Rift Event.** **Proposed Treatment: Cognitive Containment Protocol.

Re-framing of narrative. Eviction notice.**

He wrote fast. His handwriting was cramped, precise. He knew the inspectors would be looking for mistakes. He wouldn’t give them any.

A sound came from the back of the room. A wet, slapping noise. Like bare feet on tile.

Dale froze, and he didn’t turn around. He kept writing. If he turned around, he broke protocol.

If he broke protocol, he failed.

The footsteps got closer. Slow. Deliberate.

"Technician," a voice said. It wasn’t human. It sounded like two stones grinding together. "You are filing a false report."

Dale didn’t look up. "I am filing a preliminary assessment. The final determination will be made by the Authority upon review of the evidence."

"The evidence is nonexistent."

"The evidence is the absence of physical decay," Dale said, his voice steady. "If it were a biological entity, it would have left frass.

It would have left a nest.

It didn’t. It left a feeling. Feelings aren’t pests.

They’re conditions. And conditions are treated with therapy, not poison."

The footsteps stopped.

"You are clever, Dale Kowalski."

"I am thorough."

"Cleverness is a flaw. Thoroughness is a virtue. But you have broken the rules."

Dale finished the form. He signed it. He dated it. He stamped it with his personal seal, a small rubber stamp he kept in his pocket for exactly this kind of emergency.

He turned around.

Standing in the aisle was a figure made of smoke and static; it had no face, just a swirling vortex of gray where features should be. It was tall, nearly seven feet. It wore a suit that looked like it had been tailored from shadow.

An Inspector.

Dale’s heart hammered against his ribs. This was one of the cloud shapes. It had found him.

"Your Thoroughness Rating is failing," the Inspector said. "You have not eliminated the threat. You have attempted to classify it as non-existent."

"I have classified it as non-biological," Dale corrected. "The Authority’s guidelines for Class-9 threats require biological verification before lethal force is authorized. If I kill it without verification, I am committing an act of unlicensed slaughter. That is a violation of Code 12-A."

The Inspector tilted its head. The static around its shoulders crackled.

"You are citing Code 12-A to avoid combat?"

"I am citing Code 12-A to avoid a mistreatment penalty," Dale said.

"If I kill it, and it turns out to be a benign entity, I lose my license.

If I don’t kill it, and it’s dangerous, I get demoted. But if I document it correctly as a psychological hazard, I retain my status as a Consultant.

And Consultants are not required to carry weapons."

The Inspector stepped closer. The air around it grew cold. Frost spread across the floor, climbing up Dale’s boots.

"You think you can out-bureaucrat the Authority?"

"I think you have a backlog," Dale said. "The county is flooded with Class-1 vermin.

The Authority doesn’t have the manpower to audit every single technician in real-time.

You’re here because I flagged a Class-9. But I haven’t engaged it. I’ve filed a hold."

"A hold," the Inspector repeated. The word sounded dangerous.

"Yes.

A hold. The case is pending.

The threat is contained by lack of engagement.

I am not ignoring it. I am processing it. There is a difference."

The Inspector was silent for a long time, and the static in its face shifted, forming something that might have been a smirk.

"Interesting. You are using the system’s own laziness against it."

"I’m using its efficiency," Dale said. "The Authority wants results.

It doesn’t want paperwork.

But it can’t process results without paperwork.

So I’m giving it paperwork. I’m giving it a reason to wait."

The Inspector reached out a hand. It was long, skeletal, made of shifting smoke. It touched the clipboard in Dale’s hands.

The paper turned to ash.

Dale flinched, but he didn’t pull away. He watched the ash fall to the floor.

"Your report is destroyed," the Inspector said. "You have no proof of your filing."

Dale looked at the ash. Then he looked at the Inspector.

"I don’t need proof," he said. "The system recorded the submission. It’s in the cloud. You can’t delete data you haven’t fully received yet."

The Inspector paused. It looked at its hand. The smoke was flickering, unstable.

Dale was bluffing. He knew he was bluffing. The cloud wasn’t real.

It was a projection of his own desperate hope. But the Inspector didn’t know that.

The Inspector was bound by rules, and rules required certainty.

"You have until dawn to complete the protocol," the Inspector said. "If the entity is not resolved, or properly classified, you will be stripped of your license. And you will be terminated."

"Terminated?" Dale asked.

"Exterminated," the Inspector corrected. "You are part of the infestation now. You’ve attracted them.

You’ve drawn attention.

You are a beacon."

Dale felt a chill that had nothing to do with the temperature.

He had led the monsters here. His presence in the industrial park had acted as a lure. The Core Entity hadn’t stayed behind.

It had followed him, and he looked at the supply shelves. He had no weapons. He had no neutralizers. He had only his clipboard, which was now empty, and his mind, which was racing.

"Why are you telling me this?" Dale asked.

The Inspector stepped back. The static tightened, forming a clearer shape.

"Because we are curious," the Inspector said. "We have never seen a technician try to sue a monster. It is... inefficient. But it is novel."

The Inspector dissolved into mist, drifting up through the cracks in the ceiling.

Dale stood alone in the dark. The ash on the floor was gone, swept away by a breeze that hadn’t been there a second ago.

He looked at his hands, and they were clean.

No blood. No slime.

Just dirt and dust.

He walked to the door. He had to leave.

Now.

Before the Inspector came back. Before the Core Entity arrived.

But where could he go? The town was a hive. The Authority was watching. And he had just admitted to a Class-9 entity that he was a threat.

He stepped out into the night. The sky was clear, but the stars were moving. They weren’t twinkling. They were pulsing.

His phone buzzed, and he pulled it out.

**New Notification.** **Incoming Job: Priority Alpha.** **Target: Unknown Entity. Location: Your Location.** **Reward: Immediate License Suspension.**

Dale stared at the screen.

The Authority wasn’t just auditing him. It was hiring him. It was setting him up as bait.

And then, a second message.

**From: Dale Kowalski (Self).** **To: Dale Kowalski (Self).** **Subject: Exit Strategy.**

He hadn’t sent that, and he opened it.

Inside was a schematic of the industrial park. And a red circle around the main warehouse.

And a note: *Don’t trust the forms. The system is learning. It doesn’t want you to be thorough. It wants you to be afraid.*

Dale looked up, and the warehouse doors were open. A light was glowing inside.

Not the harsh white of a halogen bulb.

The soft, pulsing purple of the Rift.

He wasn’t alone.

Someone else was in there. Someone who knew the system. Someone who had hacked it.

Dale gripped his utility knife. It was useless, but it was all he had.

He took a step toward the warehouse.

The ground beneath his feet vibrated. A low hum, rising in pitch.

He realized then that the Inspector hadn’t left. It hadn’t dissolved. It had merged with the environment. The entire industrial park was now the Inspector.

And it was waiting for him to make a move.

Dale looked at the empty field guide. Entry 405 was still there. But below it, a new entry was forming.

**Entry 406: Bureaucratic Manifestation.** **Weakness: Unknown.** **Threat Level: Absolute.**

He couldn’t fight it. He couldn’t run.

He had to change the job description.

Dale pulled out his pen. He wrote on his arm, in ink, directly on his skin.

**Protocol: Surrender.**

He walked into the purple light.
