Chapter 59: Taxonomy Audit

# Chapter 59

The auditor's pen stopped on page fourteen.

"That's a misclassification."

Dale didn't look up. He'd already found it.

Three weeks ago, cross-referencing his field guide against the Authority's taxonomy updates, he'd spotted the entry.

The second organism he'd documented during his first six weeks was listed as Rattus profundus, and it should have been Rattus profundus var. Albus. White variant.

Same species, different subspecies.

Different frass coloration. Different nesting depth.

Different response to calcium-based substrates.

He'd caught it. He'd updated his guide. But the original entry was what the Authority had on file.

"I identified the error and corrected it within forty-eight hours," Dale said.

"The correction was filed after treatment completion. The original identification governed protocol selection."

Dale looked up. The auditor sat across from him at the folding table they'd set up in the county records office. Her name was Voss.

She had the posture of someone who'd been doing this long enough to find it tedious. Her certification badge read Level VII.

She'd been auditing for six years.

"The protocol I selected was appropriate for both variants," Dale said.

"Containment, isolation, elimination. Same sequence.

Same tools. The only difference between R. profundus and R. profundus var. albus is the substrate recommendation for nest destruction. I used a calcium-lime blend that works on both."

"That's not how the system evaluates protocol selection."

"The system awards points for completed protocols. I completed the protocol.

I documented the correction, and the treatment was effective. The organism was eliminated and verified."

Voss made a note. "The Authority's position is that an incorrect identification at the point of protocol selection constitutes a documentation failure regardless of outcome."

"So the treatment worked but the paperwork failed."

"Precisely."

Dale set his pen down.

He'd been expecting this. He'd been expecting it since he'd found the error and realized the audit window covered his first six weeks.

He'd spent the intervening time trying to make the original entry valid.

He hadn't found a way for that specific treatment. But he'd found something else.

He pulled the 1987 report from his folder and slid it across the table.

Voss picked it up. Read it. Set it down. Picked it up again.

"This is a county filing. Not an Authority record."

"It's a pest control job report filed under the county's pest control division. The county's pest control division was the Authority's local interface before the Rift.

They processed reports, and they issued provisional certifications.

This report has a provisional certification number. The same number I photographed from a wall at the Elm Street property."

"That proves nothing about your work."

"It establishes a treatment chain." Dale opened his notebook to the page where he'd worked it out. "Chain of custody is a standard documentation requirement in pest management. When one technician performs a treatment and a second technician performs a follow-up, the second technician inherits the first technician's treatment validity as long as the documentation chain is unbroken.

I've been treating Rattus profundus at that property since my first encounter. The 1987 report establishes the first treatment.

My work establishes continuity.

The chain is unbroken."

Voss turned the report over. Checked the back. "Chain of custody is a county protocol. Not an Authority protocol."

"The Authority adopted county protocols as its baseline when it formalized the certification system. Section four, subsection C of the Licensing Framework. Treatment validity transfers through documented chains of custody. It's in the Authority's own rulebook."

Voss pulled a tablet from her bag. Tapped through it. Her expression didn't change, but something shifted in her shoulders. A small recalibration.

"Section four, subsection C," she said. "Yes. That exists."

"It applies to my case."

"It applies to any case where a documented chain of custody exists between treatments. The question is whether a thirty-seven-year gap constitutes a valid chain."

"The chain isn't broken.

The property wasn't re-treated in the interim. No other technician filed a report.

The organism persisted.

My first treatment at that location was a continuation of the 1987 treatment. I just didn't know it at the time."

Voss made another note. "You're arguing that your original entry is validated because the organism was correctly identified and treated in 1987, and your treatment is a continuation of that documented effort."

"I'm arguing that the chain of custody validates my entry. The subspecies misclassification doesn't matter if the treatment inherits from a correct prior identification."

Voss set her pen down. She looked at Dale for a long moment. "That's creative."

"It's procedural."

"It's a loophole."

"It's a rule. You just haven't seen anyone use it."

Voss picked up her pen. She wrote something on her form. Dale couldn't read it from where he sat.

"I'll note it," she said. "But the Authority may not accept the interpretation."

"The Authority wrote the rule."

"The Authority also retains discretion in interpretation."

"Then I'll be ready to argue interpretation."

Voss turned to page fifteen. The audit continued. Dale went back to his notes.

They worked in silence for forty minutes. Voss flagged two more items.

A missing photograph from a treatment at a commercial property on Ninth Street.

A protocol sequence that was technically correct but performed out of the standard order.

Neither was fatal. Both would dock his Thoroughness Rating if they stood.

At page twenty-two, Voss stopped again.

"This entry. The third organism you documented. You listed it as Rattus profundus."

"Yes."

"It wasn't."

Dale looked at the page. He remembered the organism. He remembered the treatment. He remembered the verification.

"It was," he said.

"It wasn't. The Authority's taxonomy database has a reclassification on file. The organism you encountered at the commercial property on Fifth Street was not Rattus profundus.

It was a mimic. Rattus profundus mimic.

Different species.

Different weakness. Different frass. You treated it with a protocol designed for R. profundus."

Something cold moved through Dale's chest.

Cross-contamination. He'd deployed a protocol designed for one creature type against another.

That was a forty percent debuff and a twenty-four-hour Field Guide contamination.

But he hadn't been penalized at the time. He hadn't been flagged.

"The organism was eliminated," he said.

"It was eliminated with a debuffed protocol. The Authority's system didn't flag it because the organism died before the contamination window closed.

But the documentation is wrong.

You identified it as the wrong species.

You used the wrong protocol. You got a result by accident."

"I got a result because I used a substrate that works on both species."

"That's not how the system evaluates it."

"Then the system is evaluating it wrong."

Voss looked at him. "The system is evaluating it correctly. You had a forty percent debuff.

You didn't notice because the organism was already weakened from a prior encounter, and the system gave you credit for a clean elimination.

It was not a clean elimination.

It was a contaminated elimination that happened to succeed."

Dale pulled out his own notebook.

Found the entry. Read it back.

He'd noted the organism's condition at the time of encounter.

Partially necrotic tissue. Reduced mobility. He'd attributed it to a prior territorial dispute with another Rattus profundus.

If Voss was right, the organism had been weakened by something else. Something that had already deployed a different protocol against it.

"Who else was treating that property?" Dale asked.

"That's not relevant to the audit."

"It's relevant to me. If another technician was on that property, they should have filed a report. If they didn't, that's an unlicensed treatment. If they did, the chain of custody applies again."

Voss was quiet. She made a note.

Then she turned to the back of the audit form.

A section appeared that Dale had not previously encountered.

A section at the bottom of the last page, printed in smaller type than the rest of the document.

"Additional finding," she said. "The Authority has flagged an irregularity in the 1987 report you submitted."

"Irregularity?"

"The name on the report was redacted. Standard practice for older county filings."

"Yes."

"It wasn't redacted by the county. The county's digitization records show the name was present in the original filing. It was removed during upload. By the Authority."

Dale stared at her.

"The Authority only redacts names of currently active technicians," Voss said. "They don't redact historical records. They redact active ones."

"So the 1987 technician is still active."

"The 1987 technician is currently on the Authority's roster. They have been active continuously since the filing was made. Their Thoroughness Rating is 9.8. It has never dropped below 9.7 in thirty-seven years."

Dale thought about the report. The redacted name. The perfect rating. The fact that someone had been doing this job since before the Rift, before the Authority made itself known, before anyone else in the county had a clipboard.

"Who is it?" he asked.

Voss closed the audit form. She capped her pen. She looked at Dale with an expression he couldn't read.

Not contempt. Not approval.

Something more complicated.

"That's not in my scope to disclose. But I'll tell you this. The Authority didn't flag your file because they suspected you of fraud.

They flagged it because the 1987 technician filed a chain of custody claim six days ago.

They're claiming continuity with your work. They're claiming your treatments are extensions of their treatment chain."

Dale's hands went still on the table.

"If they're claiming continuity," he said, "then the chain of custody defense I just argued is already valid."

"It is. Which means your misclassification on page fourteen doesn't revoke your certification. It never could have."

"Then why are you auditing me?"

Voss stood, and she gathered her papers. "Because the Authority wants to know if you found the chain on your own or if you were guided to it. The 1987 technician has been filing chain of custody claims for years.

No one has ever been able to trace them back. You're the first technician who found the 1987 report in the county database."

"I found it by searching the certification number."

"The certification number that the 1987 technician planted on the wall at Elm Street. The one you photographed."

Dale sat still.

"The 1987 technician is trying to reach you," Voss said. "They've been trying for a long time. The question the Authority is asking is whether you're going to answer."

She walked to the door. She opened it.

"Your certification is intact.

Your Thoroughness Rating will take a small hit for the page fifteen items.

You'll be eligible for the exam. " She paused in the doorway. " But the Authority is also flagging the 1987 technician's file for the same audit you just went through.

They're auditing both of you. And they've assigned me to both files."

She stepped out. The door closed.

Dale sat at the folding table. He looked at the 1987 report. He looked at the certification number. He looked at the pencil in his pocket.

His phone buzzed. Unknown number. A text message. Four words.

CHECK THE FRASS.

He looked at the report again. The frass description. The one that had looked standard. The one he'd accepted without question because it matched the Rattus profundus entry in his field guide.

He read it a second time. Then a third.

The frass was described as dark brown, cylindrical, with longitudinal ridges. Standard for Rattus profundus. Standard for every entry in his guide. Standard for every entry the Authority had ever published.

Except, and he pulled his field guide from his bag. Flipped to the Rattus profundus entry.

Read the frass description.

Dark brown, cylindrical, longitudinal ridges. Match.

He flipped to the mimic entry. The one the Authority had added after the reclassification. He hadn't studied it yet. He'd been too busy preparing for the exam.

The mimic frass description was identical. Same color. Same shape. Same ridges.

Every detail matched.

Except one. The report noted the frass was found in a vertical shaft, eighteen inches deep.

Rattus profundus nests horizontally.

Mimics nest vertically.

Someone had written the report with the correct data. They'd written it correctly enough that a trained eye would catch the nest orientation; they wrote it to appear standard to anyone not paying close attention.

They'd been testing him. Thirty-seven years ago, someone had planted a report that could only be decoded by a technician who knew the difference between a horizontal and a vertical frass trail.

A technician who would parse the report exactly as Dale had.

Not quickly.

Not dismissively. With the specific, methodical, obsessive attention to detail that the Authority's system rewarded and no one else in the world valued.

The 1987 technician hadn't just been doing the job. They'd been looking for someone to take over.

Dale's phone buzzed again. Same unknown number.

YOU HAVE FOUR HOURS. THE EXAM ISN'T THE

THE EXAM ISN'T THE PROBLEM. THE PROBLEM IS WHAT'S IN THE BUILDING WITH YOU.

Dale looked up from the phone. The room was the same. The desk.

The filing cabinet. The water stain on the ceiling tile that had been there since he started at the Authority's regional office in March.

Nothing had changed.

Nothing was crawling, and he checked the timestamp. 2:47 PM. Four hours from now was 6:47 PM. The exam started at 9:00 AM tomorrow.

He typed back. WHO IS THIS.

The response came in under three seconds.

LOOK AT THE FILING CABINET.

Dale looked at the filing cabinet.

Standard four-drawer lateral. Gray.

The top drawer held his personal correspondence.

Second drawer held inspection reports from his first year. Third drawer held reclassification notices. Fourth drawer held exam prep materials he'd been organizing for eleven days.

He opened the fourth drawer.

The exam prep materials were gone. In their place was a manila folder, unmarked, with his name written on the tab in handwriting that was not his. He opened it.

Inside was a field guide entry. Handwritten.

Not printed.

Not Authority-formatted.

Someone had written it by hand, in ink, on lined notebook paper, with the kind of cramped, urgent script that Dale recognized from his own inspection reports when he was trying to finish before a tenant came home.

The entry was for a species he'd never seen in his guide. The illustration was crude but accurate—six legs, a segmented thorax, a mandible structure that suggested a piercing-sucking apparatus. The frass description was a line: *Found in walls.

Vertical shafts. Dark brown.

Cylindrical.

Longitudinal ridges.*

The same frass.

The same frass he'd just matched to the mimic entry. The same frass that the 1987 technician had written into a report as a breadcrumb trail leading here.

To this folder.

To this entry, and he flipped to the back of the folder. A page. Typed on a typewriter. The paper was old—yellowed, the kind that hadn't been manufactured since the mid-nineties.

*If you're reading this, you found the frass. You found the folder.

You are the replacement.

Do not take the exam, and the exam is the nest. Do not take the exam.

The exam is the nest. Do not take the exam. The exam is the nest.*

Three lines. Identical. Written in the same typeface, the same alignment, the same slightly-left-of-center strike on the letter E that Dale had seen exactly once before.

On the Rattus profundus entry in his own field guide. Printed by the Authority. Formatted by the Authority. Published to every licensed technician in the county.

He looked at the filing cabinet. The fourth drawer was open.

The bottom of the drawer was not the bottom of the drawer.

A gap existed. A vertical shaft. Dark brown.

Something in the shaft shifted.

Dale closed the drawer. He stood. He checked his watch. 2:49 PM. Four hours.

He picked up his clipboard.
