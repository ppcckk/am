Chapter 44: Shared Ventilation Shaft

# Chapter 44

The laundromat smelled like lavender and ammonia, which meant someone had mopped recently. The nest smelled like neither.

It smelled like a dead dog left in a sealed car for a week, then set on fire, then doused with bleach.

Dale noted this in his clipboard. Odor profile: inconsistent with prior treatment.

He crouched behind the industrial dryer in the back row, and the unit was a Maytag, three-phase, rated for four hundred pounds of wet weight. Its exhaust duct ran through the wall and into a shared ventilation shaft that serviced the entire strip mall.

Dale had followed the shaft from the roof access panel.

The duct was warm. Something inside it was breathing.

His Field Guide pulsed. The entry populated in real time as he observed the nest structure through the gap behind the dryer.

It was a colonial species, not solitary.

The workers were roughly the size of house cats, with chitinous plates that had a blue-green iridescence under his flashlight.

The queen was deeper in the shaft, anchored to the duct wall with a secretion that looked like hardened resin.

He wrote: Colonial arthropod. Estimated colony size: 40-60. Queen anchoring method: resinous adhesive.

Nest location: HVAC infrastructure. Entry point: shared ventilation shaft, unsealed.

The Authority pinged him.

TREATMENT POINTS: 18. IDENTIFICATION CONFIRMED. FIELD GUIDE ENTRY: Ventricular Crawler (Arthropoda profunda, colonial variant). WEAKNESS UNLOCKED: Thermal disruption of resin adhesive at 140°F.

Good. He had the weakness. Now he needed to apply it.

He pulled his protocol toolkit from the truck. The Authority had upgraded his kit after his Level II certification, replacing the basic spray cans with a modular applicator system. He loaded the thermal lance, a tool that heated a localized area to a precise temperature without spreading damage. It was designed for exactly this kind of nest: you heated the resin until it softened, pulled the queen free, and the colony collapsed within hours.

He set the lance to 145°F. He reached into the gap behind the dryer.

The Authority stopped him.

PROTOCOL RESTRICTION: THERMAL LANCE (CLASS II) UNAVAILABLE UNDER CONDITIONAL LICENSE. REASON: PROTOCOL DEVIATION CITATION (CASE 44-A/7). ALTERNATIVE PROTOCOLS AVAILABLE: [LIST].

Dale stared at the notification.

Conditional license. The exam. He'd broken a rule during Vasquez's practical, and the Authority had logged it.

Not as a failure.

As a violation. He'd passed, but he'd passed with a mark against him, and now his toolkit was restricted.

He read the alternative protocols. They were all wrong.

The first option was a chemical aerosol.

He'd used it before on solitary vermin. Against a colonial species in an enclosed ventilation shaft, the aerosol would disperse unevenly, and the workers deeper in the duct would survive, regroup, and re-colonize within three weeks.

Sloppy. A Thoroughness Rating of maybe 4.

The second option was a containment seal.

Block the shaft, seal the entry points, and wait for the colony to starve.

This would work, technically. But the shaft serviced six other businesses.

The crawl space under the laundromat connected to the plumbing. If the colony starved but didn't die cleanly, the decomposition would contaminate the water table. He'd be trading one infestation for another.

The third option was a targeted pheromone disruption. This was the closest to correct.

It would scatter the workers and isolate the queen.

But the Field Guide entry he'd just generated listed the weakness as thermal disruption. Pheromone disruption was not the listed weakness. Using an unlisted method meant no targeted damage bonus.

The colony would be disrupted but not eliminated.

They'd adapt. They'd always adapt.

Dale set the thermal lance back in the case, and he looked at the nest; the workers were agitated now. They'd sensed his presence through the duct wall.

One of them pressed its face against the metal and clicked its mandibles in a pattern that his Field Guide translated as territorial warning.

He opened his clipboard.

He wrote: Prior treatment observed.

Chemical residue on duct interior consistent with Class I aerosol application. Application was partial.

Coverage estimated at 60%. Colony has developed behavioral avoidance response to aerosol dispersal patterns.

He looked at the residue. It was a standard pyrethroid blend, the kind any unlicensed technician would grab from a hardware store.

Someone had been here before.

Someone had sprayed, gotten a partial kill, and walked away. The colony had adapted. The workers now clustered away from the aerosol drift pattern, which meant they'd learned to avoid it.

This was a violation.

Not his. Someone else's.

Unlicensed application of a Class I protocol against a Class II colonial species, without documentation, without follow-up, without verifying elimination.

This was the kind of thing the Authority would normally issue a citation for.

Unless the person who did it had a license.

Dale checked the Authority's job log for this address. The notification appeared immediately.

PREVIOUS TREATMENT: 11 DAYS AGO. TECHNICIAN: KOWALSKI, D. (SELF).

That wasn't right. He hadn't been here eleven days ago.

He checked the timestamp again, which was correct.

The Authority had logged a treatment at this address, attributed to him, that he had not performed.

Someone was using his credentials.

He stood up. His knees ached. He was thirty-eight and he'd been crouching in a laundromat for twenty minutes.

He pulled out his phone and called the Authority's dispatch line; the automated system asked him to state his certification number.

He stated it.

The system confirmed it. Then it asked him to state the nature of his inquiry.

"Discrepancy in job log," Dale said. "There's a treatment attributed to me at this address that I did not perform."

A pause. Then: YOUR INQUIRY HAS BEEN LOGGED. A REVIEW AGENT WILL CONTACT YOU WITHIN 48 HOURS.

Forty-eight hours, and he looked at the nest. The workers were building.

They'd added a new chamber to the structure in the twenty minutes he'd been standing here.

The queen was producing. He could hear the wet sound of it through the duct wall.

He needed to finish this job today. His Conditional License wouldn't let him use the thermal lance.

The alternatives were all wrong.

But the Field Guide had given him the weakness.

Thermal disruption. He just couldn't use the tool that delivered it.

Unless he didn't need the tool.

Dale looked at the laundromat. Industrial dryers. Three-phase power.

The exhaust duct ran directly through the wall behind the machines. The duct was metal.

If he could heat the duct itself to 140 degrees, the resin would soften.

The queen would release. The colony would collapse.

He could do that with the dryers, and he checked the wiring.

The Maytag in the back row was connected to a dedicated circuit. If he bypassed the thermal cutoff and ran the heating element at maximum output for six minutes, the exhaust duct would reach approximately 160 degrees at the point where the nest was anchored.

The dryer would be destroyed.

The laundromat owner would file an insurance claim, and the resin would soften, and the queen would release, and the colony would die.

This was not in any protocol. This was not in his Field Guide, and this was infrastructure weaponization, the same category as the copper sulfate trick from Vasquez's exam. The Authority had flagged that as a violation.

But here was the loophole. The Authority had flagged it because he'd used it during an examination, where the protocol was prescribed. During an examination, you followed the protocol. Deviation was a violation.

But this was not an examination. This was a job.

And the Authority's own rules stated that Treatment Points were awarded for completing full extermination protocols: identify, document, isolate, eliminate, and verify.

They did not specify which tools you used to eliminate, and they specified the sequence.

Dale had identified. He had documented. He had isolated the nest location.

Now he needed to eliminate. The Authority restricted his tools.

But it had not restricted his methods, and he opened the electrical panel behind the dryers. He bypassed the thermal cutoff on the back row unit. He set the timer.

Six minutes.

He walked outside.

He waited.

The parking lot was empty; the sky was doing that flat gray thing that meant rain later but not yet. He stood by his truck and watched the laundromat's exhaust vent on the roof.

Nothing happened for three minutes. At four minutes, a smell started.

Not the dead-dog smell.

Something sharper.

Like burning hair and hot metal.

At five minutes, the workers came out. They poured out of the vent in a stream, chittering, their plates dull and gray without the iridescence. The heat had reached them.

They were confused, disoriented. They fell to the parking lot and twitched.

At six minutes, the dryer tripped its breaker. The vent stopped smoking. The workers stopped moving.

Dale checked his Field Guide. The colony entry updated. STATUS: ELIMINATED. VERIFICATION REQUIRED.

He went back inside.

The back row dryer was dead. Melted wiring, scorched housing, the whole unit cooked from the inside out. The nest was gone.

The resin had liquefied and the queen had fallen into the duct, where the heat finished the job.

He could see the remains. He photographed them.

He wrote the verification entry.

The Authority awarded him 34 Treatment Points. His Thoroughness Rating held at 9.2. The elimination method was logged as: NON-STANDARD. ACCEPTABLE.

Non-standard. Acceptable. Not a violation.

The Authority had accepted it because he'd followed the sequence. Identify.

Document.

Isolate.

Eliminate. Verify.

The method was his. The rules hadn't said which tools he could use. They'd said which steps he had to complete.

He was writing his report when his phone buzzed. Not the Authority's dispatch line. A text message from an unknown number.

It said: Nice trick with the dryer. I've been trying to figure out how to do that for two weeks.

Dale deleted the message. He finished the report. He walked to his truck.

The text came again.

You used infrastructure. I used infrastructure.

You got 34 points.

I got a citation. You know why? Because I didn't have a Conditional License.

I had a full one.

And the Authority lets you break rules when you're already flagged, and they don't let you break rules when you're clean.

It's backwards.

It's a compliance trap.

Dale started the truck. He didn't reply.

Third message.

I'm the one who treated this laundromat eleven days ago. It wasn't sloppy.

I was testing something.

I wanted to see if a colony would develop avoidance behavior against a Class I aerosol.

I needed the data. I needed someone with a Conditional License to come clean it up so the Authority would log it as a completed job under my credentials.

He needed me.

Fourth message.

I'm not your rival. I'm your predecessor. And I'm about to show you what happens when you stop being a technician and start being an instrument.

Dale put the truck in drive. He checked the rearview mirror. The laundromat was shrinking behind him. The vent on the roof was dark.

His phone buzzed a fifth time.

The Authority just issued a new directive. It's addressed to me. It says I'm being reassigned to your service area.

Permanent. They're pairing us.

He read it twice. Then he looked at the text again.

They're pairing us.

Dale drove.

The rain started. The wipers beat. He thought about the copper sulfate.

He thought about the dryer.

He thought about the fact that someone else had been running the same play he'd been running, but had done it first, and had done it with full credentials, and had been punished for it, and was now being sent to work with him.

The Authority was not pairing them to help, and the Authority was pairing them because one of them was already compromised. The Conditional License wasn't a punishment. It was a leash.

And they were tying the leash to someone who knew how to cut it.

His phone buzzed a sixth time. A new notification from the Authority.

CONDITIONAL LICENSE UPDATED. NEW RESTRICTION: CO-OPERATION WITH ASSIGNED PARTNER IS MANDATORY. REFUSAL TO CO-OPERATE CONSTITUTES PROTOCOL VIOLATION.

Dale read it. He read it again. He set his phone in the cup holder and gripped the steering wheel with both hands.

He was being forced to work with someone who had stolen his credentials, manufactured a job to test a hypothesis, and was now being sent to him as a permanent assignment. And if he refused to cooperate, he'd lose his license.

The rain got harder. The wipers couldn't keep up. He drove anyway.
