Chapter 101: Illegal Nest Inversion

# Chapter 101

The floor didn’t just crack; it liquefied.

Concrete slurry sprayed outward in a thick, gray arc, coating Dale’s boots and the lower half of his pants in a layer of wet grit. The sound wasn’t an explosion.

It was a wet, sucking gasp, like a drain unclogging after years of neglect.

The soldiers’ boots slipped on the sudden mud. One of them went down, his flamethrower hose tangling around his leg as he scrambled for purchase.

Dale didn’t move, and he planted his feet wider, feeling the vibrations travel up through the soles of his shoes. The protocol was active. *Nest Inversion*.

It was illegal under Authority bylaws 4.1 through 4.9, specifically the clauses regarding structural integrity and unauthorized terraforming.

But the system had accepted it. The prompt still hung in the air, glowing with a sickly, pulsing red light that overlaid the dim emergency lighting of the basement.

**PROTOCOL: NEST INVERSION (ACTIVE)** **STATUS: CONTAMINATION LEVEL: CRITICAL.** **PENALTY: 40% DEBUFF TO ALL NON-VERMIN PROTOCOLS FOR 24 HOURS.** **WARNING: FIELD GUIDE CORRUPTION IMMINENT.**

Dale ignored the warning. His eyes were on the worms.

The creatures hadn’t fled. That was the first sign that this wasn’t a standard infestation.

A *Rattus profundus* or a standard burrower would have panicked at the loss of structural support.

These things—the symbiotic hybrids his mother had hinted at—had simply adjusted.

They re-oriented themselves in the slurry, their translucent bodies hardening slightly against the pressure change. The one nearest Dale raised its stylus again, not to write, but to point at the ceiling.

The suit, whose name tag read *AGENT HALE*, was on his knees, shouting into his wrist comm. “Flamethrower team, hold fire! Repeat, hold fire!

Target area is compromised. Structural collapse risk high.

We need containment foam, not fire.”

“Negative,” a voice crackled back, distorted by static. “Command says burn it. The infestation is spreading upward.

The school’s ventilation system is compromised.

If those things get into the ducts, the whole block is lost.”

Dale grit his teeth, which was the real problem. Not the worms. Not the suit.

The bureaucracy. The Authority didn’t care about the worms’ sentience.

They cared about the *infestation metric*.

And right now, Dale’s illegal protocol had spiked the metric into the red.

If the soldiers burned the room, they’d kill the worms, sure. But they’d also kill Dale’s parents.

And they’d lose the *key*, and he looked at his mother’s face in the slime; it was pale, stretched tight over bone, but her eyes were open.

They weren’t the milky, vacant stare of a host.

They were sharp. Terrified. Aware.

“Dale,” she whispered. The sound vibrated through the floor, traveling up his legs. “The ventilation.”

Dale’s head snapped up, and he looked at the suit. “The suit.

Your boots.

Check your intake filters.”

Hale looked up, confusion warring with his panic. “What?”

“Your filtration systems,” Dale barked, his voice cutting through the din of shifting debris. “They’re cycling air. If the spores are in the slurry, you’re breathing them. You’re already contaminated.”

Hale froze. He looked down at his chest unit. The indicator light, usually a steady green, was blinking yellow. *Filter Saturation: 85%.*

The worm nearest Dale tilted its head. It appeared to smile. A horrible, gummy expression that exposed too many teeth.

**SYSTEM ALERT: NEW SPECIES IDENTIFIED.** **NAME: Symbiotic Hive-Node (Variant: Residential).** **WEAKNESS: VENTILATION DISRUPTION.** **BONUS: +150% DAMAGE TO AIRBORNE VARIANTS IF EXHAUST IS BLOCKED.**

Dale read the alert three times. It wasn’t just a monster.

It was a *systemic* failure.

The worms weren’t just hiding in the walls; they were integrating with the house’s HVAC system.

The “key” his mother mentioned wasn’t a physical object. It was the *flow*.

The air circulation. If the soldiers burned the room, the heat would rise, pushing the spores up into the ceiling voids, where they would seed the entire neighborhood’s infrastructure.

If they did nothing, the soldiers would breach the door in thirty seconds.

If they burned, they doomed the county.

If Dale used a standard extermination protocol, he’d trigger the contamination penalty and likely lose his certification before he could stop the spread.

He needed a loophole. A procedural error. A code violation so egregious that the Authority’s automated enforcement would override the military command’s direct order.

The clipboard was still in his hand. He had not written anything on it. The report he’d signed. *STATUS: ACCEPTED.*

He pulled out his pen. His hands were shaking, not from fear, but from the adrenaline of breaking the rules.

He flipped to the back of the report form.

A section titled *Environmental Impact Assessment* existed. He had never filled it out. It was optional for Class II technicians.

Usually, it was skipped.

Dale started writing. Fast. Illegible.

**SUBJECT: HVAC INTEGRATION.** **OBSERVATION: ACTIVE SPORE CIRCULATION VIA RETURN AIR DUCTS.** **VIOLATION: UNLICENSED BIOHAZARD DISCHARGE INTO SHARED INFRASTRUCTURE.** **ACTION: IMMEDIATE CESSATION OF COMBUSTION PROTOCOLS TO PREVENT MASS CONTAMINATION EVENT.**

He held the clipboard up, angling it toward Hale. “Code 12-B.

Unlicensed discharge.

If you burn this, you’re not just fighting monsters. You’re committing a Class A Environmental Felony. The Authority will suspend your license before the smoke clears.”

Hale stared at the paper. “That’s… that’s not how it works. We’re authorized for emergency elimination.”

“Authority bylaw 7.4,” Dale said, his voice flat, deadpan, the voice of a man who had spent fourteen years denying permits to people who wanted to kill raccoons the wrong way. “Combat protocols may not exceed local environmental saturation limits.

Check your sensors, Hale.

Your saturation is ninety percent.

You’re over the limit. If you light the pilot, the Authority’s automated fine kicks in before the flame hits the wall.

A fine of fifty thousand Treatment Points. Per man.”

Hale’s eyes widened. Fifty thousand points. That was bankruptcy. That was the end of their careers.

“Call it in,” Dale said. “Ask them if they want to pay the fine, or if they want to wait for me to treat the ducts.”

The comm unit crackled. Static hissed.

Then, a voice, colder and more bureaucratic than Hale’s.

“Kowalski. You are in violation of multiple protocols. Your Field Guide is corrupting.

Your Certification Level is at risk.

Stand down.”

“I am standing down,” Dale said, pointing his pen at the worms.

“I am *treating*. Identify. Document.

Isolate. You are obstructing a licensed technician from performing a Class II Extermination.

Step back.”

The silence stretched.

The soldiers shifted, uneasy. The flamethrowers idled, a low rumble in the basement.

The worms watched. They didn’t attack. They waited for the humans to make the mistake.

Dale turned to his mother. “What’s the key?”

She coughed, a wet, rattling sound. “The pressure.

The negative pressure.

The worms… they breathe in. If we reverse the pressure… they choke. They can’t pull air.

They can’t move.”

Dale looked at the HVAC unit in the corner, and it was a standard residential furnace, caked in dust and grime. The return vent was torn open, black sludge dripping from the filter.

He looked at the system prompt.

**PROTOCOL: VENTILATION STASIS.** **REQUIREMENTS: ACCESS TO PRIMARY HVAC UNIT. NEUTRALIZATION OF RETURN AIR INTAKE.** **COST: 200 TREATMENT POINTS.** **RISK: MEDIUM. RISK OF SUFFOCATION FOR HOSTS.**

Suffocation. His mother was suffocating.

If he used this, he might save the neighborhood, but he’d kill her.

Unless.

Dale looked at the worms; they were attached to her, to the father in the other cell, to the walls.

They weren’t just inside the hosts, and they were *part* of the ventilation. The worms were the filters.

If he blocked the intake, the worms would die. But the hosts would die too.

Unless he could swap them.

He looked at Hale. “Give me your mask.”

Hale hesitated. “What?”

“Your oxygen mask. Full cycle. Give it to me.”

Hale unclipped the emergency reserve mask from his belt, and it was a sleek, black polymer thing, worth more than Dale’s car. He handed it over without a word, his eyes locked on Dale’s.

Dale caught it. He looked at his mother. “Hold your breath.”

He crawled toward the HVAC unit. The slurry was thick, pulling at his clothes.

The worms shifted, sensing the change in his air supply.

One lunged, a whip of flesh snapping at his ankle.

Dale didn’t flinch. He slapped it away with his clipboard, the wood cracking.

He reached the unit. The fan motor was humming, a low drone that vibrated in his teeth, and the return duct was wide, gaping like a wound. Black tendrils of worm-matter were woven into the filter mesh.

Dale ripped the filter out. It was heavy, slick with mucus. He tossed it aside.

He pulled the oxygen mask from his belt and strapped it to his face. The rubber sealed tight. The hiss of oxygen filled his ears.

Then he did something stupid, and he reached into his tool belt and pulled out a can of aerosolized pesticide. Not a combat agent. A common, Class-1 ant spray. *Pyrethrin-based.* Useless against large monsters. Useless against symbiotic hybrids.

But it was labeled *Non-Repellent*. It didn’t scare insects. It killed them on contact.

He sprayed it directly into the return air intake.

**SYSTEM ALERT: ILLEGAL AGENT DEPLOYMENT.** **CROSS-CONTAMINATION WARNING: PYRETHRIN INCOMPATIBLE WITH SYMBIOTIC PHYSIOLOGY.** **EFFECT: UNKNOWN.**

The white mist swirled into the ductwork.

For a second, nothing happened.

Then, the worms screamed.

It wasn’t a vocal sound. It was a psychic shriek that tore through the basement, a wave of pure agony that made Hale drop to his knees, clutching his head. The soldiers cried out, their comms buzzing with feedback.

The worms in the walls convulsed. The black tendrils in the HVAC unit withered, turning gray and brittle. They snapped, falling into the fan blades with a series of sharp *cracks* that sounded like gunshots.

The negative pressure dropped. The vacuum seal broke.

Air rushed *out* of the ducts.

And with it, a cloud of spores.

Dale’s eyes went wide. He had misjudged the reaction. The Pyrethrin hadn’t killed the worms.

It had *irritated* them. It had made them expel their contents.

The spores hit the air. They were visible, glowing faintly blue.

“Mask!” Dale shouted, his voice muffled by the oxygen gear. “Full seals! Now!”

Hale was already moving. He grabbed his helmet, snapping it shut; the soldiers fumbled, eyes tearing up as the spores hit their exposed skin.

Dale looked at his mother. She was still alive. She was coughing, but the worms attached to her were receding, pulling back into the walls, recoiling from the chemical burn.

She wasn’t suffocating. She was healing.

But the room was filling with blue dust.

Dale checked his Field Guide.

The entry for *Symbiotic Hive-Node* was flickering, the text scrambling into gibberish. The contamination was real. His rating was dropping.

He could feel it, a hollow ache in his chest.

**THOROUGHNESS RATING: 9.2 -> 6.5**

He had saved her. But he had ruined his career.

“Dale,” his mother whispered. Her voice was stronger now.

“The Rift.

It’s not opening. It’s *closing*.”

Dale looked up. The ceiling of the basement was gone.

Replaced by a swirling vortex of blue light and black earth.

The ground above them was churning.

The worms weren’t trying to escape the house. They were trying to *feed* the Rift.

By blocking the airflow, Dale had starved the mechanism that was keeping the dimensional breach open.

But the breach wasn’t closed. It was unstable.

A hand reached out of the vortex. A human hand. Pale, long-fingered, holding a clipboard.

Not Dale’s clipboard.

A black clipboard, made of obsidian.

The figure that stepped through wasn’t a worm. It wasn’t a soldier.

It was a woman in a suit identical to Hale’s, but her skin was made of the same translucent, chitinous material as the worms.

Her face was Dale’s mother’s face, but older. Scarred.

She looked at Dale. She smiled.

“License revoked,” she said. Her voice was the voice of the Authority.

Hale stood up, shaking his head. “Who is that?”

Dale stared at the woman, and he recognized her. Not as his mother.

But as the signature on the original *Dimensional Breach* report from Chapter 1.

The one he had dismissed as a prank app.

She wasn’t a demon. She was a *technician*.

And she was here to audit him.

The vortex widened. Gravity shifted. The floor tilted. The worms, now confused and dying, dragged themselves toward the opening, drawn to the higher concentration of energy.

Dale’s oxygen tank hissed. Empty.

He pulled the mask off. The air tasted like copper and old blood.

“Agent Hale,” Dale said, his voice raspy. “Did you log the environmental violation?”

Hale nodded, pale. “Yes. Filed.”

Dale looked at the woman. She raised her obsidian pen.

**SYSTEM ALERT: AUDIT INITIATED.** **SUBJECT: DALE KOWALSKI.** **VIOLATION: UNAUTHORIZED PROTOCOL USE, CROSS-CONTAMINATION, HUMAN HOST MANIPULATION.** **SENTENCE: CERTIFICATION REVOCATION PENDING.**

The woman pointed her pen at Dale.

“Step through,” she said. “Your trial period is over. Welcome to Class IV.”

Hale raised his flamethrower. “No.”

Dale looked at Hale. He looked at his mother, who was slumped against the wall, watching him with sad eyes. He looked at the swirling void.

If he stepped through, he might die. If he stayed, he would be stripped of everything.

His license.

His purpose.

His life, and he looked at his clipboard. The report was still there. *STATUS: ACCEPTED.*

Dale Kowalski had never walked away from a job.

He took a step toward the void.

And then he stopped.

He reached into his pocket and pulled out a single, dry cockroach. A German cockroach. Small.

Brown. Common.

He crushed it in his hand.

The Authority’s system froze.

**ERROR: BIOLOGICAL SAMPLE DETECTED. LOCAL INFESTATION PRESENT.** **JURISDICTION SHIFT: LOCAL.** **AUDIT SUSPENDED.**

The woman in the suit frowned. She looked at the crushed cockroach in Dale’s palm.

Dale smiled, a dry, humorless twist of his lips.

“You want to audit me?” he said. “Start with the kitchen. I found a nest.”

The woman’s expression changed. Not anger. Interest.

She lowered her pen.

“Show me.”

The void didn’t vanish. It *paused*. The swirling blackness halted, the roaring static dropping to a low, bureaucratic hum, and the air pressure in the room equalized with a sickening pop.

Dale didn’t wait for an invitation, and he turned on his heel, boots squeaking on the slick, slime-coated linoleum, and marched toward the kitchen entrance. His clipboard was tucked under his arm, the pen clicked and ready. Behind him, the woman in the suit followed, her heels clicking in perfect, unsettling rhythm against the debris.

Hale remained frozen, flamethrower dangling uselessly at his side, watching the absurdity unfold with wide, disbelieving eyes.

Dale kicked open the kitchen door. The smell hit him first: not the metallic tang of rift-energy, but the warm, musty rot of old grease and damp drywall.

He ignored the shattered refrigerator, and he ignored the dead rat in the sink; dropped to his knees, pulling a flashlight from his belt. The beam cut through the dim light, illuminating the gap beneath the stove.

There. A frass trail. Fine, dark granules scattered across the tile.

"Entry point K-7," Dale muttered, writing in the air with his pen. The ink shimmered gold in the Authority’s field of vision.

"Structural breach.

Moisture retention present.

Active nesting site confirmed."

The woman stepped into the kitchen, her obsidian pen hovering. "This is a Class I infestation," she said, her voice devoid of emotion. "It does not justify the suspension of a Class IV audit."

"It establishes jurisdiction," Dale corrected, not looking up. He pointed the flashlight at the dark space under the cabinets. "And it establishes priority.

Local hazards supersede interdimensional administrative review. That’s in the handbook.

Page 42, subsection C."

He reached into his tool belt and pulled out a gel bait applicator; didn’t use it on the rift. He didn’t use it on Hale. He squeezed a precise, pea-sized drop onto a cardboard trap he’d ripped from a nearby cereal box.

The trap hissed. Not with magic, but with the sudden, violent twitch of a predator, and a shadow detached itself from the darkness under the sink.

It was not a rat but a spindly, chitinous creature with too many legs, moving out at frantic speed. It was a Rift-Mite, a minor parasite that fed on high-level dimensional energy leaks.

It had crawled out of the rift. It was feeding on the leak Dale had just documented.

Dale didn’t flinch.

He didn’t attack. He simply held the trap steady. The creature lunged, sticking to the gel.

Dale sealed the trap with a twist of his wrist.

**SYSTEM ALERT: SPECIES IDENTIFIED: *Acarus riftis* (Rift-Mite).** **STATUS: CONTAINED.** **JURISDICTION: LOCAL.** **AUDIT CANCELLED. NEW WORK ORDER GENERATED.**

The woman in the suit stared at the trap. Her frown deepened, lines of genuine confusion etching her face. For the first time, the bureaucratic mask slipped. She looked at the trap, then at Dale, then at the dying worms outside the window.

"You used a Class I pest control method to neutralize a Class IV dimensional byproduct," she said slowly. "That’s... technically legal."

"It's effective," Dale said, standing up and brushing dust off his knees. "Now. If you’re done with the audit, I have a violation report to file. And you’re standing in my workspace."

The woman opened her mouth to speak, but the lights flickered. Not just the kitchen lights. Every light in the house died. The hum of the void outside stopped.

Silence. Total, absolute silence.

Then, a sound from the walls.

Not a scurry.

A thud. Heavy.

Wet. Coming from everywhere at once.

Dale looked at his Field Guide. The page for *Rattus profundus* was blank. The page for *Acarus riftis* was fading, turning gray.

The woman in the suit stepped back, her eyes wide. "Dale," she whispered. "What have you done?"

Dale looked at the walls. They were bulging.

"I haven't done anything yet," Dale said, his voice flat. "I just documented the entry point."

The wall exploded.
