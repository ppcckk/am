Chapter 49: Unlicensed Entity

# Chapter 49

The notification was trying to classify him.

Dale could see it in the corner of his vision, that faint amber glow that used to mean a new infestation had been detected in his service area. Now it meant something else.

The text kept cycling.

UNLICENSED ENTITY. UNLICENSED ENTITY. UNLICENSED ENTITY.

It couldn't complete the classification.

The form kept failing at the species field because the Authority had no entry for him, and he had no entry for it, and the whole system was grinding against a gap it couldn't fill.

He stood in the parking lot of the duplex where he'd lived for six years and watched the notification fail for the ninth time.

The text flickered. Reset. Tried again.

A new attempt at categorization, and he pulled out his notebook.

Wrote: 0647.

Parking lot. Authority attempting classification.

Failing.

The amber text glitched. For half a second it showed something else. Something that looked like a form field being filled in by invisible hands, and then it snapped back to UNLICENSED ENTITY and the cycle resumed.

Dale tore out the page. Folded it. Put it in his breast pocket.

Across the street, the ground was splitting again.

Not the slow, geological crack of a major Rift event. This was smaller.

A seam in the asphalt, maybe two feet long, widening with a sound like a zipper being pulled by something underneath.

Dale could see the frass. Fine dark particles pushing up through the crack, accumulating at the edges in a pattern he recognized from fourteen years of residential inspections.

Carpenter ant frass.

But wrong. Too uniform.

Too geometric, and the particles were all the same size, which meant they weren't frass at all. They were eggs. Microscopic eggs, pushed up in bulk by something below.

He wrote it down. 0651. Asphalt seam, east side of lot.

Substrate disruption consistent with oviposition event.

Frass composition: uniform.

Not biological waste. Egg mass.

The amber notification pulsed harder. The Authority was trying to register the event. But it couldn't.

Not while he was standing here. His unlicensed status was contaminating the detection radius.

The system couldn't log an infestation that fell within his personal classification zone, and his personal classification zone was the size of a man standing in a parking lot.

He understood it immediately. The Authority's detection field worked by proximity to a licensed technician. He was the antenna. And the antenna was broken.

Which meant nobody was going to see this infestation. Not the Authority. Not the other survivors with their swords and their improvised spears and their half-formed Field Guides. The crack in the asphalt was invisible to the system because he was standing next to it.

Dale walked to the crack. Crouched. Looked at the eggs.

They were arranged in rows. Perfect rows. Each one exactly the same distance from the next, like someone had laid them out on a grid.

He'd never seen anything like that.

Real insects don't do geometry. They do density.

They follow pheromone gradients and moisture differentials.

These were placed. Deliberately. With spacing that suggested a planter, not a creature.

He wrote: 0654. Egg arrangement: grid pattern, 3mm spacing.

Suggests directed placement.

Not natural oviposition.

Source unknown.

The amber text changed. For the first time it showed something other than UNLICENSED ENTITY. It showed a number.

003.

Just the number. No context. No label.

He knew what it meant. Sub-Entry 003.

Kowalski.

The handwriting on the basement door. The Authority had him filed somewhere. Had a number for him.

Was using that number to track the gap in its own system.

He closed the notebook. Stood up. Walked back to his truck.

The engine started on the first try. He pulled out of the lot and headed east, toward the commercial strip where the second Rift event had opened three days ago.

He'd been monitoring it.

Not through the Authority. Through his own notes. Through the damage spreading from the site, a pattern unlike anything in his guides.

He was three miles out when the notification changed again.

This time it wasn't amber. It was red. And it wasn't trying to classify him. It was trying to warn him.

INCOMING LICENSED OPERATOR. PROTOCOL: ELIMINATION. TARGET: UNLICENSED ENTITY.

Dale looked at the road ahead. In his rearview mirror, he could see a figure on the sidewalk.

Walking.

Fast.

Carrying a clipboard, and he recognized the clipboard. It was Authority issue. Standard issue. The same kind he'd used for two years before he'd started writing in his own notebook.

The figure was getting closer. Not to him. To the parking lot he'd just left.

The figure stopped at the crack in the asphalt. Crouched.

Dale could see the clipboard tilt as they examined the eggs.

He pulled over. Watched.

The figure was young. Maybe twenty-five. Wearing the standard-issue vest, the one with the Authority seal on the breast pocket.

They were writing fast, their pen moving in short sharp strokes.

Their Field Guide was open on the clipboard. Dale could see the page structure even from this distance.

The header fields.

The identification box. The weakness notation.

They finished writing. Stood up. Turned. Looked directly at Dale's truck.

He didn't move. Didn't start the engine. Just sat there with his hands on the wheel and watched them walk toward him.

They reached the driver's window. Looked down at him.

Their eyes were flat.

Professional.

The look of a technician who'd identified a violation and was deciding whether to write a citation or escalate.

"You're the unlicensed one," they said.

"Yes."

"I can see the notification. You're in my detection radius."

"I know."

They looked at their clipboard. Looked at him. Looked at their clipboard again.

"My Field Guide has an entry for you."

Dale said nothing.

"It populated this morning. I woke up and it was there. Full entry.

Species name, habitat, behavior pattern, weakness notation. " They flipped a page. " I didn't write it.

I've never seen you before today."

"Did you read it?"

"Yes."

"Did it help you identify me?"

They hesitated. That hesitation told Dale everything. The entry had worked.

The identification was complete.

The Authority had populated this technician's guide with a record of Dale Kowalski, and the technician had read it, and the system had accepted it, and now Dale was a documented species in someone else's Field Guide.

The targeted damage bonus.

The mechanic that let a technician hit harder when they'd studied a creature's weakness before engaging. It worked on monsters. It worked on infestations.

And now it worked on him.

"What's the weakness notation?" Dale asked.

The technician's hand tightened on the clipboard. "I'm not going to tell you."

"That's fine."

"It says you'll freeze.

When the protocol starts.

It says you need to establish a workspace before you can engage. It says you need to document before you can act.

And if the documentation is already complete, you'll stop trying to do it yourself because the system has done it for you. You'll stand there. You'll wait for the notification to finish.

And it won't finish because you're unlicensed."

Dale looked at the technician. Waited.

"That's what the entry says will happen," they continued. "That's what it says I should do. Wait for you to freeze. Then treat."

"Then treat," Dale repeated.

"Like an infestation."

"Yes."

He started the engine. The technician stepped back. Dale pulled out. Watched in the mirror as the figure stood in the parking lot, watching him go, clipboard in hand, Field Guide open to a page that described him.

He drove for four miles before he pulled over again. Opened his notebook. Wrote:

0712.

Licensed operator, unknown name. Authority-issued Field Guide populated with entry for Kowalski. Identification complete.

Weakness notation: documentation paralysis.

Protocol: wait for freeze, then treat, and he underlined the weakness notation. Then he crossed it out.

Not because it was wrong.

Because it was right. And because writing it down gave it a reality the Authority's entry lacked, and the Authority's entry was a system record.

It could be updated, overwritten, deleted. His entry was ink on paper.

It existed outside the system.

It couldn't be edited by the Authority, and he wrote again: COUNTER-PROTOCOL: Do not document when documentation is already complete. Do not wait for notification.

Do not establish workspace. Engage immediately. Use existing documentation.

Do not freeze, and he read it back.

It was the opposite of everything he'd done for fourteen years. Every protocol he'd ever followed started with documentation. Every job began with observation, identification, and notation.

The Authority's entry had found the one weakness in his professional identity and named it.

He drove on, and the commercial strip was a half mile ahead; could see the Rift event from here.

A column of dark substrate rising from the parking lot of what used to be a hardware store.

The substrate was moving. Not crawling. Growing.

Like a coral formation, branching upward in fractal patterns, each branch ending in a cluster of those geometric eggs.

The Authority couldn't see it. His presence was blocking detection. The licensed operators in the area couldn't see it either, because the Authority hadn't logged it, because the Authority couldn't log it while he was within proximity.

He was the problem. His existence was preventing the system from treating the infestation. And his own documentation method, the manual method, was making him more visible to the system, more trackable, more sub-entry.

He parked. Got out. Walked toward the growing structure.

The eggs were the size of grains of rice. Each one attached to the substrate by a thin filament, and the structure was maybe six feet tall and still growing. He could see new branches forming at the top, pushing upward at a rate he could track second by second.

He opened his notebook.

Wrote: 0718.

Structure height: 6.2m. Growth rate: approximately 4cm per minute.

Egg density: high. Substrate composition: unknown. Not soil.

Not organic matter. Appears to be dimensional material.

Source: Rift.

The structure pulsed. Not a heartbeat. A signal.

He could feel it in his teeth, in the fillings, in the small bones of his inner ear.

A frequency. A broadcast.

He wrote: 0719.

Structure emitting signal. Frequency: unknown. Effect: perceptible to human nervous system.

Suggests communication or coordination.

The signal changed, and he felt it shift.

A new pattern. Different rhythm.

And then he understood.

The structure was broadcasting his location. His notebook entries.

His coordinates.

His time stamps. Everything he'd written down was being transmitted through the signal, encoded in the pulse pattern, sent to the Authority.

His manual documentation was not private.

It never had been. The Authority had designed the system so that any documentation method, licensed or unlicensed, fed back into the classification network.

Writing in a notebook was not an escape, which was a different input channel. Same data. Same destination.

He tore the page out. Crumpled it. Threw it on the ground.

The structure pulsed again. He could see the signal ripple outward from the top, a visible distortion in the air, like heat shimmer. It passed through him. And where it passed, the amber notification in his vision flickered.

003. Just the number. Updated.

His sub-entry was being revised. Every notebook entry he made was being incorporated into the Authority's file on him. Every observation he made about the world was being used to refine the Authority's understanding of what he was. And what he was, according to the system, was an infestation.

He picked up the crumpled page. Smoothed it. Read it. Then he did something he had never done in fourteen years of professional work.

He tore the page in half. Then in quarters. Then in eighths. He put the pieces in his mouth and swallowed them.

The notification flickered. The number changed.

004, and he was no longer Sub-Entry 003. He was Sub-Entry 004.

The Authority had updated its file.

The old entry was gone.

The new one was forming. And it was forming without his documentation because he'd destroyed it.

He stood in the parking lot with ink-stained teeth and watched the structure grow and felt the signal pass through him and realized that the Authority was not trying to exterminate him.

It was trying to understand him. And understanding was the first step in treatment.

His phone buzzed. Unknown number. He answered.

"Kowalski." A voice he didn't recognize. Calm. Procedural.

"This is the Licensing Authority. Your certification has been revoked.

You are now classified as a Class-IV infestation.

Treatment protocol has been assigned to Operator Yuki Tanaka, Certification Level VI. She is en route to your location."

Dale looked at the structure. Looked at the road. No one was coming. Not yet.

"You're using a licensed operator to treat me," he said.

"You are an infestation. Infestations are treated."

"I have a Field Guide."

"You have a notebook.

That is not a Field Guide. That is evidence of an unlicensed entity attempting to replicate Authority functions.

Which is itself a violation.

Which increases your classification tier."

Dale hung up. Opened his notebook. Wrote one line.

She's already here, and he looked up; the technician from the parking lot was standing at the edge of the commercial strip.

Watching.

Clipboard in hand.

Field Guide open.

The same technician. The one who'd told him the weakness notation.

They raised their pen.
