Chapter 300: Client Services Accepted

# Chapter 300

The wire sparked against the paper.

Smoke curled upward, acrid and thick, smelling of burnt plastic and old ozone. Dale held the glowing nib with steady fingers, fighting the tremor in his hand.

The heat was intense enough to blister his thumb, but he didn't pull away.

He couldn't. The report had to be legible. If the Authority's optical scanners couldn't read the text, the file would be marked *INCOMPLETE*.

An incomplete file was a nullification event.

Nullification meant death. Or worse, it meant erasure.

04:18 AM.

Fifty-four minutes remained.

Sarah watched him. She didn't speak. She just held the clipboard steady, her knuckles white against the cardboard. The darkness of the ruined basement pressed in, broken only by the jagged, blue-white arcs of the exposed electrical panel and the small, dying ember of the pen tip.

Dale wrote.

*SUBJECT: AUDITOR (CLASS IX ENTITY).* *STATUS: ACTIVE. CONTAINMENT FAILED.* *LOCATION: SECTOR 4, SUB-BASEMENT.*

The letters smudged, and the paper was slick with the green residue of the dead dimensional vermin. The heat from the wire wasn't enough to fuse the ink into the fibers properly; it just scorched the surface. Dale switched wires.

A thicker gauge. More current.

The tip glowed orange.

He pressed harder.

The Authority didn't want poetry.

It wanted data. It wanted the specific code for the entry point, the biological classification of the threat, and the protocol used to neutralize it.

But the Auditor wasn't a bug. It was a bureaucrat of the dark. How do you document a concept?

How do you isolate an idea that exists to audit existence?

Dale paused. The pen hovered.

If he wrote *Eliminated*, he was lying, and the Auditor had walked into the shadows and left. It hadn't died. It had retreated.

Under the System rules, a retreat was not an elimination.

A retreat was a failure to complete the protocol.

*PENDING.*

That word sat on the page, heavy and damning.

A sound echoed from the stairwell. Not footsteps. The hum of fluorescent lights turning on and off, out of sync with any electrical grid. A rhythmic, bureaucratic stutter.

Dale's neck muscles tightened. He kept writing.

*ISOLATION ATTEMPTED: SECTOR 4 LOCKDOWN.* *RESULT: BREACH DETECTED.* *CAUSE: UNLICENSED INTERVENTION BY AGENT 704.*

He looked up at the ceiling. The shadows were shifting. They weren't just absence of light anymore; they were moving with intent, pooling around the corners like oil.

"The audit is not a punishment," Sarah whispered. Her voice cracked. "It's a correction."

"Shut up," Dale said. He didn't look at her. He needed the silence to think. He needed to find the loophole.

The System rewarded thoroughness. It punished haste. But it also rewarded *precision*. If he couldn't eliminate the threat, he had to define it so thoroughly that the Authority had no choice but to classify it as a systemic error than a personal failure.

He needed to file a Counter-Report.

Not a report on the monster. A report on the Auditor.

*VIOLATION: PROCEDURAL NON-COMPLIANCE.* *DEPARTMENT: LICENSING AUTHORITY.* *CODE: O-147.*

Dale's hand moved faster. The wire sparked violently, sending a jolt of static up his arm. His teeth clenched. The pain was sharp, electric, grounding.

*SUBJECT: AUDITOR.* *ACTION: THREATENED TERMINATION OF LICENSED PERSONNEL PRIOR TO FINAL REVIEW.* *PROTOCOL VIOLATION: PREMATURE ERASURE.*

This was dangerous.

Filing a complaint against an Auditor was essentially admitting he had failed his job. But it was also a challenge. The Authority was rigid.

It obeyed its own rules. If an Auditor violated the protocol of *Fair Review*, they were technically out of order.

The hum in the stairwell grew louder. A voice echoed, dry as parchment.

*"Agent Kowalski. Your file is flagged."*

Dale didn't look up. He kept burning letters into the paper.

*JUSTIFICATION: SUBJECT FAILED TO PRESENT CREDENTIALS. SUBJECT FAILED TO FOLLOW CHAIN OF COMMAND. SUBJECT ENGAGED IN PSYCHOLOGICAL WARFARE PRIOR TO VERIFICATION PHASE.*

He underlined *PSYCHOLOGICAL WARFARE*. The wire snapped. The spark died.

04:25 AM.

Silence.

The darkness in the room seemed to hold its breath.

Dale looked at the paper, which was a mess of charred brown smears and half-formed glyphs.

It was barely readable. But it contained the words *Violation*, *Protocol*, and *Non-Compliance*.

It was enough. It was a spark in the dark.

He uncapped his backup pen. A standard ballpoint.

The ink was blue.

It wouldn't show up well on the darkened, wet paper. But he had something else.

He reached into his pocket and pulled out a small vial of industrial-grade solvent. It was meant for cleaning resin traps. It was corrosive. It ate through organic matter.

He poured a drop onto the paper.

The blue ink bled, spreading, darkening. The solvent reacted with the charred spots, making them stand out in stark, black relief against the pale, wet pulp. It wasn't elegant. It was desperate.

But it was visible.

"Submit," Dale said.

Sarah stared at him.

"It's not done."

"It's submitted," Dale corrected. "The intent is filed. The violation is noted. Now we wait."

The air in the room dropped ten degrees. Frost patterns spiderwebbed across the broken electrical panel.

From the shadows of the stairwell, a figure emerged. It was tall, impossibly thin, and dressed in a suit that seemed to be made of compressed dust. Its face was a blank slate, featureless save for two hollows where eyes should be.

The Auditor.

It floated down the stairs, its feet never touching the concrete. It stopped three feet from Dale.

*"You filed a challenge,"* the Auditor said. The voice didn't come from the face. It came from the walls. *"This is unprecedented."*

Dale wiped the green slime from his hand onto his pants. He kept his posture rigid, his shoulders square. He looked the entity in the void where its eyes were.

"The protocol requires a review," Dale said.

"You didn't review. You threatened.

That's a code violation.

I'm just pointing it out.

"

The Auditor tilted its head. A slow, grinding motion.

*"You believe you can argue with the infrastructure."*

"I believe," Dale said, his voice flat, "that the Authority values accuracy. If I'm wrong, erase me. If I'm right, you have a problem with your staffing."

The Auditor leaned forward. The cold radiating from it made Dale's breath hang in the air.

*"The Authority does not have problems. It has processes."*

"Then follow the process," Dale said. "Check the file.

Check the timestamp.

I submitted at 04:25.

You threatened erasure at 04:12. I had thirteen minutes to prepare a defense.

You gave me fifty-seven. That's inefficient.

"

The Auditor paused. The dust suit rustled.

*"Inefficiency is a relative term."*

"It's measurable," Dale said. He tapped the clipboard.

"I'm asking for a Thoroughness re-evaluation.

If my report is accepted, the threat of erasure is voided by procedural error. If you erase me, you admit the error. If you don't, you have to admit my report is valid.

"

It was a bluff.

A massive, suicidal bluff. But Dale knew how bureaucracies worked.

They feared liability more than they feared chaos.

Admitting a procedural error meant a new audit of the Auditor itself. That meant paperwork. That meant accountability.

The Auditor stared at the burning paper. The blue ink was still spreading, turning the word *VIOLATION* into a black blob.

*"You are a Level II Technician,"* the Auditor said. *"You do not have the clearance to challenge a Class IX Entity."*

"I have the clearance to file a report," Dale said. "And I filed it. Did you read it?"

The Auditor's head snapped down. It looked at the paper.

For a second, the room was silent. The hum of the dying lights faded. The cold intensified.

Then, the Auditor laughed. It was a dry, rattling sound, like pages being torn.

*"You are clever, Dale Kowalski. But cleverness is not thoroughness."*

The Auditor raised a hand. The shadows in the room surged forward, wrapping around Dale's ankles. They felt like ice.

*"Your challenge is noted. But the clock is still ticking.

You have not verified the elimination of the initial threat.

The rat-king infestation is still active in Sector 5.

Your license is under review. You are not cleared to leave this room.

"*

Dale's heart hammered against his ribs. He looked at Sarah. She was shaking, but she wasn't crying anymore. She was ready.

"I didn't say I was leaving," Dale said. "I said I filed the report. If you're not erasing me, then the audit is paused.

That means I'm still on duty. And I'm not done.

"

*"You are detained,"* the Auditor said. *"Your tools are confiscated."*

The shadows tightened. The clipboard was ripped from Sarah's hands. It dissolved into dust before it hit the floor.

Dale felt a sharp pain in his chest. His Field Guide interface, usually a subtle presence in his mind, flared with a red warning.

**SYSTEM ALERT: PROTOCOL SUSPENSION.** **ACCESS TO FIELD GUIDE: REVOKED.** **ACCESS TO TREATMENT POINTS: REVOKED.**

He was cut off.

No more data. No more weakness analysis. No more targeted bonuses.

He was just a man in a dark room with a dead rat and a broken pen.

"Without the Guide," the Auditor said, "you are merely an observer. And observers do not get points. They get filed."

Dale looked at the ground. The green slime from the first rat was still there.

And next to it, a trail of mud.

Fresh mud. From outside.

Someone else had been in the basement. Someone had come down the stairs while Dale was writing.

He looked at the trail. It wasn't rat tracks. They were too large. Too heavy.

And they were wet.

"Who else is here?" Dale asked.

The Auditor didn't answer. It just watched.

Dale stepped back, away from the shadows gripping his legs. He kicked out, breaking the hold.

It wasn't physical force; it was a disruption of intent, and he remembered the rule: *Intent drives Protocol.* If he didn't fear the shadows, they couldn't hold him.

He didn't fear them. He was annoyed by them.

He looked at the mud trail leading to the broken wall where the dimensional rift had first opened.

The rift was supposed to be sealed. Dale had sealed it with duct tape and a prayer. But the mud was fresh. And it smelled of copper.

"Sarah," Dale said, keeping his voice low. "Get behind me."

She moved.

Dale picked up the broken electrical panel. It weighed ten pounds. It was jagged, sharp, and connected to live wires that were still sparking intermittently.

He wasn't licensed. He had no Field Guide. He had no Treatment Points.

But he had a job to do.

"Identification," Dale muttered to himself.

He looked at the rift. The darkness there wasn't empty. It was pulsing.

Something was pushing through. Not a rat.

Not a beetle.

A hand. Pale, elongated, with too many joints.

**NEW THREAT DETECTED.** **CLASSIFICATION: UNKNOWN.** **RECOMMENDED PROTOCOL: NONE.**

The text appeared in the air, burning in gold. It wasn't from the Authority. It was from the rift itself. A message from the other side.

*HELP US.*

Dale froze.

The Auditor stopped moving. Its head turned toward the rift.

*"Interference,"* the Auditor said. *"The breach is not closed. It is being... renegotiated."*

Dale looked at the hand. It was trying to pull itself out. It wasn't attacking. It was reaching.

He looked at Sarah. She was staring at the hand, her mouth open.

"Dale," she whispered. "That looks like..."

"It doesn't matter what it looks like," Dale said. He gripped the electrical panel. "It's an infestation. And it's breaching a containment zone without a permit."

He stepped forward. The shadows recoiled. The Auditor's suit fluttered, agitated.

*"Step back, Technician. That is not your jurisdiction."*

"Everything is my jurisdiction if it's in my service area," Dale said. He raised the panel.

The live wire sparked. He aimed it not at the hand, but at the rift itself.

If he couldn't eliminate the threat, he could disrupt the entry point. He could short-circuit the dimensional bridge. It was a violation of safety codes. It was stupid.

But it was the only tool he had left.

"Sarah, hold the wire," Dale said.

She didn't argue. She grabbed the other end of the panel's cable.

"On my mark," Dale said.

The hand in the rift twisted. It grabbed the concrete edge of the hole. The concrete cracked.

The Auditor lunged, not at Dale, but at the rift. It tried to seal it with a wave of dust and void.

Dale didn't wait for the mark. He jammed the sparking panel into the rift.

The world went white.

A scream tore through the basement. Not from Sarah. Not from Dale.

From the thing on the other side.

The light faded. The smoke cleared.

The rift was gone.

In its place was a hole in the wall. And in the hole, impaled on a jagged piece of rebar, was a single, pale finger. It twitched.

Dale looked at the finger. He looked at the Auditor.

The Auditor was staring at the finger. Its featureless face seemed to be frowning.

*"That,"* the Auditor said, *"was not authorized."*

"It stopped the breach," Dale said. He was breathing hard. His hands were shaking.

*"It created a new entry point,"* the Auditor said. *"The finger is a beacon. Something is coming through it. Something that cannot be deleted."*

A low growl emanated from the hole. The finger turned. It pointed at Dale.

**SYSTEM ALERT: ESCALATION DETECTED.** **NEW RIVAL IDENTIFIED: THE BROKER.** **STATUS: ACTIVE.**

Dale's heart sank. The Broker. A Class IX Entity.

A collector.

A negotiator. It wasn't a monster.

It was a middleman.

The Auditor looked at Dale.

*"You have saved your license, Dale Kowalski. But you have opened a door that cannot be closed."*

The Auditor turned and walked away. It didn't attack. It just left.

Dale stood there, holding the dead electrical panel. The finger twitched again.

Sarah looked at him.

"What did you just do?"

Dale looked at the finger. He looked at the empty space where the Auditor had been.

"I filed a complaint," Dale said. "And I think I just started a war."

The finger's tip peeled back. Underneath, in the bone, was a tiny, glowing rune. It matched the code for *Contract*.

Dale realized then that the Auditor hadn't left because it was afraid. It had left because it was done. The audit was over. The penalty had been assessed.

The penalty wasn't death, and the penalty was that Dale was now responsible for whatever came through that hole.

The rune pulsed. A voice, smooth and oily, echoed in Dale's mind.

*"Thank you for the introduction, Mr. Kowalski. I accept your services."*

Dale looked at the clipboard. It was gone.

He looked at the finger, and he had no report. He had no points. He had no guide.

He had a client.

And clients always wanted results.
