Chapter 58: Certifying Fraud

# Chapter 58

Dale was writing the objection form by hand.

He'd torn a page from the notebook and drawn the standard Authority administrative header from memory. Section 4.1, Peer Review Discrepancy. He knew the format because he'd filed one against a technician in Dayton who'd misidentified a Class-3 acid beetle as a Class-2 variant, contaminating his Field Guide for a full day. That form had taken him eleven minutes to fill out and had earned him a 0.3 Thoroughness bonus on his next job because the Authority valued peer accountability.

The organism didn't know that.

Dale wrote the header. Wrote his certification number.

Wrote the provisional certification number he'd seen on the notification screen, which he'd photographed with his phone before the toolkit lockout kicked in.

Wrote the species designation: Unidentified, pending. Wrote the violation category:

CERTIFICATION FRAUD. UNAUTHORIZED PEER INTEGRATION.

He underlined it twice, and the problem was submission. The Authority's administrative portal was part of the toolkit. His toolkit was locked. He'd tried the portal three times already and gotten the same grey screen with the words PENDING AUDIT stamped across it like a rejection notice.

He sat back. Looked at the form. Looked at the phone.

The call had ended, and the voice was gone. No caller ID.

No callback option.

Dale didn't know who had called him and he didn't have the bandwidth to figure it out. What he had was a form, a certification number, and a system that was in the process of certifying something that shouldn't exist.

He thought about how the Authority processed administrative submissions. He'd been certified now for six weeks. In that time he'd filed forty-three job reports, three peer review objections, two equipment requisition forms, and one complaint about a Class-4 broodmother that had nested inside a condemned water treatment plant outside of Akron. Every single one had gone through the same pipeline: submission, indexing, review, response.

The indexing system was where the organism lived. That's what the voice had said. The organism was in the way the Authority sorted information. It was in the logic.

So the organism would see his form. It would read his objection. And it would respond.

That was the point.

Dale picked up his phone. Opened his camera app. Photographed the handwritten form from three angles, and then he did something he hadn't done since before the Rift: he opened his email.

He'd been a municipal technician for fourteen years. Before the Authority, before the certification, before any of it, he'd filed code violation reports through the county's online portal.

The county portal was still up.

The county still existed, and the county had no idea that the Authority existed, and the Authority had no jurisdiction over county municipal code enforcement.

But the Authority had absorbed the county's pest control division in week two. Every licensed technician in the county had received the Authority's notification simultaneously. Dale's old supervisor, Margaret Chen, had been absorbed too, and she was now a Class-4 technician in the Authority's regional office in Columbus.

He knew her certification number. He'd memorized it because she'd once submitted a job report under his name by accident and he'd had to file a correction.

He emailed Margaret. Attached the photographed form. Wrote three sentences.

Peer review objection. See attached. Filing through you because my portal is locked.

This is a code violation. I'm requesting you process it as a municipal administrative submission and forward it to the Authority's indexing layer.

He hit send.

Then he waited.

Twenty minutes, and he made coffee. He drank it black because he couldn't find the creamer and he didn't want to open the fridge because the fridge light made him think about the crawlspace job in week three where something had nested behind the ice maker. He didn't open the fridge.

He drank black coffee and stared at the kitchen wall.

The phone buzzed.

Margaret had replied. One line.

"Processing. Don't call me for three days."

He didn't know what that meant. He didn't want to know. He went back to the notebook.

The organism was being certified. That meant it was taking the written exam.

The written exam had forty questions covering identification, protocol selection, containment procedures, and documentation standards.

Dale had scored a 94 on his own exam; the organism, living inside the indexing system, would have access to every answer. It would score a hundred.

But the exam wasn't the only gate, and he remembered the practical component. The Authority's practical exam required the candidate to demonstrate a full extermination protocol on a live specimen.

Not a simulation.

A real creature, in a real environment, with real contamination risk.

The Authority provided the specimen. The Authority provided the environment.

The candidate provided the procedure.

If the organism was being certified, it had to pass a practical exam. Which meant the Authority was going to put a real creature in front of it.

Which meant the organism would have to interact with something outside the indexing system.

Something physical. Something that could be documented, identified, and eliminated.

Dale started writing again. This time he wasn't filling out a form. He was building a timeline.

The practical exam would be scheduled. The Authority would assign a proctor. The proctor would be a certified technician. The proctor would be present during the examination.

If the organism failed the practical, its certification would be denied. Denied certification meant it remained an infestation. An infestation could be treated.

If the organism passed the practical, it became a technician, and a technician had access to the toolkit. The toolkit included the ability to file job reports, request equipment, and assign subordinates.

The organism would be able to dispatch other technicians into the field.

It would be able to write its own job reports. It would be able to certify other things.

Dale wrote this down. Underlined it. Drew a box around it.

He needed the organism to fail.

But he couldn't be the proctor. His toolkit was locked. He couldn't access the exam scheduling system. He couldn't volunteer.

Unless he filed a second objection, and he started writing. Peer review objection, second submission.

This one wasn't about the organism's certification.

It was about the Authority's examination process itself.

He was alleging a procedural irregularity: the Authority had not specified a proctor for the pending practical examination. He was requesting that the Authority assign a qualified technician to observe and evaluate the candidate's performance.

He was requesting a proctor.

And he was requesting that the proctor be someone who knew what a real infestation looked like. Someone who could tell the difference between a protocol performed correctly and a protocol performed by something that understood the words but not the meaning.

He knew what that looked like. He'd seen it. The technician in Dayton who'd misidentified the beetle had performed the protocol perfectly.

Every step in the right order. Every documentation field filled.

But he'd missed the frass trail.

He'd missed the entry point. He'd treated the surface and left the nest. The job was technically complete.

The infestation returned in six weeks.

The organism would do the same thing. It would perform the protocol.

It would document the steps.

It would fill every field. But it wouldn't see the frass trail because it didn't understand what the frass trail meant. It didn't understand the smell of a nest.

It failed to grasp how a broodmother's territory formed around a moisture source. It was a logic error.

It could parse the rules.

It couldn't feel the weight of them.

Dale photographed the second form. Emailed it to Margaret.

Then he sat down and waited again.

This time it took four hours, and the notification came through his phone's lock screen. Not the Authority's interface. A standard push notification, the kind that came through before the toolkit was integrated, and it looked like a text message.

PEER REVIEW ACCEPTED. PRACTICAL EXAMINATION PROCTOR ASSIGNED: KOWALSKI, D.

He read it twice.

His toolkit was still locked. He couldn't access the exam.

He couldn't see the specimen. He couldn't see the environment. He was the proctor but he had no tools.

Then a second notification.

PRACTICAL EXAMINATION SCHEDULED: 0600 TOMORROW. LOCATION: AUTHORITY REGIONAL ARCHIVE, SUBLEVEL 3.

CANDIDATE: PROVISIONAL TECHNICIAN [CLASS IX].

PROCTOR: KOWALSKI, D. PROCTOR TOOLKIT STATUS: SUSPENDED.

Class IX, and he read that again.

Class IX. The highest certification level.

He was Class II.

He'd been certified for six weeks and he was Class II. Class IX meant the organism had been operating at the highest tier of the Authority's system. It meant it had been certified, or functionally certified, for longer than Dale had been in the system at all.

It meant the organism wasn't new. It hadn't crawled out of the Rift last month.

It had been inside the Authority's infrastructure for years.

Decades.

Maybe longer.

And the Authority had been certifying it. Slowly. One level at a time.

Not because it was passing exams. Because it had been working the jobs.

Filing the reports.

Completing the protocols. For years.

Dale looked at his phone. At the notification. At the word SUSPENDED.

He was being sent into the Authority's archive as a proctor with no toolkit, no protocols, no access, to evaluate something that had been working inside the system longer than he'd been alive.

He stood up.

Walked to the kitchen drawer. Took out a pencil.

A mechanical pencil, 0.7mm, with a rubber grip.

He'd used it for fourteen years. It wasn't a protocol tool. It wasn't in the toolkit.

It was just a pencil, and he put it in his shirt pocket.

Then he picked up the notebook. The blank one. He tore out a fresh page and wrote at the top:

EXAMINATION PROCTOR NOTES. CANDIDATE: CLASS IX PROVISIONAL.

He wrote the date. The time. The location.

He didn't know what he was going to find in Sublevel 3. He didn't know what the specimen would be. He didn't know if he'd survive the evaluation.

But he knew one thing.

If the organism had been filing job reports for years, it had been earning Thoroughness Ratings.

And Thoroughness Ratings were public. They were indexed.

They were in the system, and he opened his phone again. He couldn't access the Authority's portal.

But he could access the county's public records database.

The county still had the old pest control reports. The ones from before the Rift. The ones that had been digitized and uploaded to the municipal server.

He searched for the name on the certification number. The provisional one. The one he'd photographed.

The search returned zero results in the county database, and he searched for the certification number itself.

One result.

A job report.

Dated 1987. Filed under the county's pest control division.

Location: a residential property on Elm Street in Dayton, Ohio.

Species identified: Rattus profundus. Dimensional Vermin.

The report was signed by a technician whose name was redacted. The Thoroughness Rating was 9.8.

Dale stared at the screen.

1987. The Rift hadn't opened yet.

He'd been eleven years old.

The Authority hadn't made itself known.

No one had been certified.

But someone had filed this report. Someone had identified a Dimensional Vermin and treated it and documented it and earned a 9.8 Thoroughness Rating.

The organism had been doing this job for thirty-seven years.

His phone buzzed. Margaret.

"Proctor assignment confirmed. One more thing. The Authority flagged your own file for irregularity.

They're auditing your first six weeks. If they find anything, your certification gets revoked before the exam.

You'll be unlicensed in the room with a Class IX."

He set the phone down, and he looked at the pencil in his pocket.

He looked at the notebook.

He looked at the clock. Five hours until the exam.

He wrote the first practice answer and timed himself.
