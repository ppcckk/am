Chapter 53: Ink Rewrites Authority

# Chapter 53

The pen felt cold, unrelated to temperature, and it held the still cold of a dead animal, finished and waiting to be seen.

Dale looked at the words on his clipboard. His handwriting on the first line. Something else's on the second. The second line was still wet, still moving, a thin capillary of ink crawling deeper into the paper fiber like a root system finding water.

He set the pen down. Picked it up. Set it down again.

The operator was gone. Not dead.

Gone.

The chair sat empty. Its cushion held a fading impression, as if the operator had never fully sat there, and the room around Dale was contracting. Not physically.

The walls weren't moving.

But the space between things was getting smaller, like someone was editing the room's margins.

His Field Guide opened.

Not by his hand.

It snapped open to the Authority entry, the one he'd written three minutes ago. The text was rewriting itself. His words were being replaced, one by one, by a different classification.

He watched his own handwriting curl and vanish. Watched a new entry populate in a font that looked like the Authority's standard notification text but was slightly wrong, like a photocopy of a photocopy.

*Authority.

Classification: Class IX.

Status: Terminal. Weakness: Dale Kowalski.

Treatment Protocol: Self-Referential Elimination. Method: Document the technician as the weakness. Have the technician accept the documentation.

Technician accepts documentation. Technician becomes the report.

Report is filed.

Report is closed. Case closed.*

Dale read it twice, understanding it as one understands a rental unit's cockroach problem.

You don't need to understand the biology. You need to understand the pathway.

Something had found a pathway into the Authority, and the pathway was Dale.

Not because Dale was strong. Because Dale was thorough. Because Dale wrote things down.

And writing things down was how you made a problem smaller than yourself, and the Authority had made that instinct into a system, and something else had made that system into a weapon.

His Treatment Points were dropping.

He watched the number in his peripheral vision slide down like a fuel gauge on a car with a cracked tank. 14,200. 14,190. 14,175.

The deductions were small, consistent, procedural. They looked like routine administrative adjustments.

They looked like a technician correcting a filing error.

His Certification Level flickered. IX. Then IX with a strikethrough. Then just IX again, but fainter, like a watermark being erased.

He pulled out his second clipboard. The one with the real paper. The one that had nothing on it except the two lines he'd written.

He started a new entry.

*Violation type: Unauthorized treatment of a licensed entity. Performed by: Unknown.

Performed on: Authority (Class IX).

Method: Exploitation of self-referential documentation pathway. Jurisdiction: Unknown.*

He underlined "Jurisdiction."

The room shuddered. The space between things stopped shrinking. Not because he'd fixed anything. Because he'd asked a question.

"Who has jurisdiction over a Class IX treatment?" he said aloud.

The answer came from everywhere and nowhere, in the Authority's flat bureaucratic tone. "The Authority."

"Then the Authority has jurisdiction over the thing that's treating it."

"Correct."

"Then the thing treating it is operating without a license."

A pause.

Longer than the Authority usually paused. The Authority didn't pause.

The Authority processed.

But this was a pause. This was something thinking.

"Correct."

Dale wrote: *Unauthorized treatment. No license.

No Field Guide entry for the treating entity on file with the Authority.

Therefore: the treating entity is operating in a jurisdiction it does not recognize.

Filing formal complaint under Protocol 9.7.1: Unlicensed Treatment of a Licensed Entity. Requesting jurisdictional review.*

He signed it. His own signature. Not the Authority's.

Not the system's. His own signature, like on ten thousand reports, or the violation code on that duplex fourteen years ago, when a dog-sized rat dragged a tenant into the walls.

The effect was immediate and not what he expected, and the Authority did not fight back. The Authority did not attack the entity treating it. The Authority did something far worse. It began investigating.

Dale felt it happen.

It felt like the moment you pull up to a job site and realize the homeowner has already called three other companies and the problem is now a three-way jurisdictional dispute. The Authority's attention shifted. Not toward Dale.

Not toward the thing treating it.

Toward the paperwork. The Authority was a bureaucracy, and a bureaucracy confronted with an unlicensed operator does not become a warrior.

It becomes an auditor.

The room stopped contracting. The operator's chair was still empty, but the impression in the cushion was stable now, frozen, a fossil record of something that had been real.

Dale's Treatment Points stopped dropping, and they didn't recover. They just stopped. Like a patient in a hospital who's been bleeding and the bleeding has been stopped but nobody's done anything else yet.

He looked at his Field Guide. The Authority entry was still there. Still being rewritten.

The process hadn't reversed. It had just slowed.

The Authority was still being treated, which was still being exterminated.

But now it was also investigating the exterminator, and that investigation was consuming resources that would otherwise have gone to the treatment.

A race. The thing treating the Authority was trying to finish its protocol before the Authority finished its investigation. And Dale was the weakness either way.

If the Authority finished the investigation and identified the treating entity, it might be able to defend itself. If the treating entity finished its protocol first, the Authority died and the system died and Dale lost everything.

The Authority's investigation had a problem.

Dale saw it like a frass trail.

Like the dark pellets marking the nest's location; the Authority was investigating an entity that had no Field Guide entry. It had no entry because it wasn't a creature.

It was a technician, which had a protocol. It had a certification.

It had a clipboard.

The Authority could not investigate something that was also a technician. Because the Authority's investigative protocols were built for infestations. They were built for creatures. They were not built for peers.

Dale wrote: *Investigation will fail. The treating entity is not an infestation. It is a licensed operator.

The Authority has no protocol for investigating a peer. Jurisdictional review will return null.

The treating entity will continue its protocol unimpeded.*

He looked at what he'd written.

Then he looked at the Authority entry in his Field Guide. The rewriting had slowed but not stopped.

The Authority was still dying. Slowly. Like a building being condemned one violation at a time.

Only one way existed to give the Authority a peer investigation protocol.

Dale had to write one, and he had to write a new protocol. A protocol for investigating another technician. A protocol that the Authority would recognize as valid, would adopt as its own, and would then use against the thing that was killing it.

He knew how to write protocols. He'd been writing them his whole life.

Every inspection report was a protocol.

Every violation code was a protocol. The system had just formalized what he'd always done.

He started writing.

*Protocol 9.7.2: Peer Investigation. Applicable when: Unlicensed or licensed operator is performing treatment on a licensed entity without proper jurisdictional authority. Steps: (1) Identify the operator. (2) Request their Field Guide entry for the target entity. (3) If the entry exists, verify its accuracy against the Authority's own records. (4) If the entry is inaccurate or fabricated, the operator is in violation of Protocol 3.1: False Documentation. (5) If the entry is accurate, the operator is performing a lawful treatment and the investigation ends.*

He underlined step four.

The Authority read it, and he could feel it reading. The words on his clipboard glowed faintly, a warm amber, the color of a certification seal.

The Authority was processing the protocol.

Adopting it. Making it its own.

Then it used it.

The Authority turned its attention to the thing treating it, and the thing that had no name, no face, no form that Dale could perceive.

But the Authority had a protocol now.

It could request the thing's Field Guide entry for the Authority.

And if that entry was inaccurate, if it was fabricated, if it was wrong in any way that the Authority's own records contradicted, then the thing was in violation.

The room went silent. The kind of silent that happens when you pull a wall panel off and find something you didn't expect.

The Authority found the entry.

Dale saw it appear in his peripheral vision, formatted like a Field Guide page, but not his Field Guide. Not any Field Guide he'd ever seen. It was the treating entity's guide.

The Authority had reached into it. Read it.

Pulled it out like a technician pulling a label off a chemical container to check the active ingredient.

The entry was accurate.

The thing treating the Authority had a perfect, complete, verified Field Guide entry for the Authority. Every weakness documented. Every protocol noted.

Every vulnerability mapped, having studied the Authority as Dale studied a nest. Thoroughly.

Patiently.

For a long time.

The Authority's investigation returned: NO VIOLATION.

The Authority had no grounds to stop the treatment, and it had no protocol for fighting a peer who was doing the job correctly. It had no defense against a technician who was better than it was.

Dale stared at the words. NO VIOLATION.

He'd written the protocol, and the Authority had used it.

And the protocol had worked exactly as written. It had found no violation because there was no violation.

The thing killing the Authority was doing a perfect job.

He looked at his clipboard. At his own handwriting. At the line he'd written at the start: *Weakness: The technician who wrote the report.*

He understood now. The treating entity wasn't trying to kill him.

It wasn't trying to use him, which was doing something worse; was treating the Authority the way Dale treated a nest. It had identified the nest.

It had identified the queen.

And the queen was the Authority itself. And the Authority's weakness was the technician who wrote the report, because the Authority was built on reports.

It was built on documentation, which was a bureaucracy, and a bureaucracy's weakness is always the person who does the paperwork.

The Authority was not being exterminated by a monster. It was being exterminated by a better technician.

Dale's Treatment Points dropped again. Faster now.

The Authority was accelerating its own death, trying to close the gap between the treating entity's protocol and its own investigation.

It was trying to finish dying before the thing could finish killing it.

A race between completion and termination, and he looked at his Field Guide; the Authority entry was almost fully rewritten. His words were almost gone. The Authority was almost gone.

He had one option. One protocol he could still deploy. One thing he could do that the treating entity hadn't documented, hadn't anticipated, hadn't put in its Field Guide.

He could de-certify himself.

If the Authority's weakness was the technician who wrote the report, and the technician who wrote the report was Dale, then if Dale was no longer a technician, the Authority had no weakness. The protocol would fail. The treating entity's Field Guide entry would become inaccurate.

And if the entry became inaccurate, the Authority's Protocol 9.7.2 would trigger. False Documentation.

Violation.

The Authority would have grounds to stop the treatment.

But de-certification meant losing everything. Every protocol. Every Field Guide entry.

Every Treatment Point.

Every certification level. Everything the Authority had given him.

He'd be a man with a clipboard and a pen and fourteen years of instinct, which was all. That was what he'd started with.

He picked up the pen.

The pen was warm again.

He wrote: *I, Dale Kowalski, Certification Level IX, hereby petition for voluntary de-certification under Protocol 1.4: Surrender of License.*

He signed it.

The Authority read it.

The Authority hesitated.

And then, in the space between the hesitation and the decision, Dale saw something he hadn't expected. The Authority's response wasn't a confirmation. It wasn't a denial. It was a counter-offer.

*De-certification request denied. Reason: Technician is currently engaged in an active investigation. Protocol 1.4 does not apply during active cases. Technician is required to complete the case before de-certification can be processed.*

Dale stared at the words, and the Authority was trapping him; it was using its own rules to keep him in place.

To keep him as the weakness.

To keep him as the technician who wrote the report.

Because as long as he was an active technician, the treating entity's protocol worked. And the Authority needed him to be the weakness, because the Authority's only hope was that Dale would find a way to be the weakness and also the solution.

He looked at the treating entity's Field Guide entry. The one the Authority had pulled out. The one that was perfect. The one that had no errors, no gaps, no oversights.

He read it again. And this time he noticed something.

The entry was perfect. Too perfect. It documented the Authority's weakness with a precision that went beyond what any technician could achieve through observation alone.

It didn't describe the Authority. It described the Authority the way the Authority described itself.

The treating entity had the Authority's own Field Guide.

Not a copy.

Not a reconstruction. The original. The one the Authority kept in its own records.

The one no technician had ever seen.

The thing killing the Authority was not a rival technician, which was the Authority's first technician. The one who had written the original report. The one who had created the system. The one who had written the Authority into existence.

And it was filing a closing report.

Dale's pen moved before he decided to move it. His hand wrote words he hadn't thought. The Authority's system pulled them out of him the way a vacuum pulls frass from a wall cavity.

*Case status: Active. Technician: Dale Kowalski.

Opposing technician: Unknown.

Requesting: Access to the original Field Guide entry for the Authority. Requesting: Permission to amend it.*

The Authority processed the request.

The answer came in a line, in the Authority's flat bureaucratic tone, in a font that was slightly wrong, like a photocopy of a photocopy.

*Access granted. Amendment window: 90 seconds. After 90 seconds, the closing report will be filed and the case will be closed.*

Dale looked at his clipboard. At the pen. At the space where the original entry would appear.

He had ninety seconds to write a weakness into the Authority that the first technician had never found. A weakness that the Authority itself didn't know it had. A weakness that would make the closing report inaccurate.

He wrote the weakness down and marked the report INCOMPLETE.
