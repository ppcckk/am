Chapter 19: Unknown Field Entry

# Chapter 19

The grate shifted three inches and stopped. Something under it pushed back with a slow, grinding pressure that Dale felt through the wrench handle. He didn't pull harder. He set the wrench down and crouched.

The pulse under the asphalt was wrong. It had been rhythmic before, a steady throb that matched the nest's respiration cycle he'd logged in his Field Guide.

Now it was syncopated.

Two rhythms overlapping. One from below, one from the side.

He pressed his palm flat against the grate. Felt the vibration travel through the iron and into his wrist, and the secondary rhythm was coming from the pipe direction. Downstream.

He pulled his clipboard from his belt and flipped to the isolation page.

Drew a quick sketch of the drain layout from memory. The grate sat at the street's low point, feeding into a six-inch storm pipe that ran forty feet to the nearest catch basin.

He'd sealed the four secondary entry points along the street surface.

Cracks in the sidewalk. A utility penetration near the mailbox. Two gaps where the curb had separated from the asphalt.

All Class-1 breaches. All documented.

All sealed with fire-rated sealant.

But the nest wasn't in the street. It was in the pipe.

He stood.

Walked to the nearest manhole cover, twenty yards east, and it was a standard municipal cast-iron lid, twenty-four inches across, bolted at three points.

He'd passed it without checking. Why had he passed it? Because the Authority's initial scan had flagged the street surface breaches, not the subsurface infrastructure.

The system had pointed him at the easy targets, and he pulled his wrench from his belt and approached the manhole.

The bolts were newer here. Galvanized. He turned the first one in six seconds.

The second in eight.

The third seized. He applied penetrating oil from his kit, waited ninety seconds, then broke it free.

The lid came up with a sucking sound. The smell hit him first. Ammonia and something sweet, like overripe fruit left in a closed car. He angled his flashlight into the opening.

The pipe was alive.

Not alive, unlike the nest.

The walls of the pipe were coated in a pale, waxy substance that pulsed with the same secondary rhythm he'd felt through the grate. It looked like candle wax mixed with something fibrous. Egg cases.

Dozens of them, packed into the pipe's interior surface in overlapping rows.

He counted them. Forty-three. Maybe more, hidden in the curve of the pipe beyond his flashlight's reach.

His Field Guide pinged. A notification he'd never seen before, formatted differently from the standard treatment alerts. The text was red.

INFRASTRUCTURE COMPROMISE DETECTED. Secondary infestation vector identified: municipal drainage system. This job now carries a Cascade Risk flag. Failure to address secondary vectors within the treatment window will result in automatic thoroughness deduction and potential reassignment.

He read it twice.

Then he looked at his watch, and the treatment window was the time between initial identification and final verification. He'd identified the nest fourteen hours ago.

The window was twenty-four. He had ten hours left.

He pulled out his sealant can. Checked the nozzle.

Checked the sealant's viscosity rating.

Standard fire-rated compound was rated for vertical surfaces and horizontal gaps up to two inches wide. The pipe was six inches in diameter. The waxy coating was three inches thick in places.

Standard sealant wouldn't adhere to a curved surface under moisture conditions.

He'd need a different product, and he didn't have one.

He looked at the egg cases.

They were clustered near the pipe's crown, the highest point of the interior curve. Below them, the pipe floor was dry. The wax was only on the upper half.

That meant the nest had built upward from the water line. Which meant the water line was the boundary.

Below it, the nest hadn't gone.

Above it, it had, and he could flood the pipe. Push the water line up and drown the egg cases.

The Authority's protocols for aquatic infestations were in his toolkit, but he'd never used them. He'd never needed them. His service area was suburban Ohio, not the Everglades.

He checked his toolkit. He had a portable pump, rated for residential water removal. He had a garden hose, fifty feet. He had a shut-off valve for the storm main, which meant he could isolate the pipe section and fill it from the manhole.

He didn't have a way to get water into the pipe fast enough to reach the egg cases before the nest sealed them off. The wax was a barrier. If he flooded slowly, the nest would sense the pressure change and seal the cases with additional wax. He'd seen that behavior in the Field Guide entry for Rattus profundus, under the subheading "defensive adaptations." The species could harden its egg cases into a waterproof shell within ninety seconds of detecting a threat to the brood.

Ninety seconds. He needed to fill forty feet of pipe with enough water to reach the crown in under ninety seconds.

He did the math. Six-inch pipe, forty feet long.

Roughly five hundred gallons.

His pump moved forty gallons per minute. He'd need twelve minutes. Way too slow.

He needed a different approach, and he sat on the curb and looked at his Field Guide.

The entry for Rattus profundus listed seventeen documented behaviors. Defensive waxing was one.

Tunneling was another.

Aggressive territorial response was a third. None of them addressed the specific problem of flooding a pipe containing egg cases.

Then he remembered something. The Field Guide had a second page for the species, a page he'd barely glanced at when it populated back in Chapter 3. He flipped to it.

The second page was blank except for one line of text at the top: BEHAVIORAL OBSERVATIONS (USER-LOGGED). Below it, empty space.

He'd never filled it in. The system gave you the base entry, but you could add your own observations. He'd been too busy to do it. Now he looked at the pipe and started writing.

The nest builds upward from the water line. Egg cases are clustered at the pipe crown.

The wax is a moisture barrier, not a structural barrier.

It's designed to keep water out from above, not to resist pressure from below.

The nest assumes threats come from the surface. It doesn't defend against flooding from the pipe floor.

He wrote that down. The Field Guide pinged. A notification appeared.

BEHAVIORAL OBSERVATION LOGGED. Targeted damage bonus applied: flooding protocols against Rattus profundus egg cases now deal 180% effectiveness. Bonus expires in 4 hours.

He stared at the number. 180%. He'd never gotten a bonus that high; the targeted bonuses from his existing Field Guide entries were usually 15 to 30 percent. This was six times that.

Because the bonus was specific. Not just "flooding works against rats." Flooding works against the egg cases specifically, from below, because the nest doesn't defend against it. The system had rewarded him for identifying a gap in the creature's defenses.

He stood up. Pulled his pump from the truck. Connected it to the garden hose.

Ran the hose to the manhole. Fed the end into the pipe until it hit the floor.

Started the pump, and the water went in. He watched the level rise. The wax coating didn't react to the water immediately.

The nest hadn't detected the threat yet.

The water was below the wax line, in the dry zone, and the nest's sensors were tuned to surface vibration, not subsurface pressure changes.

He pumped faster.

The pump screamed. Forty gallons per minute. The water line climbed.

Thirty seconds. Forty seconds.

The wax coating started to glisten as the water reached it.

The pulse under the asphalt changed again.

Faster. The nest knew.

Fifty seconds. The water touched the lowest egg cases. The surrounding wax darkened and hardened. The ninety-second timer started.

Sixty seconds. The water reached the middle cases. They were hardening too.

But the water was already around them. The hardening was too late.

The cases were sealed, but they were underwater.

Seventy seconds. The water reached the crown. All forty-three cases submerged.

The wax was fully hardened, a perfect waterproof shell around each one.

But the water was already inside the shell's boundary. The cases were underwater.

He kept pumping.

Ninety seconds. One hundred seconds. Two minutes.

The water overflowed the manhole and ran down the street. He let it run for another minute, making sure the pipe was full, then shut off the pump.

He pulled his clipboard and started writing the verification report.

Egg case count: forty-three.

Treatment method: controlled flooding from subsurface access point. Duration of submersion: three minutes minimum.

Expected mortality: complete.

The Authority pinged.

VERIFICATION PENDING. Flooding protocol requires 24-hour observation period before mortality can be confirmed.

Job status: INCOMPLETE.

Thoroughness rating on file: 6.1. Cascade Risk flag active.

Twenty-four hours, and he couldn't verify the kill for twenty-four hours. The treatment window was ten hours.

He'd have to wait.

The job would go incomplete unless he found a way to verify faster.

He looked at the pipe. The water was still there, standing in the curve.

The egg cases were submerged but intact.

If he pulled the water out now, they might survive.

If he left it, they'd drown. But he couldn't confirm they were dead without pulling the water and checking.

He needed a way to check without draining the pipe, and he pulled out his flashlight. Shined it down the manhole. The water was murky, brown with sediment.

He couldn't see the cases. He needed a camera.

He didn't have one.

He needed to look at this differently. The Authority's verification step required confirmation of mortality. The standard method was physical inspection. His protocol toolkit listed another method, unused because it required equipment he lacked.

Acoustic verification. Listen for heartbeat. If the cases were alive, they'd have a faint pulse. If they were dead, silence.

He didn't have a stethoscope. He didn't have a hydrophone. But he had a wrench.

He dropped the wrench into the water. It hit the pipe floor with a clink. He waited.

Nothing, and he dropped it again, aiming for where the egg cases should be. The wrench hit something soft.

He pulled it back up.

The handle was coated in the pale wax, but no fluid. No movement.

He dropped it three more times. Each time, the contact felt different.

The first two hits were soft, yielding, and the third was harder.

The fourth was hard and brittle. The cases were breaking apart underwater.

The flooding was working.

He wrote that in his verification report. Acoustic verification method: non-standard. Physical contact confirmation: cases are brittle and fragmenting. Confidence level: high.

The Authority pinged.

VERIFICATION ACCEPTED (PROVISIONAL). Thoroughness rating updated: 6.4 to 7.4.

Provisional entry credit (species #47): +1.8. Effective rating: 9.2.

Cascade Risk flag: RESOLVED.

Treatment Points awarded: 340. Total Treatment Points: 1,847.

He sat on the curb. Looked at the number. 340 points for a job he'd nearly botched. The flooding protocol had been a last resort, improvised, undocumented until the moment he deployed it. The Authority had given him provisional verification, which meant the rating could be revised down if the cases turned out to be alive in twenty-four hours.

He needed to check tomorrow. He'd set a reminder.

He stood. Packed his kit. Sealed the manhole with the bolts. Replaced the drain grate with a new one he pulled from his truck's supply bin, bolted it down with fresh hardware.

He was walking back to his truck when his phone buzzed. Not the Authority notification. A text message. From a number he didn't recognize.

"Kowalski.

Authority dispatch. Your job at Elm and 4th has been flagged for quality review.

A second technician is en route.

Do not leave the site."

He read it twice. He looked at the street.

The sealed cracks.

The bolted grate.

The manhole he'd just closed. Everything was done.

He'd completed the protocol.

He typed back: "Job complete. Verification provisional. I'm leaving the site."

The response came in three seconds. "Negative.

A Level II technician named Voss is arriving in eleven minutes.

He has a competing claim on this job. You are ordered to remain on-site for joint review."

He looked at the text. He looked at the street. He looked at his clipboard, where the job was signed, dated, and closed.

Then he looked at the drain. The one he'd just sealed. The one where the nest had been.

Something was moving under the asphalt. Not the slow pulse of a nest. Something faster. Something with legs.

His phone buzzed again. A new Authority notification.

INFESTATION DETECTED.

Species: UNKNOWN. Entry vector: sub-surface.

Location: 12 feet from current treatment site.

Field Guide: NO MATCH, and he stared at the notification. He'd never gotten a "no match" before.

Every creature he'd encountered had populated his guide.

Even the weird ones.

Even the ones that didn't make sense.

He pulled out his flashlight. Aimed it at the asphalt. The pulse was under the sealed sealant.

Under the crack he'd filled six hours ago. The sealant was bulging.

Cracking.

Something was pushing through from below, and he reached for his sealant can. His wrench. His clipboard.

His phone buzzed a third time. A text from Voss.

"Kowalski.

I'm two minutes out. Don't touch anything.

That's a Class-4 breach and you're not certified for it.

Stand down."

Dale looked at the bulging sealant. At the crack spreading through it. At the thing pushing through from below.

He looked at his Field Guide. No match.

No entry.

No weakness identified.

No targeted bonus available, and he looked at his wrench.

He filled the entry anyway, and the bonus field stayed empty.
