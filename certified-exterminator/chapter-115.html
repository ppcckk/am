Chapter 115: Offline and Overrun

# Chapter 115

The darkness smelled of wet concrete and ancient rot.

Dale landed on the slick bottom of the storm drain with a heavy thud, his boots sliding on the algae-coated surface. The ladder above him was gone. The grate was shut. Silence pressed against his ears, thick and suffocating.

No notifications blinked in his peripheral vision. No blue text floated in the air. The System had been severed.

Dale knelt. He touched the ground.

His fingers brushed against something slimy and fibrous.

He pulled his hand back, shaking it off, and wiped it on his coveralls. The fabric was already stained with muck, but he noted the texture. Fungal hyphae.

Dense.

Active.

He stood up slowly.

His knees popped. The pain in his lower back was a familiar companion, dull and constant, but it was manageable. What wasn't manageable was the absence of the Field Guide.

Without the holographic overlay, he couldn't identify the species. He couldn't see the weakness tags.

He couldn't see the threat level.

He was blind.

But he wasn't untrained.

Dale turned on his headlamp.

The beam cut a sharp cone through the dust. The drain was a circular tunnel, roughly six feet in diameter.

The walls were lined with old brick, cracked and bulging with mold. Water trickled along the channel at the bottom, black and sluggish.

He took a step forward. Then another.

The air grew colder. A low hum vibrated through the soles of his boots.

It wasn't mechanical.

It was biological. A frequency.

Dale stopped. He held his breath.

*Listen.*

That was the first rule of inspection. Before you spray, you listen. You watch. You map the traffic patterns.

The hum grew louder. It came from ahead. And behind.

He was not alone in the pipe.

Dale reached into his belt pouch.

He had no spray cans. No traps.

No bait stations.

He had a heavy-duty flashlight, a multi-tool, and a roll of industrial-grade zip ties.

He unclipped the multi-tool. The pliers snapped open with a metallic click.

It was a pathetic weapon against a Class 3 monster, but it was a tool.

And Dale Kowalski was a technician.

Tools were neutral until applied.

He moved forward, keeping his back to the curved wall. This was standard procedure for tight quarters. It minimized exposure to lateral attacks. It allowed him to pivot.

Twenty feet in, the tunnel split. A T-junction. The left branch sloped upward. The right branch sloped downward, deeper into the earth.

The hum was coming from both.

Dale paused. He scanned the walls. Near the junction, he saw them.

Scorch marks. Not fire. Acid. The brick was pitted and white, where the mortar had been eaten away.

He ran his gloved finger along the edge of the corrosion. It was still warm.

Recent activity.

He crouched. The ground here was different. The concrete was littered with small, chitinous shells.

Not rat bones. Insect carapaces.

But larger.

Much larger.

Dale picked one up. It was a thorax segment, the size of a hockey puck. Translucent amber, hardened to the density of ceramic.

He turned it over. There were puncture marks on the underside. Clean. Precision cuts.

Something with mandibles designed for severance had harvested this shell.

Dale placed the piece back down. He didn't break it. Breaking evidence altered the timeline. He needed to know what had happened here before he stepped into the ambush.

He pulled out his phone. The screen was dead, black glass reflecting his pale face. He tapped it. Nothing.

He sighed. A short, sharp exhale through his nose.

*Note to self: The Authority does not provide emergency comms.*

He shoved the phone back in his pocket.

He needed a new plan. Without the System's passive detection, he had to rely on sensory input.

Smell.

Sound. Vibration.

He closed his eyes.

The hum shifted. Pitch dropped. A grinding sound. Like stones rubbing together.

It came from the left. The upward slope.

Dale opened his eyes. He looked at the right branch.

The downward slope.

It was silent.

Too silent. Dead air.

In pest control, silence is a warning sign. It means the nest is empty, or the nest is waiting.

He chose the left. The noise was a variable he could track. The silence was a trap.

He walked up the incline. The walls narrowed. The ceiling dropped. He had to stoop.

The air grew thick with a scent. Ammonia. And something sweeter.

Rotting fruit? No.

Fermentation.

Dale's stomach tightened. He recognized the smell. It was the scent of a secondary infestation. A brood site.

He reached the end of the branch. It wasn't a dead end. It was a chamber.

He swept his light across the room.

It was a sewer pump station. Old, decommissioned. Rusted pipes hung from the ceiling. A large, cylindrical tank sat in the center, its lid half-open.

And the floor was covered in eggs.

Not rat eggs. Not spider eggs.

They were black, leathery, and pulsing. Each one was the size of a football. Hundreds of them.

Dale froze. His heart hammered against his ribs, a frantic bird trapped in a cage. He forced his breathing to slow.

In through the nose.

Out through the mouth. Four counts.

He scanned the eggs. They were clustered around the base of the tank. A central mass. The source of the heat.

He looked for the mother.

She wasn't there.

The eggs were unattended.

That was wrong.

In every infestation, the female protects the brood. If the female is gone, the brood is vulnerable. Or the female is watching from a distance.

Dale stepped closer. He kept his distance. Five feet. He had enough space to react.

He pointed his light at the nearest egg. The shell rippled. Something moved inside.

He leaned in. Just an inch.

He saw the eyes.

They were compound. Faceted. Black and wet. They looked directly at him.

Dale stepped back.

The hum intensified. The grinding sound came from below the tank.

He looked down.

A gap in the floor. A dark hole leading into the water channel.

Something was climbing out.

It started with legs.

Long, spindly legs made of segmented, black chitin. They clicked against the concrete. Then a torso.

Low to the ground. A wide, armored back.

It was a Beetle. But not a beetle Dale had ever seen.

It was the size of a large dog. Its shell was iridescent, shifting from green to purple as the light hit it. Mandibles snapped open, revealing rows of serrated teeth.

Dale recognized the posture. Defensive. Aggressive.

He didn't have a spray. He didn't have a trap.

He had a zip tie.

And he had knowledge.

Dale looked at the beetle. He looked at the eggs.

The beetle was a guardian. But it was also a resource.

*Cross-contamination penalty,* he thought. *Deploying a protocol designed for one creature type against another inflicts a 40% debuff.*

He didn't have a protocol. He didn't have a license for this species.

But he knew the biology.

These creatures were subterranean. They relied on vibration. They hunted by sensing tremors in the ground.

Dale shifted his weight. He moved his left foot.

The beetle's head snapped toward him. Its antennae twitched.

He moved his right foot.

The beetle turned slightly.

It was tracking him.

Dale stopped. He stayed perfectly still.

He looked at the tank. The lid was half-open. It was rusted. Weak.

He looked at the beetle. It was circling him now, slow and deliberate. It wasn't attacking. It was assessing.

Dale reached for his multi-tool again. He didn't aim it at the beetle. He aimed it at the tank's lid.

He threw it.

The tool spun through the air, hitting the rusted edge of the tank. It bounced off.

The noise echoed. Sharp. Metallic.

The beetle flinched. It turned its head toward the sound.

Dale didn't wait. He kicked the ground. Hard.

He stomped on the concrete. Once. Twice.

The vibration traveled through the floor.

The beetle shrieked. A high-pitched, clicking scream. It didn't charge. It retreated.

It scrambled back toward the hole in the floor.

Dale moved forward. He didn't chase the beetle. He went to the eggs.

He pulled a zip tie from his belt. He looped it around the nearest egg. He pulled it tight.

The egg cracked.

Green fluid oozed out.

The beetle shrieked again. It spun around, mandibles flaring.

Dale pulled another zip tie. He looped it around the next egg.

One by one.

He wasn't killing them. He was containing them.

The beetle lunged.

Dale sidestepped. The massive mandibles snapped at his shoulder. He felt the wind of the blow.

He threw his headlamp at the beetle.

The light smashed against the creature's shell. It didn't hurt it, but it blinded it. For a second, the creature was confused.

Dale grabbed the cracked egg. He pulled the shell apart. The larva inside was soft, white, and writhing.

He squeezed it.

The beetle screamed. It didn't attack him. It attacked the egg.

It rushed toward the broken shell, trying to protect the larva.

Dale kicked the tank. The rusted lid fell off completely. It crashed into the water.

The splash was loud. The water rushed over the edge.

Dale grabbed a handful of eggs.

He didn't break them.

He stuffed them into his jacket pockets. Heavy.

Bulky.

He backed away. Toward the junction.

The beetle was distracted by the broken egg and the falling lid. It clicked its mandibles, confused by the sudden chaos.

Dale reached the T-junction. He looked left. Right.

The left was blocked by the beetle.

The right was dark. Silent.

He went right.

He ran.

He didn't look back. He listened.

The clicking grew softer.

The beetle wasn't following. It was guarding the broken egg.

Dale reached the main channel. The water here was deeper. Knee-high.

He waded through. The cold water numbed his legs.

He found the ladder he had climbed down. It was still there. But the door was locked.

He tried the handle. Rusted shut.

He looked around. The exit was gone.

He was trapped.

But he had eggs.

He looked at the eggs in his pockets. He had stolen them.

The beetle was guarding the nest. It would come looking for the missing brood.

Dale pulled out a zip tie. He used it to secure a loose pipe bracket on the wall. He tested it. Solid.

He wasn't trying to escape.

He was setting a trap.

He waited.

Five minutes passed.

Then, a vibration in the water.

The beetle emerged from the side tunnel. It was agitated. Its antennae were twitching wildly.

It saw Dale.

It charged.

Dale didn't move. He stood his ground. He held the zip tie in his hand like a weapon.

The beetle leaped.

Dale ducked.

The creature flew over him. Its legs scraped the ceiling, spraying dust and debris.

It landed behind him.

Dale turned. He swung the zip tie. He wrapped it around the beetle's back leg. He pulled.

The zip tie snapped.

The beetle shrieked. It turned, mandibles open.

Dale stepped into the water. He grabbed a large rock from the bottom. He threw it at the beetle's eye.

It hit. The creature recoiled.

Dale grabbed the bracket. He pulled. The metal groaned.

He pulled harder. His muscles burned. His back screamed in protest.

The bracket tore free from the wall.

Dale swung it.

He hit the beetle's thorax. The impact was dull and heavy. The shell cracked.

Green fluid sprayed out.

The beetle went limp.

It didn't die. But it was stunned.

Dale didn't hesitate. He grabbed the beetle's leg. He dragged it into the water. He pulled.

He dragged the massive creature toward the open grate at the end of the tunnel.

It was heavy. The water resisted.

He dragged. And dragged.

His lungs burned. His arms felt like lead.

He reached the grate.

He pulled the beetle's front half through.

He pulled the back half through.

The beetle was outside. In the street. In the light.

Dale climbed out.

He stood up. He was soaked. Shivering.

The street was empty. The storm drain grate was closed behind him.

He looked around.

No people. No cars.

Just the rain.

He looked at his hands. They were covered in green slime.

He looked at his jacket. The eggs were there.

He had escaped.

But he had left the beetle outside.

The beetle was waking up.

A notification appeared. Not in his vision. On his phone.

The screen flickered to life.

*Message Received.*

*From: Central Server.*

*Subject: Unauthorized Extraction.*

*Body: You have removed biological material from a restricted zone. This constitutes theft of Authority assets.*

*Penalty: Certification Revocation Pending.*

*Action Required: Return the specimens to the designated disposal site within 15 minutes or face permanent license suspension.*

Dale stared at the phone.

The battery died.

The screen went black.

He looked at the beetle. It was struggling to stand.

He looked at the eggs.

He looked at the empty street.

A car turned the corner. Headlights swept across the wet pavement.

Dale ran.
