Chapter 322: License to Violate

# Chapter 322

The engine coughed. Once. Twice. Then it caught, a low, grinding roar that vibrated through the steering wheel and into Dale's palms.

Rain hammered the windshield, turning the streetlights into smeared yellow bruises. The wipers beat a frantic rhythm against the glass, slapping away sheets of oily water that smelled of copper and old rot.

Dale wiped the condensation from the side mirror with a sleeve.

His hand was shaking. Not from fear. From adrenaline.

From the cold, sharp clarity of a man who realized he had broken the first rule of his life: *Document everything before you touch anything.*

He had torn the permit. He had ignored the suspension notice. He had driven into a Class IX infestation zone with a half-empty tank and a corrupted Field Guide.

The Authority's notification blazed in his peripheral vision, a persistent red blink that refused to be dismissed.

**VIOLATION CODE: 404-UNAUTHORIZED DEPLOYMENT.** **PENALTY: IMMEDIATE CANCELLATION OF CERTIFICATION.** **STATUS: PENDING APPEAL (72 HOURS REMAINING).**

Dale ignored it. He kept his eyes on the road.

The GPS on his dashboard was dead, the screen flickering with static snow.

He navigated by memory and by the sound of the pipes. The city's infrastructure was groaning. A high-pitched shriek cut through the rain, coming from three blocks east.

A main break. Or something worse.

The truck hydroplaned as it rounded the corner onto Elm Street.

Dale corrected the wheel, his knuckles white, and he scanned the road. No rats.

No beetles. The streets were empty, swept clean by the panic that had emptied the neighborhoods hours ago. But the air was thick.

Humid. It clung to the inside of the cab like a wet coat.

The water supply. The Mimic.

It wasn't just in the pipes. It was in the pressure.

Dale slowed the truck to a crawl, and he rolled down the window. The smell hit him instantly. Not sewage.

Not mud.

It was sweet. Cloying.

Like overripe fruit left in a hot car.

*Ammonia?* No. *Ethyl acetate.*

He pulled his notebook from the passenger seat. The pages were blank, the ink of his previous notes dissolved into gray swirls. He flipped to a fresh page.

The paper was dry. Good.

He uncapped his pen.

If he couldn't see the monster, he could see the symptoms.

The streetlights flickered. Not electrically. They dimmed, then brightened, pulsing in a slow, rhythmic pattern. A heartbeat.

Dale stepped out of the truck.

The rain soaked his uniform instantly, but he didn't care. He walked to the nearest storm drain.

The grate was gone. Not pried off. Dissolved.

The metal bars were thin, rusted slats that crumbled under his boot.

Black water bubbled up from the hole. It wasn't black. It was dark violet, almost purple, shifting with a bioluminescent sheen.

Dale knelt, and he didn't touch it. He held his flashlight over the surface. The light didn't reflect.

It was absorbed.

He pulled a sampling tube from his belt. He dipped it in.

The liquid resisted.

It clung to the glass like syrup. He capped the tube and held it up to the light. The liquid inside churned, forming tiny, perfect spheres that popped and reformed.

**IDENTIFICATION ATTEMPT: UNKNOWN.** **ERROR: FIELD GUIDE CORRUPTION.** **SUGGESTION: OBSERVE PHYSICAL PROPERTIES.**

Dale stared at the tube. The spheres were moving.

Not randomly, and they were arranging themselves.

Into a grid. Into a map.

He looked up. The map in the tube matched the layout of the street. The houses.

The curb. The truck.

It was learning.

A scream tore through the air. Not from a person. From the ground.

The asphalt in front of Dale cracked. A section of pavement lifted, three feet high, held up by a mass of violet sludge that had hardened into a crude, fleshy tentacle. The tentacle waved, blind and groping, before slapping down onto the truck's hood.

Dale dove backward, rolling onto the wet grass. The truck groaned. The metal of the hood buckled inward, caving in like a crushed soda can.

The tentacle retracted. The sludge poured back into the cracks, flowing with impossible speed, defying gravity. It moved uphill. It moved toward the nearest house.

Dale stood up, and he checked his spray tank. Half-full. Standard mixture.

Insecticide, fungicide, and a binding agent for dimensional residue.

Useless against a Mimic. The Authority's manual stated that Class IX entities required *containment*, not elimination.

You didn't kill the water.

You sealed the pipe.

But the pipes were underground. And the Mimic was above ground.

He ran to the truck. The hood was ruined, but the engine was intact.

He jumped in.

The ignition sputtered, and he turned the key again. The engine roared.

He slammed the gearshift into drive. He reversed, tires spinning on the wet asphalt, then swung the truck around. He aimed for the house with the tentacle.

No. Not the house. The meter box.

Every residential zone had a water meter. Every meter had a shut-off valve. It was the first thing Dale learned in his first week of training. *Know your shutoff.*

He drove straight at the front lawn of the house. He didn't stop. He drove right over the grass, through the flowerbed, and parked the truck nose-to-nose with the meter box embedded in the sidewalk.

The violet sludge was pooling at the base of the meter. It was seeping into the brass valve.

Dale grabbed his wrench. A heavy, steel tool, standard issue for his Level II license. He jumped out.

The air was screaming now. A low, resonant hum that vibrated in his teeth. The house's windows were dark, but the curtains were moving. Blowing in a wind that didn't exist.

Dale approached the meter box. The sludge recoiled from his shadow. It sensed the tool. It sensed the intent.

He didn't hesitate. He jammed the wrench into the gap of the valve handle. It was stuck.

Corroded. Or fused.

He kicked the wrench. The valve didn't budge.

He tried to turn it.

His arms strained. The muscles in his shoulders burned. The sludge surged, trying to engulf his boots.

He stepped back, wiping his boot on the grass, and he didn't look at it. He looked at the valve.

"Come on," he muttered. "Open. You stupid piece of junk."

He pulled his flashlight from his belt and wedged it into the valve's mechanism, using it as a lever. He put his entire body weight onto the wrench.

The valve squealed. A high, metallic shriek.

Then it gave.

The handle turned. A quarter turn. Half. Three-quarters.

The flow of sludge stopped. The pool at the base of the meter froze. The violet light dimmed.

The hum in the air cut out.

Silence.

Dale stayed in position, holding the wrench, breathing hard.

His chest heaved. He checked his hands. No cuts.

No contamination, and he turned to look at the house.

The curtains stopped moving. The windows were still dark.

He walked to the porch. He didn't knock. He listened.

Nothing, and he tried the door.

Locked.

He looked for a key. No key.

He looked for a window. The back door was broken. Glass scattered on the patio.

He entered.

The house smelled of the sweet fruit scent. The living room was empty.

Furniture pushed to the walls.

Not by wind. By force.

On the coffee table sat a single glass of water. It was full. Unspilled.

Dale picked it up. He examined the water. Clear. Normal.

He set it down, and he walked to the kitchen. The sink was running.

The faucet was on, water flowing into the drain.

But the drain wasn't taking the water. The sink was overflowing, but the water wasn't spilling onto the floor. It was rising.

Rising in the sink, defying the overflow hole, climbing higher and higher, staying in a perfect column of liquid that defied gravity.

It reached the counter. It reached Dale's chest.

He stepped back.

The column of water stopped. It held its shape. A perfect cylinder. Three feet tall.

It had no eyes. No face. But it had a presence.

Dale pulled out his Field Guide. The pages were still blank. But as he looked at the water, a single line of text appeared, written in a shaky, handwriting font that wasn't his.

*Subject: Mimic, Liquid State.* *Weakness: Surface Tension.* *Protocol: Break the Cohesion.*

Dale stared at the text. He hadn't written it. The Authority hadn't awarded it. It had appeared.

A glitch? A reward for the shutoff?

Or a warning?

The water column twitched. It leaned toward him.

Dale backed into the hallway, and he pulled his spray tank from his shoulder.

He had one nozzle left. A wide-angle dispersal tip.

Designed for roach nests.

He aimed at the water column, and he squeezed the trigger.

The spray hit the column. The chemicals hit the surface.

The water didn't splash. It absorbed the spray. The column grew thicker. Darker.

**CROSS-CONTAMINATION PENALTY ACTIVE.** **DEBUFF: 40%.** **FIELD GUIDE ENTRY CORRUPTED.**

Dale lowered the gun. He had made the same mistake he always made.

He treated it like a pest, and he tried to kill it. But it wasn't a pest. It was a breach.

He needed to seal it. Not spray it.

He looked around the kitchen. A roll of plastic sheeting. A bag of sand from the pantry? No, sand was for drainage.

He needed something that would absorb the water. Something that would expand.

Cat litter, and he saw it in the corner, next to the trash can. A large bag, unused. Clumping clay litter.

He grabbed the bag. It was heavy. Fifty pounds.

He ripped the bag open with his teeth. He scooped up handfuls of the gray pellets and threw them at the column.

The pellets hit the water. They sank. They didn't clump. They dissolved.

The column pulsed. It grew taller.

Dale threw another handful. Nothing.

He threw the entire bag. A mound of litter hit the water.

The water absorbed it. The column expanded, widening its base.

It was feeding on the material.

Dale backed out of the kitchen. The hallway was dark. The floorboards creaked.

He ran to the front door, and he needed to get out. He needed to call for backup.

He needed Vane.

He needed a Class IX team.

But Vane was outside. Vane was suspended.

Dale burst through the front door. The rain had stopped. The air was still.

The truck was gone, and he turned in a circle. The street was empty. His truck was nowhere to be seen.

He looked down. The meter box. The valve was open. He had closed it.

No. He had turned it *open*. He had been holding the wrench, but in his confusion, he had twisted it the wrong way. He had reconnected the line.

The violet sludge was back. It was everywhere. It was seeping from the curb, from the gutters, from the air vents of the nearby houses.

It was forming shapes. Human shapes.

Dale backed up against his house. He was trapped.

He pulled out his pen. He opened his notebook. The pages were blank again. The glitch was gone.

He wrote: *Mimic feeds on containment materials. Do not use absorbents.*

He looked at the sludge. It was gathering. Forming a wall between him and the street.

He had no spray. No truck. No license.

He had a pen. And a notebook.

And a thought.

The Authority rewarded documentation.

The Authority rewarded *identification*, and he hadn't identified the Mimic's core. He had only identified its state. Liquid.

But Mimics weren't liquid. Mimics were *mimics*. They copied.

It was copying the house. It was copying the plumbing. It was copying the water.

What was it copying *now*?

It was copying *Dale*.

The sludge shifted. It formed a figure.

Tall.

Thin.

Wearing a uniform. Holding a pen.

It had no face. But it had a clipboard.

Dale looked at his own clipboard. He looked at the figure.

The figure raised its hand. It pointed at Dale.

**ALERT: MIMICRY EVENT DETECTED.** **SUBJECT: DALE KOWALSKI.** **THREAT LEVEL: CLASS IX.**

Dale's blood ran cold.

The Authority wasn't just tracking the infestation. It was tracking *him*. The Mimic had copied him.

And because it had copied him, it had access to his license. To his protocols.

To his Field Guide, which was using his own data against him.

The figure stepped forward. The ground shook.

Dale looked at his notebook. He looked at the figure.

He couldn't fight it. He couldn't spray it.

But he could out-document it, and he opened a new page. He wrote. Not a report. A *complaint*.

He wrote: *Violation: Unauthorized Impersonation of Licensed Technician.*

He wrote: *Violation: Failure to Maintain Structural Integrity.*

He wrote: *Violation: Cross-Dimensional Identity Theft.*

He wrote as fast as he could. His hand cramped. His pen scratched against the paper.

The figure paused. It tilted its head.

The Authority notification flashed.

**DOCUMENTATION SUBMITTED.** **PROCESSING...**

The figure shuddered. Its form flickered. It looked at its own clipboard. It looked at Dale's.

It hesitated.

The Authority didn't care about monsters. It cared about paperwork. And the Mimic hadn't filed the correct forms for impersonation.

The sludge unravelled. Not destroyed. *Denied*. Rejected by the System's bureaucratic logic.

Dale kept writing. He wrote every violation he could think of. Code K-7.

Code Z-9.

Code L-1.

The figure dissolved into mist. The mist rose into the air, scattering into rain.

Dale stood there, alone, in the empty street, and he looked at his notebook. The pages were full.

He looked at his license. It was still flashing red.

But then, a new notification appeared. Green this time.

**APPEAL ACCEPTED.** **THOROUGHNESS RATING: 10.0.** **CERTIFICATION: CLASS IX (MASTER) GRANTED.** **NOTE: THIS LEVEL REQUIRES IMMEDIATE REPORTING TO THE CENTRAL AUTHORITY.** **LOCATION: THE CORE.**

The ground beneath him rumbled again. But this time, it wasn't an infestation.

It was an invitation.

Dale looked at the sky. The Rift was widening. And from it, something large, something with too many eyes, was looking down.

It held a clipboard.

Dale gripped his pen. He had a new problem. And he had a new license.

He opened the truck's emergency kit. Inside was a flare gun. And a map to the Core.

He loaded the flare, and he fired it into the sky.

The red light bloomed, cutting through the dark.

From the Rift, the thing with the clipboard looked down. It smiled.

And it wrote.
