Chapter 1: K-7 Infestation Report

# Chapter 1

Dale Kowalski pressed the ballpoint pen harder against the clipboard and wrote K-7 in block capitals, then underlined it twice. The kitchen smelled like a dead mouse that had been dead long enough to become a smell you stopped noticing, which meant the mouse was in the wall. He could feel it behind the drywall panel where the plumbing met the foundation, a faint sweetness that no amount of Febreze could mask.

The tenant, a woman named Patricia who had been crying in the living room for twenty minutes, had stopped crying because something else had started screaming.

Dale looked up from the clipboard. The floor beneath the kitchen sink had split along the grout line, a clean fracture that ran from the baseboard to the center of the linoleum.

Something was pushing up from below.

The tile buckled with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and then a rat the size of a border collie pulled itself through the gap with a wet tearing noise. Its fur was matted and black, slicked with something that wasn't water. It had too many teeth visible when it opened its mouth, and the teeth were wrong, angled inward like fish hooks.

Patricia screamed. The rat grabbed her ankle with both front paws and dragged her backward across the floor, her fingernails leaving white streaks on the tile. She hit the wall and the rat kept going, pulling her into the gap in the floor like a dog pulling a rope toy from under a couch.

Dale finished writing the violation code, and he added the date. He signed his initials. He tore the carbon copy from the triplicate form and placed it on the kitchen counter, right next to the fruit bowl, where Patricia would find it when she came back. If she came back.

Then he pulled the flashlight from his tool belt and clicked it on.

The gap in the floor was about eight inches wide.

The rat had squeezed through it, which meant it was compressible, which meant the bones were more flexible than a normal rat's. Dale noted this.

He noted the size, the fur texture, the teeth, and he noted that the gap had not existed during his initial walk-through twenty minutes ago. The foundation had not been compromised when he started this inspection.

He crouched beside the gap and shone the flashlight down. The crawlspace below was dark and smelled like ammonia and something else, something organic and sharp, like the smell of a drain that had been left uncapped too long. He could hear Patricia's voice, muffled, getting farther away, and the rat was dragging her toward the back of the crawlspace.

Dale reached for the spray can on his belt. He grabbed it because it was there, because his hand knew where it was without his brain deciding to reach.

He unclipped the nozzle and held it at his side, useless.

The can contained a pyrethroid blend rated for German cockroaches and house spiders.

It would not kill a rat the size of a dog. He knew this.

He held it anyway, and he went down through the gap.

The crawlspace was eighteen inches high. He had to pull his knees to his chest and drag himself forward on his elbows, the clipboard wedged under his left arm.

The flashlight beam bounced and jittered, catching the underside of floor joists, the vapor barrier, the occasional tangle of old wiring.

The ammonia smell was stronger here. The floor above him creaked as Patricia's weight shifted, and he heard a sound that was not a scream. It was a wet sound, like someone pulling their hand out of mud.

He moved forward for six feet. Then the crawlspace opened up.

The broodmother was in the corner where the foundation met the exterior wall, which was a rat, but it was not a rat. It was the size of a large dog and it was lying on its side, and its abdomen was distended, and from the abdomen, thin translucent tubes extended into the wall cavity.

Each tube ended in a knot of tissue that pulsed faintly.

The tubes were anchored to the drywall, the insulation, the vapor barrier. The creature had threaded itself into the structure of the house like a parasite threading into a host.

Dale's brain did what it always did when he encountered a new species in the field. It started cataloguing.

Rodent morphology, but exaggerated.

Reproductive biology suggesting rapid gestation, and the tubes were not parasitic in the traditional sense. They were structural.

The creature was using the house's infrastructure as a support system for its brood.

He identified it before he knew he was identifying it. He said the words out loud without meaning to.

"Dimensional vermin. Rattus profundus. Broodmother. Estimating twenty to thirty juveniles in the wall cavity based on tube count."

The flashlight flickered. Not the flashlight.

The air.

Something in the air flickered, like heat shimmer, and then text appeared in his peripheral vision. Not in the air. It hung in his vision like a floater, but this one had edges and serifs.

UNLICENSED INFESTATION DETECTED. TREATMENT PROTOCOLS UNAVAILABLE. CERTIFICATION LEVEL: UNREGISTERED.

Dale blinked, and he looked directly at the text. It did not resolve into anything physical.

It stayed in his peripheral vision, a translucent overlay that followed his gaze without ever being centered.

He read it three times. He considered whether he had been exposed to a solvent during the inspection that could cause visual disturbance. He considered whether Patricia's kid had installed a prank app on his phone and was projecting through his Bluetooth earpiece.

He considered whether the ammonia concentration in this crawlspace was high enough to cause hallucination.

He found the explanations unsatisfactory, yet irrelevant; the broodmother lived, juveniles infested the wall, and Patricia was likely dead.

He put the spray can back on his belt, and he pulled the trash bag from his bag, the heavy-duty contractor grade, thirty-gallon, rated for eighty pounds.

He pulled the duct tape from his tool belt, the silver kind, industrial grade. He had used this combination before, on a raccoon that had gotten into a residential HVAC return.

The raccoon had been larger than this creature. The raccoon had been more dangerous. This creature was not moving.

Its abdomen was still distended, still pulsing, still threaded into the wall.

Dale evaluated the situation as he would any nest site, concluding the broodmother was not the issue. The juveniles in the wall were the problem. The broodmother was the source.

If he killed the broodmother without addressing the juveniles, the juveniles would mature in six to eight weeks and the infestation would return.

If he sealed the wall without killing the broodmother, the broodmother would produce more juveniles and the infestation would return. The protocol was clear.

Identify the source.

Isolate the source. Eliminate the source. Verify that no viable reproductive capacity remains.

Document everything, and he could not eliminate the source with a spray can. He could not eliminate the source with his hands. But he could isolate it.

He wrapped the trash bag around the broodmother's abdomen, the part where the tubes connected, and taped it.

He taped it tight, wrapping the duct tape in overlapping strips, sealing the bag against the creature's body.

The tubes were still attached to the wall, and he could not cut them without risking the juveniles dispersing into the structure.

So he sealed the connection point; the bag created a barrier between the creature's reproductive output and the wall cavity. The juveniles already in the wall would mature, but no new juveniles would enter the structure.

It was not a kill. It was a containment. It was not complete. It was enough to stop the active infestation from spreading while he figured out how to actually kill the thing.

The broodmother moved. It raised its head and stared with oversized, highly reflective eyes, and the glare resembled a cat’s, but the reflection was incorrect, sharp and intentional. It opened its mouth and the wrong teeth were visible again, and it made a sound that was not a squeak. It was a low vibration that Dale felt in his sternum.

He did not run. He wrote on his clipboard, and wrote the species identification, the estimated population, the containment method, the time, the location. He wrote it in the same handwriting he used for code violations and inspection reports, the same cramped block letters, the same abbreviations.

The text in his peripheral vision changed.

IDENTIFICATION CONFIRMED. SPECIES: Dimensional Vermin (Rattus profundus).

CONTAINMENT PROTOCOL: Improvised (non-standard).

TREATMENT POINTS AWARDED: 12. CERTIFICATION LEVEL: I (PROVISIONAL). FIELD GUIDE: 1 entry populated.

Dale stared at the text.

The text stared at nothing. He wrote down "12 points" on his clipboard because he did not know what else to do with the information.

He did not know what Treatment Points were, and he did not know what a Certification Level meant. He did not know who or what had awarded him these things. He knew that he had contained a broodmother in a trash bag and that the containment was imperfect and that the juveniles were still in the wall.

He finished the report. He crawled back through the gap.

The kitchen was quiet. Patricia was not in the kitchen.

Her shoes were by the door, which meant she had been wearing them when she was dragged, which meant she had not been able to kick free.

Dale looked at the gap in the floor, and the rat that had dragged her was not there. The gap was still there, the clean fracture in the tile, the dark space below.

He went outside.

The duplex was in a cul-de-sac in Westerville, Ohio. There were six houses on the street. Four of them had people standing on their lawns.

The people were looking at the ground. The ground was cracking.

Not just at the duplex.

At the house across the street, a seam had opened in the driveway. At the house to the left, the lawn had buckled and split, and from the split, something dark was emerging. Not a rat.

Something with too many legs.

A man in a bathrobe was standing on the sidewalk holding a broom; he was shouting at the thing in the lawn. The thing was the size of a large cat and it had legs like a spider but a body like a beetle and it was clicking its mandibles and the man was hitting it with the broom and the broom was not working.

Dale walked past the man. He walked to the edge of the property and looked down the street.

The cracks were spreading.

They were following the foundation lines of the houses, following the plumbing runs, following the places where the ground was weakest. The cracks were not random. They were structural.

They were following the same logic Dale used when he mapped an infestation. They were following the path of least resistance through the built environment.

The man with the broom turned and saw him. "You see that? You see that thing?"

Dale looked at the thing. He examined its leg structure, body segmentation, and orientation toward the cracked foundation. He identified it.

"Acid beetle. Class-1 dimensional vermin. Probably three inches of carapace, so your broom won't penetrate. You need to get the carapace wet with something caustic and then hit it at the joint."

The man stared at him.

Dale looked back at the street, and the cracks were widening.

The things were coming through faster now. He could count them.

Seven. Nine. Twelve.

He tallied them as he did cockroach sightings or foundation entry points. He counted everything, since counting was the initial step, and people always skipped the initial step.

The text appeared again.

CERTIFICATION LEVEL I: PROVISIONAL.

NEXT EXAMINATION: WRITTEN (48 HOURS). PRACTICAL (14 DAYS). CURRENT BACKLOG: 1,247 UNTREATED INFESTATIONS IN YOUR SERVICE AREA.

THOROUGHNESS RATING: PENDING.

Dale read it. He looked at the street. He looked at the man with the broom, who was now running. He looked at the cracks, which were still spreading, which would spread until every foundation in the county was compromised, until every crawlspace and basement and wall cavity was threaded with tubes and brood and wrong teeth.

He went back inside the duplex. He went to the kitchen. He picked up his clipboard. He opened to a fresh page.

He wrote the header for a new inspection report.

The text in his vision changed one more time.

ADDITIONAL NOTIFICATION: THE AUTHORITY NOTES YOUR THOROUGHNESS INSTINCT. A PROVISIONAL LICENSE HAS BEEN ISSUED.

YOU ARE NOW AUTHORIZED TO TREAT CLASS-1 INFESTATIONS WITHOUT SUPERVISION.

YOU ARE NOT AUTHORIZED TO TREAT CLASS-2 OR ABOVE. WARNING: THE SOURCE OF THIS INFESTATION IS NOT THE BROODMOTHER. THE BROODMOTHER IS A SYMPTOM.

THE CAUSE IS STILL ACTIVE AND IS CLASSIFIED AS CLASS-4. YOU WILL ENCOUNTER IT WITHIN 72 HOURS.

SURVIVAL PROBABILITY AT CURRENT CERTIFICATION LEVEL: 3%.

Dale set down his pen. He looked at the crawlspace gap in the kitchen floor. He looked at the trash bag, still sealed, still containing the broodmother, which was no longer moving. He looked at the clipboard, at the fresh page, at the header he had written.

He picked up the pen again. He began writing the report.

The Authority would grade it. The Authority would rate his thoroughness. The Authority had given him a number, 3%, and he did not know what to do with that number except the one thing he knew how to do with every number.

He wrote it down.
