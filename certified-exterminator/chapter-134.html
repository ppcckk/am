Chapter 134: Threat Not Report

# Chapter 134

The pen moved across the parchment.

It didn't stop.

The ink was black, wet, and smelled faintly of ozone and old copper. Dale watched the nib dig into the fiber, tearing a tiny hole in the white surface of the Authority's record book.

The woman at the desk, Auditor Vane, hadn't blinked since she'd spoken.

Her suit was too crisp, the creases in her trousers sharp enough to cut glass. Behind her, the shadowy figure held the pen steady, its wrist locked at a ninety-degree angle.

"Form 14-B, Section 4: Unauthorized Possession of Class-IX Artifacts," Vane read, her voice flat and bored. "Penalty: Immediate suspension of Certification Level. Revocation of Field Guide entries. Mandatory memory scrubbing of non-standard combat techniques."

Dale's hands were shaking. He placed them flat on the counter, fingers spread wide, palms down. A grounding technique. Standard protocol for high-stress inspections.

"I don't have a sword," Dale said.

The lie tasted like ash in his mouth.

Vane tapped the photo. The image of Dale from ten years ago shimmered under the sterile lights. The sword in the picture glowed with a sickly, violet hue. It looked less like steel and more like solidified regret.

"Your biometric signature matches this image," Vane said.

"Your Field Guide lists 'Swordsmanship' as a dormant skill. Dormant, Mr.

Kowalski, implies it exists.

It implies you chose not to use it.

"

"It was a costume," Dale said. The words came out faster than he wanted.

"Halloween.

Ten years ago.

I wore it to a party. I lost it in the laundry.

"

"A Class-IX Artifact does not go missing in the laundry." Vane closed the folder. The sound was like a coffin lid shutting. "We have detected residual mana traces on your clipboard. Specifically, on the third page of your last inspection report for the Henderson residence."

Dale looked down at his clipboard. The third page. The entry for the *Giant Centipede* infestation in the Hendersons' basement.

He had written the treatment log in pencil. Just pencil.

"Paper doesn't hold mana," Dale said.

"Standard paper, no," Vane said. "But you used the Authority-issued carbon copy. And you annotated the weakness of the centipede using a term that is not in the licensed lexicon."

Dale frowned, and he remembered that annotation. He had written *'Chitinous plating vulnerable to harmonic vibration'* in the margin. It was a professional observation.

He had noticed the centipede shivering when he hit the floor with his boot.

"That's a note," Dale said. "It's documentation. That's what I do. I document."

"It is an invocation," Vane said. "By identifying the harmonic vulnerability, you inadvertently channeled a fragment of the banned resonance protocol.

You didn't just kill the centipede, Mr.

Kowalski. You disintegrated its nervous system with a frequency that no licensed Pest Control Technician is allowed to know.

"

The system notification pinged in Dale's peripheral vision. It was red. A color he had never seen in the interface before.

**WARNING: CONTAMINATION DETECTED.** **Field Guide Entry: Dimensional Centipede (Class II) is now flagged as COMPROMISED.** **Cross-Contamination Penalty: Active. 40% debuff to all kinetic attacks for 24 hours.** **Note: Auditor intervention imminent.**

Dale felt a sudden weight in his legs. His knees buckled, just slightly.

The debuff.

It felt like his muscles had turned to wet sand, and he leaned against the counter to keep from falling.

"You're framing me," Dale said. His voice dropped an octave. The dryness was gone, replaced by a low, dangerous rumble.

"We are auditing you," Vane corrected. "The distinction is legally significant. Framing implies malice. Auditing implies procedure."

The shadowy figure behind Vane stepped forward. It was not human. The hand holding the pen had too many joints.

The skin was grey, papery, like dried parchment. It dropped the pen on the desk.

The sound was heavy, like a stone hitting tile.

"Sign here," the shadow said. The voice didn't come from a throat. It came from the air itself, vibrating in Dale's molars.

Dale stared at the signature line. It was labeled *Consent to Memory Erasure*.

If he signed, he would forget how to identify a *Rattus profundus* nest. He would forget the smell of acidic beetle frass. He would forget that thoroughness was the only thing that kept the monsters from eating his neighbors.

He would be a blank slate.

A generic citizen.

If he didn't sign, they would revoke his license. Without a license, he couldn't legally interact with the infestations. He would be arrested. Or worse, he would be reclassified as a 'Non-Licensed Infestation Vector.'

He looked at Vane. She was watching his eyes, not his hands. She was waiting for a tell.

Dale picked up the pen. The shadow's fingers twitched.

"I need a moment," Dale said.

"You have thirty seconds," Vane said.

Dale turned the pen over in his hand, and he didn't look at the signature line. He looked at the ink well. It was filled with a viscous, black fluid.

It bubbled slightly, though the room was cold.

"Section 7, Paragraph 3 of the Licensing Code," Dale said, speaking quickly. "It states that if an auditor identifies a potential violation based on circumstantial evidence—such as anomalous annotations—they must first offer the technician a chance to demonstrate the 'Legitimate Professional Context' of the action."

Vane's eyebrow twitched. A microscopic movement.

"There is no such paragraph."

"There is," Dale said.

He pointed to the folder.

"Page 12. Subsection C.

'Contextual Clarification Protocol.' It applies to Class I and II violations. A Class-IX artifact is Class IX. But my violation is the annotation.

The annotation is a Class II documentation error. You are trying to use a Class IX penalty to cover up a Class II procedural mistake.

"

Silence stretched across the white hall.

The shadowy figure tilted its head. The joints cracked.

Vane opened the folder again, and she flipped pages with a speed that blurred her fingers. She stopped at page 12. She read it.

Her lips pressed into a thin line.

"The clause requires a live demonstration," Vane said. "You must prove, in real-time, that your annotation was a standard, licensed observation and not the result of an unlicensed skill."

"Fine," Dale said. "I can do that."

"You have no tools," Vane said. "And your Field Guide is compromised. You cannot access the *Centipede* entry to verify the plating structure."

"I don't need the guide," Dale said. "I need a sample."

He pointed to the floor, and the white hall was sterile, but the edges were fading. The transition zone between the Authority's dimension and the physical world was thin here.

Through the translucent floor, Dale could see the Hendersons' basement.

The *Giant Centipede* was dead, its carapace cracked open by Dale's earlier strike. A pool of yellowish hemolymph was spreading across the concrete.

"Bring the remains," Dale said.

"That is not permitted," Vane said. "Interdimensional retrieval requires a Class IV Permit."

"Grant it," Dale said. "Or I file a counter-complaint for procedural violation.

I have the receipt for the carbon copy.

I have the timestamp.

I know how the Authority's bureaucracy works, Vane. If you miss a box on Form 14-B, you have to re-file the entire audit.

It takes six weeks. Do you want to spend six weeks in this chair?

"

Vane stared at him. Her eyes were cold, hard stones. She hated that he knew the code better than she did. She hated that he treated her like a code violation.

The shadowy figure moved. It placed a small, glass vial on the desk. Inside was a shard of the centipede's leg. It was jagged, black, and dripping.

"Analyze it," Vane said. "Prove your annotation was professional."

Dale picked up the vial. He didn't look at the shard. He looked at the pen.

He uncapped the pen. The ink smelled of ozone.

"If I demonstrate the harmonic vibration," Dale said, "I prove I know it. If I don't, I prove I don't."

"If you fail, you are erased," Vane said.

"If I succeed, you drop the Class-IX charge," Dale said.

Vane nodded. "Proceed."

Dale held the vial in his left hand. In his right, he held the pen.

He didn't tap the vial, and he didn't hum. He didn't use a magical frequency.

Instead, Dale did something mundane.

He looked at the shard of chitin.

He noted the grain. He noted the stress fractures where the blade had hit.

He noted the moisture content, and he noted the angle of the break.

Then, he clicked the pen.

The sound was sharp. Click.

He clicked it again. Click. Click. Click.

A rhythmic, mechanical sound.

Nothing happened. The shard didn't vibrate. It didn't shatter.

Vane smiled. A cruel, thin smile.

"You failed, Mr.

Kowalski.

You attempted to use a sonic weapon. It did not work.

Your Field Guide confirms you lack the skill. Prepare for erasure.

"

"I wasn't attacking it," Dale said.

He held the pen up to the light. The ink was still flowing from the nib, dripping onto his thumb. Black and cold.

"I was checking the viscosity," Dale said.

He dropped the pen. It hit the floor with a clatter. Then, he picked up the shard of chitin and held it over the pool of hemolymph visible through the floor.

He tilted the shard.

A single drop of the Authority's black ink fell from the pen's nib onto the chitin.

The shard hissed.

It didn't vibrate. It *dissolved*. The black ink ate through the chitin, breaking down the molecular bonds, turning the solid shell into a slurry of grey mud.

Dale looked up at Vane.

"Chitinous plating," Dale said. "Vulnerable to high-pH corrosive agents.

That's what I wrote in the margin.

I didn't write 'harmonic vibration.' I wrote 'harmonic vibration' because I made a typo. I meant 'high-pH.' I have always struggled with the letter 'H' and the number '4' on this keyboard.

"

He pointed to the keyboard on the desk. The '4' key was worn smooth.

"My annotation was a clerical error," Dale said. "Not a skill. Not a violation. A typo."

Vane's face went pale. The shadowy figure froze, its many-jointed hand hovering over the desk.

"That..." Vane started, then stopped. She looked at the dissolved shard. She looked at the code. She looked at the definition of 'Clerical Error.'

It was a loophole. A tiny, bureaucratic crack in the armor of the Authority.

If the annotation was a mistake, there was no unauthorized skill.

There was no Class-IX link. The sword in the photo was irrelevant.

It was just a photo.

A costume. A lie.

But the memory remained.

Vane closed the folder. Her hands were trembling. Just a little.

"The audit is... inconclusive," Vane said. Her voice was strained. "Pending further review."

"Review takes six weeks," Dale said. He picked up his clipboard.

He brushed the black ink off his thumb.

"I have a job to do.

The Hendersons' basement still needs a final check. The broodmother isn't dead.

I heard it."

Vane didn't answer. She was already shuffling papers, her mind racing through the new paperwork she had to generate.

Dale turned to leave. The white hall faded, the edges dissolving into grey mist. He stepped backward, his boots finding traction on the solid ground of the real world.

He was back in the Hendersons' basement. The air was damp and smelled of rot.

The *Giant Centipede* was dead.

But the walls were shaking.

Not from vibration. From scratching.

Hundreds of tiny legs, tapping against the drywall.

Dale checked his Field Guide. The *Centipede* entry was greyed out. COMPROMISED.

He couldn't use his standard sprays. The cross-contamination penalty was active. His hands shook, not from fear, but from the debuff. His muscles felt heavy, useless.

He had one tool left.

His clipboard, and he flipped to a blank page. He pulled a ballpoint pen from his pocket. Not the Authority pen. A cheap, blue Bic pen.

He wrote.

*Entry: Unknown Infestation.* *Behavior: Swarm intelligence.* *Weakness: Structural integrity of wall cavity.*

He wasn't identifying a weakness in the monster. He was identifying a weakness in the house.

The scratching grew louder. The drywall bulged.

Dale took a deep breath. He raised the clipboard.

He didn't swing it like a weapon, and he swung it like a hammer.

He smashed the corner of the clipboard into the baseboard. Once. Twice. Three times.

The wood splintered.

The scratching stopped.

Silence.

Then, a voice from the shadows of the crawlspace.

"Nice form," the voice said. "But you're holding the wrong tool."

Dale spun around.

Officer Miller was standing in the corner of the basement. He wasn't holding a gun.

He was holding a sword.

A glowing, magical sword.

The same sword from the photograph.

Miller smiled. It wasn't a friendly smile. It was the smile of a man who had been waiting for Dale to make a mistake.

"The Authority doesn't know about this one, Dale," Miller said. "But I do."

Dale looked at his empty hands. He looked at the shattered baseboard. He looked at Miller.

The system notification flashed in his eyes.

**NEW VIOLATION DETECTED.** **Unlicensed Collaboration.** **Penalty: Immediate Termination.**

Dale didn't run. He didn't fight.

He opened his Field Guide to the last blank page, and he wrote down the date.

And then he looked at Miller, and for the first time in his career, Dale Kowalski didn't write a report.

He wrote a threat.
