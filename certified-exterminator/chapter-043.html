Chapter 43: Certified by Breaking Rules

# Chapter 43

The hallway was not a hallway.

Dale knew this within four steps. The linoleum had been the same beige as every other linoleum in the county, but now it was warm.

Not heated-warm.

Body-warm. The tiles pressed slightly against his boots like skin over muscle, and the grout lines had narrowed to hairline cracks that wept a clear fluid with a pH Dale could smell before he saw it.

He stopped. Kneaded his knees. Stood back up.

His clipboard buzzed.

NOTIFICATION: PRACTICAL EXAMINATION SCENARIO INITIATED. OBJECTIVE: IDENTIFY, ISOLATE, ELIMINATE, VERIFY. TIME LIMIT: NONE. NOTE: THIS IS NOT A DRILL.

Dale read it twice.

Then he crouched and touched the floor with his gloved index finger. The fluid came up clear, but when he brought his hand to his face, the smell was wrong.

Not chemical.

Biological. It smelled like the inside of a mouth.

He pulled out his spray bottle. Not the Authority-issue canister with its graduated concentration dial.

His personal bottle.

Half-empty, and the label was faded but still legible: Pyrethrin 0.5%. Useless against anything above Class 1.

He held it anyway. Habit.

The hallway extended. It should not have extended.

The municipal building had a hallway that ran forty feet to the stairwell, then turned left toward the records room.

Dale had walked this hallway eleven times during his certification paperwork. He knew the tile count. He knew the number of fluorescent panels.

The hallway now ran two hundred feet in a straight line. The panels above were not fluorescent. They were membrane-thin, veined with capillaries that pulsed a dim amber. The walls were drywall on one side and something chitinous on the other, segmented plates that clicked when the air moved.

Dale walked forward.

Slowly. His clipboard was out.

His pen was out, and he was writing.

Habitat modification. Substrate replacement. Bioluminescent substrate. Chitinous wall overlay.

The Field Guide populated a new entry before he finished the sentence.

NEW FIELD GUIDE ENTRY: ARCHITECT (Habitat Modification Organism). CLASS: 4.

STATUS: UNDOCUMENTED IN PREVIOUS EXAMINATION.

NOTE: ARCHITECTS DO NOT FIGHT.

THEY BUILD. THE ENVIRONMENT IS THE WEAPON.

WEAKNESS: NOT YET IDENTIFIED.

Dale stopped writing. Read the entry. Read it again.

The Authority had given him a Class 4 creature with no identified weakness and no time limit. His personal spray was rated for Class 1. His Authority-issue canister was rated for Class 2. His Field Guide was empty on this species.

He should not be here. He should not be in this hallway.

The protocol was clear: if the workspace does not meet standards, you do not engage.

You file a non-compliance report and you leave. You do not walk two hundred feet into a chitinous throat with a half-empty pyrethrin bottle.

But the notification said THIS IS NOT A DRILL, and the smell said that the building had been empty for weeks. And the floor was warm and breathing and the walls were clicking.

Dale walked forward.

The hallway opened into a room.

Or what had been a room. The ceiling was twenty feet high and lined with the same veined membrane.

The floor was the warm tile, and the walls were chitin and drywall in alternating panels, like someone had tried to renovate and gotten halfway through.

In the center of the room stood a woman.

She was tall. Class VI insignia on her collar.

She held a clipboard that looked newer than Dale's by about three years.

Her boots were clean.

Her gloves were clean. Everything about her was clean, which meant she had been here less than five minutes and was already leaving.

"You're late," she said.

"Late for what?"

"The exam." She checked her watch. "You were supposed to enter the scenario window at 0600. It's 0947."

"I didn't know there was a scenario window."

She looked at him the way a building inspector looks at a homeowner who's been sleeping in a condemned house. "The notification went out three days ago."

"I didn't get a notification."

"You got a notification." She pointed at his clipboard. "That's the notification."

Dale looked down. The notification was still there. PRACTICAL EXAMINATION SCENARIO INITIATED.

No timestamp. No window.

He'd been reading it for the last three minutes and it hadn't changed.

"The window closed," the woman said. "The scenario is still active, but you're outside the evaluation period. Everything you do in here now is unscored."

"Then what's the point?"

"The point is that the Architect is still here." She gestured at the room. "And it's been building for three days without a technician present. Do you know what an Architect does when it's not being monitored?"

Dale looked at the walls. The chitin was thicker than it had been in the hallway, and the panels were overlapping, layered, like plywood laid over plywood.

The membrane on the ceiling had grown into something that looked like the inside of a lung.

Alveoli. Hundreds of them.

"It's been nesting," Dale said.

"It's been renovating," the woman corrected.

"And when it finishes, this room won't be a room anymore. It'll be an organ. And the building will be a body.

And everything in a thirty-block radius will be substrate."

Dale looked at his clipboard. He looked at the room. He looked at the woman.

"What's your name?" he asked.

"Vasquez. Class VI. I set up the scenario."

"You set up the scenario and left."

"I set up the scenario and left. That's my job. I prepare. You execute."

"You prepared wrong."

Vasquez's expression didn't change. "The scenario was calibrated for a 0600 entry. You entered at 0947. The discrepancy is yours."

"The discrepancy is yours.

You didn't lock the perimeter.

You didn't set a containment protocol. You walked out and let a Class 4 build for three days in an enclosed space with no ventilation control." Dale pointed at the ceiling.

"That's a ventilation violation. Code 12-C. Inadequate air exchange in a contaminated workspace."

Vasquez looked at the ceiling. Looked at him. "You're citing a building code."

"I'm citing a protocol violation. If the workspace wasn't prepared to standard, the scenario is invalid. I can't be evaluated in an invalid scenario."

"You can't do that."

"I can file a non-compliance report." Dale was already writing. "Workspace fails ventilation standard.

Substrate contamination exceeds threshold.

Scenario parameters exceeded due to technician negligence. Recommendation: scenario voided, re-scheduled."

The clipboard buzzed.

NOTIFICATION: NON-COMPLIANCE REPORT FILED. STATUS: UNDER REVIEW. NOTE: EVALUATION SUSPENDED PENDING AUTHORITY DETERMINATION. ESTIMATED REVIEW TIME: 4-6 HOURS.

Vasquez stared at the notification. Her jaw tightened. "You just bought yourself six hours in this room."

"I bought myself six hours where I'm not being evaluated."

"That's not what the suspension means. The suspension means you're still in the scenario. The scenario doesn't stop because you filed a report. The Architect doesn't stop because you filed a report."

Dale looked at the walls, and the chitin was spreading. He could see it now, if he looked at the edges where it met the drywall.

A slow tide.

The plates were pushing outward like frost on glass.

"Then I'll work," Dale said.

"You're not scored."

"I know."

"You won't get Treatment Points."

"I know."

Vasquez turned to leave. At the door she stopped. "You know what happens if you don't clear this room in six hours?"

"The building becomes an organ."

"The building becomes an organ. And then the city becomes a body.

And then the county becomes a body.

And at some point, the Authority stops sending technicians and starts sending demolition teams.

And demolition teams don't write reports."

She left. The door clicked shut. Dale heard her footsteps fade down the hall, shifting until they vanished.

Dale was alone with the Architect, and he looked at his Field Guide entry. ARCHITECT. CLASS 4. WEAKNESS: NOT YET IDENTIFIED.

He looked at the room. The renovation. The substrate replacement.

The bioluminescent panels. The lung ceiling.

He walked to the wall where the chitin met the drywall.

He put his hand on it. The plates were warm. They flexed under his palm like a living hinge.

He pulled his pen out and scraped at the edge where the chitin overlapped the drywall.

The material came up in a curl, like a paint chip. Beneath it lay a fibrous substance.

White.

Threaded.

Mycelium.

Not chitin. Fungal hyphae.

The plates weren't exoskeletal, and they were fruiting bodies.

The Architect wasn't an insect. It wasn't a crustacean.

It was a fungus that had learned to build.

Dale's Field Guide updated.

FIELD GUIDE ENTRY REVISED: ARCHITECT (Habitat Modification Organism). CLASS: 4.

CORRECTED CLASSIFICATION: FUNGAL.

NOT ARTHROPOD. WEAKNESS IDENTIFIED: SENSITIVE TO COPPER SULFATE (FUNGICIDE). TARGETED DAMAGE BONUS: +45% AGAINST ARCHITECT.

Dale looked at his Authority-issue canister. Class 2. Pyrethrin-based. Useless against fungi.

He looked at his personal bottle. Pyrethrin 0.5%. Useless against fungi.

He looked at the room.

Two hundred square feet of fungal infrastructure spreading at a rate he could see with his eyes. The alveoli on the ceiling were filling.

The walls were thickening, and the floor was softening.

He had no fungicide. He had no copper sulfate.

He had no protocol for Class 4 fungi.

His certification was Class II.

His toolkit was Class I and II. Everything in his kit was designed for arthropods and rodents.

The notification said NO TIME LIMIT. But the room disagreed. The room was closing.

Dale sat down on the floor. The floor was warm. It yielded slightly under his weight like memory foam. He put his clipboard on his knee and started writing.

He wrote the report he'd already filed. He wrote it again, in different words. He wrote the observations he'd made.

The substrate. The hyphae.

The fruiting bodies.

The growth rate. The ventilation failure. The technician negligence.

He wrote for twenty minutes.

The room grew around him. The alveoli were closer now.

The walls were closer, and the floor was softer.

At the twenty-second minute, he stopped writing. He looked at his Field Guide.

ARCHITECT. FUNGAL. COPPER SULFATE.

He looked at the building around him. Municipal building.

Built 1987.

Plumbing was copper.

He'd seen the copper pipe exposed during the inspection that started all of this. Fourteen years ago.

Chapter one of his life.

The plumbing. The building's plumbing was copper. And copper corrodes. And corrosion produces copper sulfate when exposed to moisture and sulfur compounds.

The Architect was producing moisture. The room was warm and wet.

The floor was weeping, and the walls were sweating. If he could find a copper pipe that was exposed, corroded, and in contact with the fungal moisture...

He stood.

He walked to the wall where the drywall met the chitin; pulled his pen out and cut through the drywall.

Behind it: a copper pipe.

Green with verdigris. Old. Leaking slightly at the joint.

Dale pressed his clipboard against the pipe, and the moisture from the pipe touched the fungal hyphae at the wall's edge. The hyphae curled. The fruiting body at the edge of the wall blackened and sloughed off like dead skin.

It worked. Copper sulfate. The corrosion product. The building's own plumbing was a fungicide.

Dale pulled the drywall back further. More pipe.

More corrosion, and he found the shut-off valve.

He turned it. The pipe stopped leaking.

He turned it back. The pipe leaked again. He was controlling the dose.

He walked the perimeter of the room. Every wall with drywall had a pipe behind it.

Every pipe was corroded.

Every corroded pipe produced copper sulfate when it contacted moisture. The Architect was producing moisture. The room was feeding itself poison.

Dale cut the drywall in sections, and he exposed the pipes. He opened the joints.

He let the moisture from the floor contact the copper.

The blackening spread. The alveoli deflated. The chitin plates curled and fell.

He worked for three hours. His hands were raw.

His knees were worse.

The room shrank.

The membrane receded. The floor cooled.

The tiles hardened.

At the fourth hour, the room was a room again. The ceiling was a ceiling.

The walls were drywall and paint and water stains, and the floor was linoleum. Cold. Hard.

Dead.

His clipboard buzzed.

NOTIFICATION: SCENARIO RESOLVED. EVALUATION STATUS: SUSPENDED (NON-COMPLIANCE REPORT UNDER REVIEW).

TREATMENT POINTS: 0.

NOTE: SCENARIO RESOLVED OUTSIDE EVALUATION WINDOW. NO POINTS AWARDED. NOTE: THOROUGHNESS RATING: NOT ASSESSED.

Dale read it. He put the clipboard down.

Then it buzzed again.

NOTIFICATION: ADDITIONAL ENTRY. THOROUGHNESS RATING: 9.7.

NOTE: RATING ASSIGNED BY EXTERNAL OBSERVER.

NOT VALID FOR CERTIFICATION PROGRESS.

NOTE: EXTERNAL OBSERVER: VASQUEZ, M. CLASS VI.

NOTE: VASQUEZ, M. HAS FILED A RECOMMENDATION FOR YOUR IMMEDIATE PROMOTION TO LEVEL IV. NOTE: CLASS IV REQUIRES WRITTEN EXAMINATION.

NOTE: WRITTEN EXAMINATION WILL BE ADMINISTERED BY VASQUEZ, M. NOTE: VASQUEZ, M.

HAS NOT YET PASSED HER OWN CLASS V EXAMINATION.

NOTE: THIS IS A PROTOCOL VIOLATION.

Dale stared at the notification. Vasquez had rated his work. Vasquez had recommended his promotion. Vasquez was not qualified to do either of those things.

And the Authority had accepted the recommendation anyway.

His clipboard buzzed one final time.

NOTIFICATION: WRITTEN EXAMINATION SCHEDULED.

DATE: TOMORROW. TIME: 0600.

LOCATION: [REDACTED].

EXAMINER: VASQUEZ, M. NOTE: EXAMINER HAS INDICATED THAT THIS EXAMINATION WILL TEST YOUR COMPLIANCE WITH AUTHORITY DIRECTIVES THAN YOUR TECHNICAL KNOWLEDGE. NOTE: THIS IS NOT A PROTOCOL VIOLATION.

THIS IS THE PROTOCOL.

The hallway was a hallway again.

Linoleum.

Fluorescent. Coffee smell.

Fresh coffee. Someone was alive in the building.

Dale walked to the stairwell. He went down.

He went outside, and the parking lot was a parking lot. The sky was gray and normal and Ohio.

He sat in his truck. He looked at his clipboard. He looked at the notification about tomorrow's exam.

Vasquez was going to test whether he'd follow orders. Not whether he knew how to kill things. Whether he'd follow orders.

And the Authority had approved that.

Dale started the truck, and he drove home. He did not think about the fact that the Authority had lied to him about the notification window.

He did not think about the fact that the scenario had been designed to fail.

He did not think about the fact that Vasquez had known the room would be three days into its renovation cycle when she left it unattended.

He thought about copper sulfate. He thought about the fact that a building's own infrastructure could be weaponized against an infestation if you understood the materials.

He thought about the fact that this was not in any protocol, which was not in any Field Guide.

This was not in any certification track.

He thought about the fact that tomorrow, the Authority was going to ask him whether he'd follow their rules.

And he thought about the fact that he'd just won by breaking one.

He wrote it down anyway.
