Chapter 4

The blank was no longer blank. Dale had filled it in with three words in his standard block print, the same handwriting he used on violation notices and service tickets, and the moment the pen left the page the notification arrived.

FIELD GUIDE ENTRY COMPLETE. SPECIES: Cavernous Burrower (Geotermes profundus). WEAKNESS IDENTIFIED: Ventral plating discontinuity at segment 7-8 junction. PRE-IDENTIFICATION BONUS: +35% targeted damage, ventral segment 7-8, duration 48 hours. NOTE: Bonus voids if Field Guide entry is contaminated.

Thirty-five percent. Not much. But it was the difference between a protocol that worked and a protocol that didn't, and Dale had spent fourteen years learning that the difference between working and not working was the whole job.

He set the clipboard on the truck bed and pulled out his phone. The clock read 11:47. The ninety-minute window had started at 11:15. He had twenty-eight minutes before he knew whether the bait had killed the colony or announced his presence to something that could kill him back.

He used the time to check his perimeter. The duplex was still settling, the foundation cracking in that slow geological way that made the whole structure groan like a ship. The tunnel entrance was sealed with the trash bag and duct tape he'd improvised in Chapter One, which was now bulging outward at the center. Something was pushing from below.

Dale noted it. Wrote it down. Perimeter integrity: compromised. Estimated breach time: unknown.

That was when the second truck showed up.

It was a white pickup with a rack in the bed and a logo on the door that Dale recognized. Apex Wildlife Removal. A competitor from the next county over, the kind of operation that did wildlife exclusions and bat guano cleanups and charged triple for after-hours calls. The truck parked at an angle that blocked Dale's service lane. The driver's door opened and a man got out wearing a face shield, leather gauntlets, and a hunting vest with too many pockets.

The man looked at Dale. Dale looked at the man.

"You the one running the job here?" the man asked.

"I'm running the job here," Dale said.

"Authority flagged it as open. I got a ping on my phone twenty minutes ago."

"Then your ping is wrong."

The man pulled out his phone. Held it up. The screen showed a notification in the same amber font Dale had been seeing for three days.

UNLICENSED INFESTATION: ACTIVE. LOCATION: 4417 MAPLE DRIVE, UNIT B. STATUS: UNCLAIMED. CLAIM THIS JOB: [TAP TO ACCEPT]

Dale looked at the screen. Then he looked at his own phone. His job status read: ACTIVE. THREAT LEVEL: CLASS 4. OPERATOR: KOWALSKI, D. CERTIFICATION: LEVEL I.

"Your system didn't update," Dale said. "The job is claimed."

The man squinted. "Must be a sync issue." He put his phone away and started pulling equipment out of his truck bed. A propane torch. A canister of something that smelled like gasoline. A hatchet.

"Hey," Dale said. "What are you doing?"

"Treating the infestation."

"You're not treating anything. You're escalating it."

The man set the hatchet on the tailgate. "I'm certified. Level One. Same as you."

"Then you know the protocol."

"I know enough." He grabbed the propane torch and walked toward the duplex.

Dale followed. Not because he was worried about the man's safety, though he was, but because the Authority's system had a rule about active treatment zones that most people didn't read closely. Section 14, subsection C: Unauthorized treatment interference within a claimed job site constitutes a Class 2 violation for the interfering operator and a shared-responsibility citation for the primary operator.

The primary operator was Dale.

"Stop," Dale said. He kept his voice flat. The same voice he used when a homeowner wanted to know why he couldn't just spray and leave. "You need to claim this job through the system first. You can't just walk into an active zone with an open flame."

"I'm not walking into an active zone. I'm treating a nest."

"That's not what you're doing."

The man had already cracked the seal on the propane torch. The pilot light hissed. He aimed it at the base of the duplex, at the foundation crack where the tunnel entrance was.

Dale watched the flame hit the duct tape.

The tape melted. The trash bag caught. The seal was gone.

And then the notification came. Not for Dale. For the man.

TREATMENT PROTOCOL: INCORRECT. OPEN FLAME IS NOT A VALID ISOLATION METHOD FOR GEOTERMES PROFUNDUS. CROSS-CONTAMINATION EVENT: FIELD GUIDE ENTRY "CAVERNOUS BURROWER" CONTAMINATED. PRE-IDENTIFICATION BONUS: VOID. CONTAMINATION DURATION: 24 HOURS.

Dale felt the notification land in his peripheral vision like a cold stone. His thirty-five percent bonus. Gone. Because this man with his hatchet and his propane torch had treated a subterranean arthropod like it was a wasp nest.

"Hey," the man said, looking at his own notification. "That's not right. I'm certified."

"You're certified to treat wasps," Dale said. "You're not certified to treat this."

"How do you know what it is?"

"Because I documented it."

The man looked at the burning trash bag. Looked at the tunnel entrance, now open again. Looked at Dale.

"Fine. You want to do it your way. Do it your way." He walked back to his truck. "But I'm not leaving. I'm filing a complaint with the Authority. This is a shared resource. You can't just lock people out of a job because you got there first."

"I didn't lock you out. You didn't claim it."

"System glitch."

"Your system glitch is now contaminating my Field Guide."

The man got in his truck. Started the engine. Didn't drive away.

Dale looked at his clipboard. The Field Guide entry was still there. The species name, the weakness, the notes. But the bonus line was greyed out. Void. He had written it correctly. He had identified the ventral plating discontinuity by measuring the frass trail diameter against the tunnel cross-section and cross-referencing it with known arthropod molting patterns. He had done it in twenty minutes with a tape measure and a flashlight. And some guy with a propane torch had erased it.

The clock read 11:58. Nineteen minutes.

Dale pulled out his spray canister. Not the one he'd used on the workers. A different one. Class 1 insecticide, standard pyrethroid blend. Useless against a Class 4. He knew it was useless. He was going to use it anyway, because the protocol required an elimination attempt, and the Authority recognized the attempt even if the substance was inadequate. What mattered was the documentation of the attempt.

But documentation wasn't enough. He needed the elimination step to actually work, and without the weakness bonus, his spray was just spray.

He needed a different approach.

He looked at the tunnel entrance. The walls were packed earth, slightly moist, with a faint iridescent sheen. Chitin residue. The creature shed as it moved. The tunnel was its body, in a way. He'd seen this pattern before. Not with monsters. With carpenter ants. The galleries they built were structural, load-bearing, and if you compromised the geometry at the right point, the whole system collapsed inward.

Behavioral isolation.

The protocol said: isolate the threat. Most people read that as physical containment. A box. A cage. A wall. Dale had been reading it differently since he was twenty-four, because you can't put a wall around a carpenter ant colony inside a wall. You isolate them behaviorally. You manipulate their movement patterns so they go where you want them to go. You make the environment do the containment for you.

He walked to the east side of the duplex. The foundation crack ran along this side too, narrower than the main tunnel entrance but connected. He could see it because he'd done a structural inspection of this building six months ago. He'd written a report about water intrusion on the east foundation. Code violation S-3. He'd noted the crack pattern.

He knew where the secondary tunnel ran.

He pulled out his phone and opened the Authority's protocol reference. Isolation methods. He scrolled past physical barriers, past chemical barriers, past thermal barriers. There, at the bottom, in the fine print that nobody read:

ISOLATION METHOD 7: BEHAVIORAL REDIRECTION. DEFINITION: Manipulation of environmental stimuli to direct target organism(s) into a confined or predictable spatial pattern, effectively preventing dispersal without physical barrier. ACCEPTANCE CRITERIA: Operator must document the redirection mechanism, the predicted organism path, and the containment zone geometry. NOTE: This method is commonly employed in structural pest management and is recognized by the Authority as valid isolation.

Dale read it twice. Then he started writing.

CONTAINMENT ZONE: EAST FOUNDATION CORRIDOR, 2.4 METERS IN LENGTH, 0.6 METERS IN WIDTH, CONNECTED TO PRIMARY TUNNEL VIA SECONDARY GALLERY AT APPROXIMATE DEPTH 1.8 METERS. REDIRECTION MECHANISM: VIBRATION PATTERN. CLASS 4 ORGANISM DEMONSTRATES THIGMOTACTIC RESPONSE TO SUBSTRATE VIBRATION (OBSERVED: WORKER RESPONSE TO FOOTFALLS, DIRECTIONAL MOVEMENT TOWARD LOW-FREQUENCY STIMULI). PREDICTED PATH: ORGANISM WILL FOLLOW VIBRATION SOURCE THROUGH SECONDARY GALLERY INTO EAST FOUNDATION CORRIDOR. CONTAINMENT METHOD: CORRIDOR IS GEOMETRICALLY CONSTRICTED. ORGANISM CANNOT TURN. IT WILL BE TRAPPED IN A 2.4-METER SPACE WHERE IT CANNOT REORIENT. ELIMINATION METHOD: APPLICATION OF CLASS 1 PYRETHROID TO VENTRAL SURFACE IN CONSTRAINED POSITION. NOTE: WITHOUT PRE-IDENTIFICATION BONUS, ELIMINATION PROBABILITY REDUCED TO 60%. NOTE: 60% IS BETTER THAN 0%.

He wrote the whole thing. Every line. The Authority's system processed it as he wrote, and when he finished, the notification came.

ISOLATION PROTOCOL: VALID. BEHAVIORAL REDIRECTION ACCEPTED. CONTAINMENT ZONE: CONFIRMED. NEXT STEP: ELIMINATION. NOTE: ELIMINATION MUST OCCUR WITHIN 15 MINUTES OF ISOLATION SETUP OR PROTOCOL DEGRADES TO INCOMPLETE.

Fifteen minutes. The clock read 12:03. The ninety-minute window had twelve minutes left. The poison bait either killed the colony or it didn't. If it didn't, the Class 4 would come up through the main tunnel. Dale needed it to come up through the secondary gallery instead.

He needed to make it come up the east side.

He walked to the east foundation. The crack was there, just like his old inspection report said. He could hear it. A low vibration, like a diesel engine idling underground. The colony was moving. The bait was being carried down. Or it wasn't being carried down. Or the thing at the bottom had tasted it and was angry.

Dale set his phone on the ground next to the crack. He opened the alarm app. Set it to a low-frequency tone. 40 hertz. The frequency that made the workers move toward it. He'd noticed it on the first day. Every time he walked near the tunnel, the workers oriented toward him. His footsteps were the stimulus. He'd been using it without knowing he was using it.

He pressed play.

The tone hummed. The ground answered. Something shifted underground.

He waited.

The vibration moved. He could feel it through his boots. It was coming toward the east side. Toward the phone. Toward the narrow corridor where a Class 4 organism couldn't turn around.

Dale uncapped his spray canister. He held it at the ready. He stood over the crack and he waited, and the clock read 12:08, and the ground was moving, and the man in the white truck was watching from his cab with his hand on the door handle, and the duplex groaned.

Then the east crack split open.

Not slowly. Not geologically. It split open the way a ribcage splits open, with a wet tearing sound, and something came up through it. Not a rat. Not a beetle. Something with too many segments and a body that was mostly armor and a head that was mostly mandibles, and it was the size of a cinder block, and it was blind, and it was looking for the vibration.

Dale sprayed.

He sprayed the ventral side. The segment 7-8 junction. The place he'd identified from frass trails and tunnel geometry and nothing else. He sprayed it without the bonus, without the thirty-five percent, with nothing but a can of pyrethroid and the knowledge of exactly where to put it.

The creature screamed. It was not a sound he had a word for. It was the sound of a joint popping in a structure that was too large to be a joint. It thrashed in the corridor. It couldn't turn. The geometry held.

He sprayed again. And again. The canister was going empty.

The creature stopped thrashing.

Dale capped the canister. He pulled out his clipboard. He wrote: ELIMINATION ATTEMPT: COMPLETE. SUBSTANCE: CLASS 1 PYRETHROID. TARGET AREA: VENTRAL SEGMENT 7-8. RESULT: PENDING VERIFICATION.

He waited.

The notification came.

ELIMINATION: CONFIRMED. COLONY STATUS: TERMINATED. THREAT LEVEL: RESOLVED. JOB COMPLETE.

Dale let out a breath he'd been holding since 11:15.

Then the second notification came.

THOROUGHNESS RATING: 9.4. CERTIFICATION PROGRESS: 340/500 TREATMENT POINTS TO LEVEL II. NEW VIOLATION LOGGED: CLASS 2 UNAUTHORIZED INTERFERENCE. OPERATOR: KOWALSKI, D. SHARED RESPONSIBILITY: 15% PENALTY APPLIED. NOTE: FUTURE INTERFERENCE EVENTS WILL INCREASE PENALTY TO 30%, THEN 50%. WARNING: OPERATOR IS ADVISED TO SECURE ACTIVE JOB SITES AGAINST THIRD-PARTY INTERFERENCE.

Fifteen percent. His rating dropped from 9.4 to 9.2. He could feel it like a bruise.

He looked up. The man in the white truck was gone. The truck was gone. The service lane was empty.

But the notification hadn't finished.

NEW INFESTATION DETECTED. LOCATION: 4417 MAPLE DRIVE, UNIT A. DISTANCE FROM PREVIOUS SITE: 4 METERS. THREAT LEVEL: CLASS 3. SPECIES: UNKNOWN. NOTE: UNIT A WAS NOT PART OF ORIGINAL JOB SCOPE. NOTE: UNIT A WAS NOT INSPECTED BY OPERATOR. NOTE: UNIT A WAS NOT SEPARATELY CLAIMED. NOTE: AUTHORITY CLASSIFIES THIS AS A SINGLE CONTIGUOUS INFESTATION EVENT. NOTE: OPERATOR IS RESPONSIBLE FOR ALL CONTIGUOUS SITES.

Four meters. The duplex next door. The one he hadn't touched. The one where, if he was right, the same broodmother had been expanding its range while he was dealing with the east side.

Dale looked at the canister in his hand. Empty.

He looked at his clipboard. One page left.

He looked at the notification.

And then his phone buzzed. Not the Authority. A text message. From a number he didn't recognize.

"Hey Dale. It's Rick. Apex. Look, I filed the complaint but I'm not an idiot. I saw what you did. The behavioral thing. I want to learn it. I've got six other sites in my county that are all going active tonight. I can't handle them. Can we talk?"

Dale stared at the text. He stared at the notification about Unit A. He stared at his empty canister.

He typed back: "Bring me a canister."

He hit send. Then he looked at the duplex. Unit A. The door was closed. The windows were dark. And the ground in front of it was moving.

Not settling. Moving. Like something was breathing underneath.
