Chapter 203: Archival Parasite Detected

# Chapter 203

The security camera feed from Sector 4 was grainy, distorted by the static interference of the dimensional rift. On the monitor, a speck of movement caught the eye of Junior Analyst Vane. He adjusted the gain, squinting at the black-and-white footage.

Most technicians would have seen a blur. Vane saw a posture.

The figure in the grey suit stood perfectly still for eleven seconds. No fidgeting.

No scanning the horizon for threats.

Just standing with the rigid, bored patience of a man waiting for a bus that was running late. Then, the figure moved. Not with the explosive energy of a combatant, but with the deliberate, rhythmic shuffle of someone checking the perimeter of a job site.

Vane tapped his stylus against the desk.

"Pause it. Zoom in on the clipboard."

The image blurred, then sharpened, and the clipboard was standard issue, municipal grade. But the hand holding it wasn't shaking.

It was steady.

And the pen tip was moving in small, circular motions.

"Is he marking entry points?" Vane asked.

The senior analyst, a woman with eyes like chipped flint, leaned over his shoulder.

"He's documenting.

He hasn't drawn a weapon, and he hasn't activated a protocol.

He's just... writing.

"

"That's insane," Vane said. "There are Class V Archival Mites in that library. They eat the concept of memory. One touch and you forget your own name."

"And yet," the senior analyst murmured, "he hasn't forgotten. Look at the footprints."

Vane looked. The footprints weren't chaotic. They were parallel lines.

A grid. The man was walking a systematic grid pattern, covering every square inch of the visible floor before stepping onto the first rung of the ladder.

"He's clearing the floor first," the senior analyst said. "Before he goes up."

"Why?"

"Because if he gets knocked off the ladder, he lands on the infestation. If he clears the floor, he has a safe landing zone. He's not fighting the monster. He's managing the hazard."

Vane felt a cold prickle at the base of his neck. In the Licensing Authority, speed was often rewarded, but thoroughness was currency. This man wasn't just thorough. He was obsessive.

"Pull his file," Vane said. "Now."

***

Dale's boots crunched on the debris of the broken ceiling. The air up here was thick, tasting of copper and ink. He didn't look at the books. He looked at the floor.

The transition from the concrete basement to the infinite library was seamless in the eyes of a novice. To Dale, it was a structural anomaly. The flooring changed from reinforced concrete to a material that looked like polished bone. It was smooth, slippery, and unsuited for traction.

He stopped. He didn't step forward.

Instead, he crouched. He pulled a small vial from his belt—the one containing crushed chitin from a previous beetle extermination—and sprinkled it on the floor. The powder hit the bone-surface and hissed. A faint grey smoke rose up.

"Corrosive," Dale muttered.

"Or acidic. Either way, it eats organic matter. Including boots.

Including feet.

"

He stood up and checked his Field Guide. The entry for *Archival Mites* was still blank regarding weaknesses, but a new sub-entry had populated automatically: *Environmental Hazard: Ink-Rust. Causes rapid degradation of cellular bonds upon contact.*

This wasn't a monster fight. This was a slip-and-fall lawsuit waiting to happen.

Dale uncapped his spray canister. It wasn't the high-yield nerve gas he usually carried.

It was a heavy-duty industrial adhesive, Class 4, designed for sealing porous surfaces in residential foundations.

He sprayed a thick, wide band across the floor in front of him. The adhesive turned black and hardened instantly, creating a rough, textured path.

He stepped onto it. His boot gripped.

Above him, the library stretched into a darkness that had no ceiling. The shelves were massive, towering structures of wood and shadow. And on them, the movement was visible.

They were small, no bigger than a thumb, but they moved with the coordinated efficiency of a school of fish. Their bodies were translucent, pulsing with a faint blue light that seemed to be the text of the books they consumed.

As they ate, the books didn't burn, and they just faded.

The text dissolved into mist, and the mist was sucked into the mite's spiracles.

If he went up there, he would be surrounded. The vertical space was a trap. The mites could drop from above, scuttle sideways, or launch themselves.

There was no cover. No walls to press his back against.

Dale looked at the ladder. It was the only way up.

He didn't climb it, and he took out his clipboard and flipped to a blank page. He wrote: *STRATEGY: VERTICAL CONFINEMENT.*

He looked at the nearest bookshelf. It was a massive pillar, easily twenty feet high, loaded with thousands of volumes. The mites were clustered around the top, feeding on the most ancient texts.

Dale walked over to the pillar. He didn't touch it. He kept a two-foot distance, maintaining a clean workspace buffer. He scanned the base of the shelf.

There, in the corner where the shelf met the bone-floor, was a gap. A structural fissure. The library's architecture was failing under the weight of the infestation.

He pointed his flashlight at the gap.

The light revealed a dark cavity beneath the shelf. Something was moving inside it. Not a mite.

Something larger.

A hand emerged from the darkness. Pale, elongated fingers with too many joints.

It gripped the edge of the shelf.

Then another hand. A head pushed through. It was gaunt, skin stretched tight over bone, with eyes that were blank, white spheres.

A *Librarian Construct*. Class VI.

It pulled itself up, its body unfolding like a carpenter's ruler. It looked at Dale.

It didn't scream.

It didn't charge.

It opened its mouth, and a sound came out that was like the rustling of a thousand pages turning at once.

"Unauthorized," the Construct rasped. "Return to your shelf."

Dale didn't answer. He didn't engage. He was checking the structural integrity of the shelf.

If he killed the Construct, the shelf might collapse. If the shelf collapsed, the books would fall. If the books fell, the mites would scatter.

Scattering was bad. Scattering made them impossible to track and verify.

"Protocol requires isolation," Dale said to himself. "I need to contain the population before I reduce the biomass."

He pulled a flare from his belt.

Not a combat flare. A signaling flare. He lit it and tossed it into the gap beneath the shelf.

The red light flooded the cavity.

The Construct recoiled. It hated light. It hissed, scrambling back into the darkness.

But the light revealed the source. The nest.

Inside the cavity was a pulsating mass of blue mist and chitinous shells. The mites were breeding in the dark, protected by the Construct.

Dale checked his Treatment Points. He had enough for one standard elimination protocol.

But not enough for a Class VI fight.

And he didn't want to fight a Class VI. He wanted to exterminate the Class V infestation feeding on the books.

He looked at his adhesive canister. He looked at the flare. He looked at the ladder.

A loophole.

The System rewarded *identification, documentation, isolation, elimination, verification.*

He had identified. He had documented. Now he needed isolation.

He couldn't seal the gap with adhesive; the Construct was too strong. He couldn't burn it; that would destroy the evidence and the books.

But he could bait it.

Dale reached into his pocket and pulled out a single, fresh notebook. A standard-issue municipal inspection log. He opened it to a blank page and wrote a single word in large letters: *NEW.*

He tore the page out, and he held the paper up. The Construct froze. The mites on the shelves above stopped moving. The air grew silent.

The Construct tilted its head. The blank white eyes seemed to focus on the paper. To a creature made of archival magic, new information was a physical force. A paradox.

"Fresh ink," the Construct whispered. "Unwritten."

Dale didn't speak. He walked to the edge of the bone-floor, away from the shelf. He placed the paper on the ground.

Then he stepped back, creating a clear, empty space.

The Construct hesitated. It looked at the mites, then at the paper.

The hunger in its blank eyes shifted from the old books to the new potential of the blank page.

It was a predatory instinct, hardwired by centuries of consuming knowledge.

It could not resist something that hadn't been read.

Slowly, the Construct unfolded itself from the gap. It crawled out onto the bone-floor, its long limbs clicking like abacus beads. It approached the paper.

Dale watched his Field Guide. The *Archival Mites* entry flashed a new status: *DISTRACTED.*

The mites on the shelves were still feeding, but their coordination had faltered. They were confused by the sudden shift in the Construct's attention.

Now.

Dale moved. He didn't run. He glided, using his adhesive-sprayed path.

He reached the base of the ladder. He didn't climb.

He climbed *down*, and he slid down the ladder, back into the basement. The library loomed above him, the Construct now fully exposed on the bone-floor, distracted by the paper.

Dale landed on the concrete of the basement. He looked up.

The ceiling of the basement was still open. The library floated above, untethered.

He pulled out his heavy-duty sealant gun. The one used for waterproofing foundations. It was loaded with a rapid-cure polymer foam.

He aimed it at the edges of the ceiling hole. Not at the library. At the frame.

He fired.

The foam expanded with violent speed, filling the cracks between the concrete floor of the basement and the bone-floor of the library above. It sealed the gap, creating a temporary, rigid barrier.

Then he aimed at the bottom of the ladder, where it connected to the library floor.

He fired again.

The foam expanded, encasing the bottom of the ladder and bonding it to the ceiling frame.

He wasn't trapping the library. He was tethering it.

By sealing the connection points, he was creating a closed system. The library was no longer just a floating anomaly. It was now physically anchored to the basement.

According to the Licensing Authority's bylaws, an infestation in a

"structurally compromised environment" could be treated with a *Containment Protocol* than a *Combat Protocol*. The penalty for using Containment was lower points, but the speed was higher, and it prevented collateral damage to the surrounding infrastructure.

Dale checked his Field Guide.

*SYSTEM ALERT: STRUCTURAL INTEGRITY RESTORED. INFESTATION ISOLATED.* *PROTOCOL AVAILABLE: SUFFOCATION.*

Suffocation.

The Archival Mites needed air. Or, more accurately, they needed the ambient magical field that sustained the library's existence. By sealing the library into the basement, Dale had cut off the external dimensional bleed that kept the library's unique physics active.

The library was now just a room.

A closed room in a concrete basement, and the air supply was finite.

Dale stepped back. He pulled a heavy canvas tarp from his pack—the kind used for covering asbestos removal sites. He threw it over the ceiling opening, weighting it down with sandbags he pulled from a nearby pile of construction debris left by the previous owners.

The seal was imperfect, but it was airtight enough, and he looked up. The Construct was still on the bone-floor, staring at the blank paper. The mites were still feeding, but the light was dimming. The blue glow of the books was fading.

Without the external dimensional energy, the magical physics of the library were collapsing. The books were becoming just paper. The mites were becoming just insects.

And insects suffocate.

Dale checked his watch. Ten minutes. That was the oxygen limit for a sealed room of that size, assuming no ventilation.

He had ten minutes to document the verification, and he sat down on the concrete floor. He pulled out a fresh report form.

*SUBJECT: ARCHIVAL MITE INFESTATION.* *METHOD: ATMOSPHERIC DEPRIVATION VIA STRUCTURAL ISOLATION.* *STATUS: IN PROGRESS.*

He wrote for five minutes. He described the behavior of the Construct. He noted the distraction tactic. He calculated the volume of the library and the estimated population density of the mites.

At minute seven, the Construct stood up. It looked at the ceiling. It sensed the change in pressure. The air was getting thin.

It looked at Dale.

It didn't attack. It walked to the edge of the bone-floor. It looked down at the basement.

Then it jumped.

It landed on the concrete with a heavy thud. The Construct was in the basement now.

Dale didn't flinch. He kept writing.

The Construct approached him. It was tall, eight feet high. Its joints cracked. It raised a hand.

"You have sealed the tomb," the Construct said. Its voice was weaker now. Thinner.

"I have sealed the workspace," Dale corrected, not looking up. "And you are in violation of code 4.2: Unauthorized Entry into a Restricted Zone."

The Construct hesitated. Its logic was breaking down. It was an entity of order, bound by the rules of the library. Dale was applying the rules of the library to a pest control job.

"If I kill you," the Construct said, "the infestation continues."

"If you don't kill me," Dale said, pointing his pen at the ceiling, "the infestation dies. And you starve."

The Construct looked at the ceiling. The tarp was shifting. The air was running out.

Dale finished his report. He signed it.

He stood up. He picked up his clipboard.

He looked the Construct in the blank white eyes.

"Verification requires the elimination of the primary vector," Dale said.

"The mites are the vector. You are the host. If the host dies, the vector is orphaned.

If the vector starves, the host is safe.

"

He paused.

"But I don't care about the host. I care about the infestation."

He raised his hand. He didn't have a weapon. He had a flashlight.

He clicked it off.

Total darkness.

The Construct's eyes flared brighter in the dark, trying to find the source of the blank paper, the source of the new information. It scrambled, its long limbs flailing.

Dale clicked the flashlight on, aiming it directly at the Construct's face.

The Construct shrieked. A sound of tearing paper.

It lunged.

Dale sidestepped. He didn't fight. He just moved. He moved to the other side of the basement, putting a support pillar between him and the creature.

The Construct crashed into the pillar, which was clumsy. Blind. The lack of air was affecting its motor functions.

Dale watched it struggle. He waited.

At minute nine, the Construct stopped moving. It collapsed, its body folding in on itself.

The mites on the library above fell. Not flying. Falling. Dead insects raining down through the small gaps in the tarp.

Dale walked to the center of the room. He looked up at the tarp.

He checked his Field Guide.

*ENTRY: ARCHIVAL MITE.* *STATUS: ELIMINATED.* *VERIFICATION: COMPLETE.*

*POINTS AWARDED: 150.* *BONUS: 50 FOR CREATIVE USE OF STRUCTURAL ISOLATION.* *THOROUGHNESS RATING: 9.8.*

Dale let out a breath. He capped his pen.

Then the tarp bulged.

Not down. Up.

Something was pushing against the bottom of the tarp. Something heavy. Something that wasn't a mite.

The Construct was dead on the floor. The mites were dead on the ceiling.

But the library wasn't empty.

The tarp tore.

A hand, larger than the Construct's, burst through. It wasn't pale. It was black, slick with ink.

And it wasn't alone.

The System notification flashed in Dale's vision, red and violent.

*CRITICAL ERROR: PRIMARY INFESTATION IS A SYMPTOM.* *SECONDARY INFESTATION DETECTED: ARCHIVAL PARASITE.* *CLASS: IX.*

Dale looked at the hand. It held a book. A book bound in human skin.

The book opened. And the pages were screaming.
