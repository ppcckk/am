Chapter 398: Administrative Error

# Chapter 398

The stamp did not land. It hovered.

A Senior Auditor from the Licensing Authority did not crush infrastructure unless the paperwork required it. The air above the gas station shimmered with a low, vibrating hum that rattled teeth and turned the spilled gasoline into a fine, gray mist. The pressure was not physical; it was administrative. It pressed against the lungs with the weight of a thousand denied claims.

Vance huddled behind the twisted wreckage of a fuel pump, his hands shaking so violently he could barely hold his clipboard. He wasn't looking at the stamp. He was looking at Dale.

To Vance, the man standing in the center of the ruined lot looked less like a survivor and more like a error in the code. Dale stood perfectly still, his posture rigid, his eyes fixed on the descending geometry of the Auditor. The stone statue in the bag at his feet had stopped vibrating. The silence was worse than the noise.

Vance had seen Dale fight things before. He had seen Dale kill things that should not exist. But he had never seen Dale wait.

The Senior Auditor's presence was a blank space in the world, a void shaped like authority. It expected Dale to run, or to beg, or to fight. It did not expect him to pull out a pen.

Vance watched the bag of stone. It was supposed to be empty.

Vance had seen it empty.

But the mass of it pulled Dale's shoulder down, a gravity well of guilt and consequence. The Auditor's light swept over them, scanning for unauthorized signatures, for forged certifications, for the messy handwriting of a man who had broken the rules to save the county.

Dale's hand moved. Not to attack. To write.

The pen scratched against the pocket-sized notepad. The sound was tiny, insignificant against the roar of the descending bureaucracy, but Vance heard it. It was the sound of a man trying to document his own execution.

Vance looked up at the stamp, which was a monolith of obsidian glass, inscribed with symbols that hurt the eyes to read. It was judging them.

Not for killing monsters.

For failing to follow protocol.

A notification pinged in Vance's peripheral vision.

**SYSTEM ALERT:** *Subject: Dale Kowalski (Class II, Provisional).* *Violation: Unauthorized Alteration of Local Reality via Administrative Containment.* *Penalty Assessment: Pending.*

Vance looked back at Dale. The man's face was pale, drained of blood, but his hand was steady. The stiffness in his fingers was visible, the joints locked in a grip that looked painful, but he didn't let go.

Dale was not fighting the Auditor. He was filing a counter-claim.

Vance realized then that Dale wasn't trying to escape. He was trying to prove that the infestation was real.

The ground shook. Not from the stamp. From below.

Dale's phone buzzed. Once.

Then again.

A pattern.

Vance's phone buzzed in sync. A code. *Safe House.*

Vance looked at Dale, desperate for a command. Dale didn't look at him. He looked at the stone bag.

He looked at the pen. He looked at the stamp.

And then, Dale spoke.

"Protocol 4-B: Emergency Preservation of Evidence."

The Auditor paused. The hum dropped an octave.

Dale didn't raise his voice. He projected it, the way he did when addressing a homeowner who refused to seal their crawl space. Dry. Factual.

"Exhibit A: The breach source. Exhibit B: The containment failure. Exhibit C: My presence."

The stamp shifted. The translucent structure rotated slightly, aligning with Dale's position. The light intensified, blinding white.

Vance squeezed his eyes shut. When he opened them, the stamp was gone.

The gas station was empty. The fuel pumps were gone. The asphalt was smooth, unbroken, pristine. The stone bag was gone.

Vance was alone, and he stood in the middle of the lot, the smell of ozone and old gasoline thick in the air. His clipboard was in his hand. He looked down. The page was blank.

His phone buzzed. A new message from an unknown number.

*Location: Substation 4. Report for processing.*

Vance stared at the message, and he looked at the empty space where Dale had been. The air felt thin, cold. He checked his Field Guide.

The entry for *Rattus profundus* was grayed out.

Contaminated.

He walked north. His legs felt heavy. The pen in his pocket was warm.

***

Dale woke up in a chair, and the chair was plastic, molded, and uncomfortable. The air smelled of stale coffee and ozone. He blinked, his eyes adjusting to the harsh fluorescent lighting.

Substation 4. The holding area for pending appeals.

His wrist throbbed.

The stiffness was back, sharper than before. He flexed his fingers. They moved, but slowly, like rusted hinges.

The pen was still in his pocket. He touched it.

It was cold, and he wasn't alone in the room.

Across from him sat a figure. Not human. A Class IX Auditor, the kind that didn't have a face, only a mask of polished chrome. It sat with perfect posture, its hands folded on a desk that hovered three inches above the floor.

Dale sat up.

His spine cracked. He didn't flinch.

He checked his Field Guide. The entry for the Auditor was blank.

"You filed a motion," the Auditor said. Its voice did not come from a mouth. It vibrated from the walls.

"I filed a complaint," Dale corrected. His voice was raspy. He cleared his throat. "Against the local jurisdiction for failure to maintain dimensional integrity."

The chrome mask tilted.

"You used an administrative loophole to contain a Class V entity. You did not have authorization."

"I had a Field Guide," Dale said. "And I had the Thoroughness Scale."

The Auditor leaned forward. The air pressure in the room dropped.

"Your Thoroughness Rating is currently 4.2."

Dale froze.

4.2, which had been 9.2. Then 8.1. Then 5.5.

The penalty for the unauthorized containment had been severe. It wasn't just a fine. It was a demotion.

"You are no longer Class II," the Auditor said. "You are Class I.

Provisional.

You are to report to the intake center in three days for re-certification. Until then, you are barred from engaging any entity above Class II.

"

Dale looked at his hands. The stiffness was in his knuckles now. He could feel the veins pulsing against his skin, tight and hot.

"The infestation isn't gone," Dale said. "The Rift is widening. You can't stop it by auditing me."

"We do not stop it by auditing you," the Auditor said. "We stop it by removing the variables that cause instability. You are a variable, Dale Kowalski. You are sloppy."

The word hit Dale harder than a punch. *Sloppy.*

In fourteen years, he had never been sloppy. He had been thorough. He had been precise.

"I identified the weakness," Dale said. "I isolated the nest. I eliminated the threat."

"You contaminated your own certification," the Auditor said.

"You mixed protocols. You used a Class III containment spell on a Class V entity.

The cross-contamination penalty is not a debuff.

It is a corruption.

"

The Auditor reached into the desk and pulled out a file. It slid it across the floating surface.

"Your Field Guide entry for *Rattus profundus* is corrupted. It is no longer valid data. If you attempt to use it, it will misidentify threats. It will lead you into traps."

Dale looked at the file. The pages were blank.

"The Authority does not punish error," the Auditor said. "It punishes arrogance. You thought you could outsmart the system."

"I thought I could fix the problem," Dale said.

"Then fix your paperwork."

The Auditor stood. The chrome mask reflected Dale's face, distorted and small.

"You have forty-eight hours to complete the re-certification exam. If you fail, your license is revoked. You will be classified as a civilian. You will be removed from the active zone."

Dale stood up. His legs felt like water.

"And if I pass?"

"Then you get your license back. You get your points back. And you get to watch the rest of the county die while you fill out forms."

The Auditor dissolved. The room was empty.

Dale walked to the door. His hand shook as he touched the handle.

The pen in his pocket burned, and he pulled it out.

The ink was black, but the tip was white.

He looked at the wall. There was a schedule posted.

*Exam: Practical Identification. Date: Today. 0900 Hours.*

Dale checked his watch. 0855, and he had five minutes.

He walked out into the hallway. The Substation was quiet. Other technicians moved through the corridors, their heads down, their Field Guides glowing faintly at their sides. They avoided eye contact.

Dale found the exam room. It was a white cube, soundproofed. The door opened with a hiss.

Inside, there was a single object on a pedestal, which was a rat.

But it was wrong.

The rat was too big. Its fur was matted with something black and viscous. Its eyes were missing.

In their place were two holes that leaked a gray powder. The creature twitched, but not with life.

With static.

Dale pulled out his Field Guide. The entry for *Rattus profundus* was grayed out. Corrupted.

He couldn't use it.

If he identified it as *Rattus profundus*, he would trigger a cross-contamination penalty. If he identified it as anything else, he would be wrong.

The system required identification. But the data was poisoned.

Dale looked at the rat. The gray powder drifted into the air. It coated the pedestal. It coated Dale's shoes.

He took a breath. He looked at the rat's legs. The joints were swollen. The claws were broken.

This wasn't a monster, which was a victim.

Dale reached into his bag. He didn't have a weapon. He had a sample vial.

He uncapped the vial. He held it under the rat's chin.

The rat screeched. A sound of tearing metal.

Dale didn't move. He waited.

The powder fell. It landed in the vial.

He capped it.

The system notification appeared.

**SYSTEM ALERT:** *Identification: Unknown.* *Status: Pending.* *Bonus: None.*

Dale looked at the vial. The powder inside was swirling. It was forming a shape.

A face.

His face.

The system beeped.

**IDENTIFICATION COMPLETE.** *Species: Kowalski, Dale (Post-Mortem).* *Classification: Class I Civilian.* *Threat Level: Zero.*

Dale stared at the words, and he wasn't the hunter anymore.

He was the infestation.

The door behind him locked. The walls closed in.

Dale looked at the vial. He looked at his hands. The stiffness was in his neck now. He couldn't turn his head.

He had to escape. Not from the room. From himself.

He had to file a complaint against his own existence.

And he had to do it before the stamp came back.

The stamp returned.
