Chapter 100: Nest Inversion Protocol

# Chapter 100

The smell hit him before the sight did. Not ozone, not rot, but the sharp, chemical tang of industrial degreaser mixed with ancient, stagnant humidity. Dale Kowalski stepped out of Miller’s truck and immediately regretted the decision. His boots sank into a slurry of gray sludge that coated the parking lot of the Authority’s Sector 4 Administrative Hub.

This wasn’t the sleek, glass-walled office Dale had imagined. It was a brutalist concrete bunker half-buried in the mud, its windows sealed with heavy iron grates. Soldiers in matte-black armor stood in rigid rows, their weapons lowered but their eyes tracking Dale’s every movement.

Miller stayed by the truck. He didn’t follow.

He couldn’t, and the perimeter line was drawn in chalk, and Miller had no clearance. Dale walked alone.

He adjusted his collar.

He checked his clipboard. He didn’t have a clipboard.

He had a mental interface, but the anxiety was a physical weight in his chest, a static charge that made his fingers twitch.

He forced himself to breathe in a four-four-four-four cycle. Protocol. Procedure.

Structure.

A man in a gray suit stepped out from the main doors. He held no weapon. He held a tablet.

“Technician Kowalski,” the man said. His voice was flat, devoid of inflection. “You are late for your scheduled intake.”

“I didn’t schedule an appointment,” Dale said, and he stopped ten feet from the line.

He looked at the soldiers. He looked at the suit.

“I have a query regarding my parental status.”

The suit tapped his screen. “Query logged. Processing. Please step into the designated containment zone.”

Dale looked at the red circle painted on the concrete, and it was roughly the size of a standard inspection bay.

He knew that space, and he had spent fifteen years working in spaces exactly like that. Confined. Controlled.

Sterile.

He stepped in.

The air inside the circle changed. It grew colder, drier. The humidity vanished. He felt a soft hum vibrate through his boot soles.

“Identification,” the suit said. “Name, certification level, current infestation count.”

“Dale Kowalski. Level IV. Current count: zero active engagements. One pending audit.”

The suit frowned. “Level IV?

The system flags you as a threat multiplier.

Your Thoroughness Rating is 9.2. That is anomalous. Most technicians hover around 6.0.

High thoroughness correlates with high lethality. High lethality correlates with instability.”

“Thoroughness isn’t instability,” Dale said.

His voice was dry, practiced.

It was the voice he used when explaining to a homeowner why they couldn’t just pour bleach down a drain. “It’s compliance.

If you don’t verify the nest, the infestation returns. If I’m unstable, it’s because your paperwork is sloppy.”

The suit ignored the jab. He swiped his screen again.

“We have a report of a dimensional breach in your last operational zone.

A Level 5 Rift. You closed it.”

“I exterminated it.”

“You killed the broodmother.”

“I isolated the vector, documented the morphology, and applied targeted elimination protocols. The killing was a byproduct of the protocol. Not the objective.”

The suit looked up.

His eyes showed interest for the first time. “Objective is extermination.

Protocol is the method.

You confuse the two.”

“No,” Dale said. “I clarify them.

If you treat a Class 3 Mite infestation with Class 9 Plasma Rounds, you get cross-contamination.

You damage the structure.

You create a secondary infestation. You fail the inspection.”

Dale took a step forward. The soldiers raised their weapons.

“Stay in the circle,” the suit warned.

Dale stopped. He looked at the suit’s tablet. He looked at the soldiers’ rifles. He looked at the vents in the walls of the bunker.

“You have a ventilation issue,” Dale said.

The suit blinked. “Excuse me?”

“The intake vents. Above the door. I can see particulate matter accumulating on the filters.

It’s gray dust. Likely chitinous debris.

You’re pulling in contaminants from the exterior.

Your air quality is compromised. If you have any sensitive documentation in there, it’s being corroded.”

The suit hesitated. He looked at the vents. He didn’t know what Dale was talking about. But he looked.

Dale pressed his advantage.

“I can show you the frass trail. It leads from the east side of the building.

There’s a breach in the foundation.

Class 2 burrowers. They’ve been nesting in the sub-basement. That’s why the air smells like old copper.

That’s why your security cameras have been glitching. Not interference.

Excrement blocking the sensors.”

The suit lowered his tablet. “You’re guessing.”

“I’m observing.” Dale pointed to the ground near the main door.

“Look at the scuff marks on the concrete. Deep gouges.

Claws. Not boots. And there’s a dark stain near the drainage grate.

Not blood. It’s too viscous.

It’s regurgitated soil.

They’re building tunnels.”

The suit stared at him. “If you are right, this is a code violation.”

“If I am right, you have an infestation in your headquarters. And you have an unlicensed technician standing in your containment zone, offering free diagnostics.”

The suit turned to the nearest soldier. “Scan the sub-basement. Sector 4, Grid Alpha.”

The soldier hesitated. “Sir, we don’t have clearance for—”

“Do it.”

The soldier walked away. The suit watched Dale. “You’re trying to get me to open the door.”

“I’m trying to get you to fix your building,” Dale said.

“I want to see my parents. The system says they’re incarcerated.

If they’re here, they’re in the cells.

But if there are burrowers in the walls, maybe they’re not in cells. Maybe they’re in the walls.”

The suit paled. “That’s absurd.”

“Is it?” Dale asked. “Look at the entry log.

When did the last pest control sweep happen?

I’ve been looking at the timestamps on the public notices.

None in three years. You’re a bureaucratic hub.

You attract vermin. You ignore them because you think you’re above them. But you’re not.

You’re just slow.”

The soldier returned, running. His face was white. He skidded to a halt in front of the suit.

“Sir,” he gasped. “There’s… something in the east sub-basement. It’s chewing through the support beams.”

The suit looked at Dale. “How did you know?”

“I didn’t guess,” Dale said.

“I looked. You don’t look. You process.

There’s a difference.”

The suit turned to his tablet. He tapped furiously. “Authorize Level 2 containment protocols. Dispatch extermination unit.”

“No,” Dale said.

The suit looked up. “No?”

“Level 2 protocols are broad-spectrum aerosols. In a confined sub-basement with structural instability, aerosols will coat the surfaces, but they won’t penetrate the deep nesting sites.

You’ll kill the adults, but the larvae will survive.

They’ll go dormant. And in six months, your building collapses.”

Dale stepped to the edge of the red circle. The soldiers tensed, but the suit raised a hand.

“I can fix it,” Dale said. “But I need to be outside the circle.”

The suit stared at him. “You’re asking for permission to enter a hazardous zone without a hazard suit.”

“I’m asking for permission to do my job. Properly.”

The suit was silent for a long time, and the wind whipped around the bunker, carrying the smell of wet earth and something else. Something organic. Something alive.

Finally, the suit nodded. “You have ten minutes. If you fail, you are detained indefinitely.”

“I don’t need ten minutes. I need a shovel and a flashlight.”

The suit signaled to a soldier. “Give him the gear.”

Dale walked out of the circle. The moment his foot crossed the chalk line, the System interface flared in his peripheral vision.

**STATUS UPDATE: ACTIVE ENGAGEMENT DETECTED.** **OBJECTIVE: RESTORE STRUCTURAL INTEGRITY OF AUTHORITY FACILITY.** **CURRENT THREAT: SUBTERRANEAN BURROWERS (SPECIES UNKNOWN).** **PENALTY FOR FAILURE: CERTIFICATION REVOCATION.**

Dale took the shovel. It was heavy.

He took the flashlight, and he clicked it on.

The beam cut through the gloom of the open door.

He didn’t run. He walked. He checked his footing.

He scanned the walls. He was looking for the signs.

The entry points.

The trails.

Inside, the air was thick, and the hallway was dark. The lights flickered.

Dale walked toward the east stairwell.

He could hear it now. A faint, rhythmic scraping. Like sandpaper on stone.

He descended the stairs. The concrete steps were slick with moisture.

He moved slowly, and he didn’t rush.

Rushing led to mistakes. Mistakes led to death.

At the bottom of the stairs, the hallway was empty. But the walls were wrong. They bulged. The paint was peeling in long, ragged strips, revealing not brick, but a pulsating, brown material that looked like hardened mud.

Dale knelt. He touched the wall. It was warm. He pulled his hand back.

He opened his Field Guide. He scrolled through the entries. *Rattus profundus*. *Acid Beetles*. *Chitin Crawlers*. None of them matched the texture. The pattern was wrong.

He activated his scanner. **SCAN: NEARBY BIOLOGICAL MATTER.**

The interface loaded a progress bar. It stalled. Error. **SCAN FAILED. LOCAL INTERFERENCE DETECTED.**

Dale frowned. He tried again. **SCAN: STRUCTURAL INTEGRITY.**

This worked. The result appeared in green text.

**INTEGRITY: 40%. COMPROMISED BY EXTERNAL BIOLOGICAL MASS.**

Not internal. External.

Dale stood up. He looked at the ceiling. The vents were there.

He looked at the floor. The drains were there.

He wasn’t looking at the right place, and he walked down the hall. The scraping sound was louder here. It came from behind a heavy steel door marked **ARCHIVE ROOM 4**.

Dale pushed the door. It was locked.

He kicked it.

Once. Twice. The metal buckled.

He kicked it again.

The lock broke.

The door swung open.

Inside, the room was filled with boxes.

Thousands of them. Stacks of paper. Files.

Records.

And crawling over them, covering the floor, climbing the shelves, were things that looked like centipedes made of wet clay. They were long, segmented, and faceless. They had no eyes. They had no mouths.

But they had tools.

In their front limbs, they held styluses. They were writing.

Dale froze, and they weren’t eating the paper. They were *annotating* it.

One of the creatures turned. It raised a stylus. It pointed at Dale.

A notification popped up in his mind.

**NEW SPECIES DETECTED: ARCHIVIST WORMS.** **BEHAVIOR: DOCUMENTATION OVER EROSION.** **WEAKNESS: UNKNOWN.**

Dale stared at them. They were pests. But they were pests that valued records.

He looked at the files. He looked at the worms. He looked at his clipboard.

He didn’t have a clipboard. But he had a pen. And he had a report form in his mind.

He took a step back. He didn’t attack. He didn’t spray.

He pulled a blank report form from his mental interface. He started writing.

*Inspection Report: Sector 4 Archive.* *Observed Behavior: Active curation of records.* *Threat Assessment: Low. High potential for symbiotic utility.*

The worms stopped moving. They watched him.

Dale looked at the suit, who had followed him down the stairs. The suit was horrified. “Destroy them!”

“No,” Dale said. “They’re organizing your backlog.”

The suit looked at the walls, and the worms had created intricate diagrams on the paper.

Maps. Networks.

Connections between files that had been lost for years.

“Are you insane?” the suit whispered.

“Thoroughness,” Dale said. “They’re doing the work you never did.”

The lead worm approached Dale. It held up a file. It dropped it at his feet.

Dale picked it up. It was a personnel file.

His father’s, and he opened it. The text was blurry, but readable.

*SUBJECT: KOWALSKI, J. STATUS: DETAINED FOR UNAUTHORIZED USE OF CLASS 9 PROTOCOLS. CHARGE: CROSS-CONTAMINATION OF DIMENSIONAL BARRIERS.*

Dale read the first sentence. Then the second.

Then he looked at the signature at the bottom.

It wasn’t his father’s, which was his mother’s.

And she had signed it *three days ago*.

Dale looked up at the suit. “My parents aren’t in cells,” he said.

His voice was cold.

“They’re in the walls. They’re *with* these things.”

The suit stepped back. “That’s impossible.”

Dale looked at the worms. They were waiting.

**SYSTEM ALERT: NEW PROTOCOL UNLOCKED: SYMBIOTIC NEGOTIATION.** **COST: 500 TREATMENT POINTS.** **RISK: HIGH.**

Dale had 1,200 points, and he could pay the cost. He could negotiate.

Or he could burn the room.

He looked at the file. He looked at the worm.

The worm tilted its head. It raised another stylus.

It wrote one word on the air, in glowing blue text that hung in the darkness.

*HELP.*

Dale felt a chill that had nothing to do with the damp air.

He looked at the suit. The suit was already reaching for his comm unit.

He was calling for backup, and he was calling for the flamethrowers.

Dale had five seconds.

He didn’t reach for his shovel.

He reached for his pen.

He signed the report.

**STATUS: ACCEPTED.**

The room went silent.

And then, from deep within the walls, a voice spoke. It wasn’t a worm. It wasn’t a soldier.

It was his mother.

“Dale,” she said. “Don’t let them burn us. We have the key.”

The suit’s comm unit crackled. “Flamethrower team, deploy.”

Dale looked at the door. The soldiers were coming.

He looked at the file. He looked at the worms.

He made his choice, and he slammed his hand onto the floor; activated the only protocol he hadn’t used yet. A protocol that required total contamination. A protocol that broke the rules.

**PROTOCOL: NEST INVERSION.**

The floor cracked open.
