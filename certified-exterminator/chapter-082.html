Chapter 82: Sealed Weakness

# Chapter 82

The ETA on Dale's phone read three minutes and forty seconds. He sat in the cab of his truck with the engine off, watching the number tick down, and stared at the Field Guide entry on his tablet.

Entry 45. His face.

His certification number.

His handwriting. The weakness field filled in like a code violation he'd written a hundred times before. CONTAMINATION ANXIETY.

TRIGGER: UNIDENTIFIED ORGANISMS.

RESPONSE: PARALYSIS. VULNERABILITY WINDOW: 3.2 SECONDS AFTER INITIAL CONTACT WITH UNKNOWN SPECIES.

He tried to delete it.

The tablet rejected the input. FIELD GUIDE ENTRIES ARE READ-ONLY AFTER AUTHORIZATION. He tried to edit the weakness field.

Same rejection. The entry was locked.

Sealed.

Official.

Two minutes fifty.

Dale ran his thumb over the entry header. AUTHORIZED BY: TECHNICIAN #7741-C.

But it was his handwriting. His loop on the capital D. His habit of writing vulnerability with the y dropping below the baseline.

He had not written this. But it was his.

The mimic.

The thing on his driveway that had looked like him and said nothing, and it had not just replicated his face. It had replicated his access.

His tablet.

His Field Guide. His authorization.

He pulled up the Field Guide index.

Forty-five entries. He'd built them over two years of fieldwork. Rattus profundus.

Acid beetles. The broodmother from his first job.

The Class-IV rift spawn from the industrial district.

Each one documented, identified, treated, verified.

His life's work in a digital folder.

And now entry forty-five was a weapon. Someone had used his own documentation system to build a kill order.

One minute forty-five.

Dale looked at the protocol the technician had selected. CLASS-IV TARGETED NEURAL DISRUPTION. Designed for organisms with a documented paralysis response.

The protocol would present an unidentified stimulus, wait for the 3.2-second freeze, and then deliver the kill payload. It was elegant.

It was exactly right, which was also, Dale realized, only as good as the data behind it.

He looked at the cross-contamination rule. He'd read it a hundred times. Deploying a protocol designed for one creature type against another inflicts a 40% debuff and contaminates your Field Guide entry for 24 hours.

The rule existed to prevent sloppy work.

To prevent technicians from grabbing whatever was in their toolkit and spraying it at things that didn't match.

The rule contaminated the entry. Not the technician. Not the protocol. The entry.

Dale looked at Entry 45 again, and he looked at the authorization line. AUTHORIZED BY: TECHNICIAN #7741-C. Not by him.

By the mimic. The mimic had used his access to write the entry, but the authorization was logged under a different certification number.

That meant the entry was not properly authorized. It was a contaminated document. An entry written by an unauthorized entity using a technician's credentials.

Which meant it was, by the system's own definitions, a foreign organism in the Field Guide.

Dale opened his protocol toolkit. He scrolled past the Class-IV neural disruption protocols. Past the containment sprays and the identification tags, and he found what he was looking for at the bottom of the list.

DOCUMENTATION PROTOCOL, CLASS I.

Designed for processing contaminated or misfiled entries.

The most basic tool in his kit. The one he used when a junior tech filed a report under the wrong species code.

The one he'd used on day one, when a rookie had tagged a brown-banded cockroach as a German cockroach and nearly poisoned a family of four with the wrong bait gel.

He selected it. Targeted it at Entry 45.

The tablet hesitated. TARGET NOT A VALID ORGANISM DESIGNATION. DOCUMENTATION PROTOCOLS REQUIRE A BIOLOGICAL TARGET.

Dale stared at the rejection. Then he went back to the entry header.

SPECIES DESIGNATION: PEST.

The mimic had filed him under a species code. Entry 45 was not a document. It was a species entry.

It was, by the system's logic, a pest.

He reselected the protocol. Targeted it at Entry 45. This time the tablet accepted.

DOCUMENTATION PROTOCOL, CLASS I, DEPLOYED. TARGET: FIELD GUIDE ENTRY 45. SPECIES DESIGNATION: PEST.

The system processed it. A progress bar appeared. Then a notification.

CROSS-CONTAMINATION DETECTED. ENTRY 45 CONTAMINATED FOR 24 HOURS. PROTOCOL EFFECTIVENESS REDUCED BY 40% FOR ALL DEPLOYMENTS REFERENCING THIS ENTRY.

Dale exhaled. The technician's protocol was still coming.

It was still targeted at him.

But now it was 40% less effective; the paralysis window might still open, but the kill payload would be weakened. He might survive the hit.

He might.

The ETA on his phone read forty seconds.

Then a second notification appeared.

AUTHORITY ALERT: FIELD GUIDE INTEGRITY BREACH, CLASS A. UNAUTHORIZED DEPLOYMENT OF DOCUMENTATION PROTOCOL AGAINST SELF-REFERENTIAL ENTRY. CERTIFICATION LEVEL VII TEMPORARILY SUSPENDED PENDING REVIEW.

Dale read it twice. His toolkit grayed out on the tablet.

Every protocol.

Every spray.

Every containment tag. Locked.

He'd just stripped his own certification to survive the next four minutes.

The truck's headlights caught a figure walking up the driveway. Technician 7741-C. Not the mimic.

The real one. A stocky man in a standard Authority vest, carrying a neural disruption emitter on a shoulder sling.

He walked like a man who'd done this a hundred times.

Because he had.

The technician stopped at the driver's side window. He looked at Dale. He looked at the tablet on the passenger seat. He looked back at Dale.

"You're the one who filed the addendum," the technician said. Not a question.

"I am."

"The addendum was accurate."

"It was."

The technician tilted his head. "Then why is your entry contaminated?"

Dale looked at him. "Because I contaminated it."

The technician stared.

His hand was on the emitter. Not raised.

Not yet.

But on.

"That's a Class A violation," the technician said.

"I know."

"You know that means you can't deploy any protocols. You know that means I can treat you as an unlicensed organism if you resist."

"I know."

The technician's hand tightened on the emitter. "Then you know I'm going to deploy the protocol."

"You know it's 40% less effective now."

The technician was quiet for a moment. Then he looked at the tablet. He looked at Entry 45. He looked at the contamination tag.

"You contaminated your own weakness," he said slowly. "You made the data unreliable."

"I made the data unreliable."

"So the protocol doesn't work."

"The protocol still works. It's just weaker. I'll still freeze. But the payload won't kill me."

The technician looked at him for a long time. Then he looked at his own tablet.

He pulled up the protocol.

CLASS-IV TARGETED NEURAL DISRUPTION.

He looked at the effectiveness rating. It had dropped.

He could see it.

"Sixty percent," he said. "That's not enough for a clean kill."

"No."

"But it's enough to hurt."

"Yes."

The technician set the emitter down on the hood of the truck. He took off his Authority vest. He folded it. He put it on the passenger seat next to Dale's tablet.

"I'm not going to deploy it," he said.

Dale watched him. "Why?"

"Because I'm a technician. I don't work with bad data."

The technician walked to the back of the truck. He opened the bed.

He pulled out a clipboard.

A real clipboard. Paper. Pencil.

He wrote something. He tore the page off. He handed it to Dale through the window.

It was a violation report.

K-7. Structural entry point.

The same code Dale had written fourteen years ago on a duplex in suburban Ohio.

The same code he'd written the day the Rift opened.

The technician pointed at the paper. "Fix your entry," he said.

"Properly.

Then call me back.

I'll re-run the protocol against accurate data."

He walked back to his own vehicle. He drove away.

Dale sat in the truck. He looked at the violation report. He looked at the contaminated Entry 45. He looked at his grayed-out toolkit.

He had twenty-four hours. His certification was suspended. His toolkit was locked.

His entry was contaminated. And the mimic that had written Entry 45 was still out there, still using his access, still building the technician's arsenal one entry at a time.

He opened the Field Guide index. He scrolled to the bottom.

Entry 45. His. Contaminated.

He kept scrolling.

Entry 46.

Name: Margaret Chen. Weakness: claustrophobia. Trigger: enclosed spaces.

Response: panic attack.

Vulnerability window: 4.1 seconds.

Entry 47.

Name: Victor Osei. Weakness: trust. Trigger: authority figures.

Response: compliance. Vulnerability window: continuous.

Entry 48.

Name: Rachel Kowalski.

Weakness: guilt. Trigger: family.

Response: paralysis. Vulnerability window: 2.8 seconds.

Rachel. His daughter.

Twenty-two years old.

A junior tech at the county Authority office. She'd been filing reports under his certification number for three months because her own Level I license hadn't come through yet. She'd been using his access.

Entry 49.

Name: Dale Kowalski. Weakness: protocol adherence.

Trigger: authority directives.

Response: compliance. Vulnerability window: continuous.

The entries were still being written. He could see the text forming.

Fresh ink.

His handwriting.

His loop on the D. His dropping y.

The mimic was still working. Still building. And Entry 49 was describing a weakness Dale did not know he had.

He thought about the technician. He set the emitter down. The way he'd said, I don't work with bad data.

Dale had followed the protocol. He'd contaminated the entry.

He'd followed the rules.

He'd done exactly what the system told him to do.

And Entry 49 said that was the weakness, and he looked at the tablet.

The Field Guide index was at Entry 50. The text was forming.

He could see the first letters of the name.

A capital R. The top of an A.

He closed the tablet. He started the truck. He drove to Rachel's house.
