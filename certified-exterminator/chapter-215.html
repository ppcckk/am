Chapter 215: Wrench to Floor

# Chapter 215

The wrench hung in the air, suspended by a trembling hand and the weight of four hundred pounds of chitinous horror.

Dale did not swing.

The spider's legs curled inward, wrapping around its own thorax. A protective posture.

Or a surrender.

The golden barrier pulsed, casting shifting shadows across Dale's sweat-stained coveralls. Foam continued to harden around the broodmother's gagged maw, turning into a calcified lump of expanding polyurethane that smelled of chemicals and ozone.

*Time remaining: 58 seconds.*

The notification hovered in Dale's peripheral vision, red text burning against the dim basement light. It pulsed in sync with his heartbeat, a relentless metronome counting down his career.

He lowered the wrench.

Not because he spared the spider.

Because the spider was not the target.

The target was the floor.

Dale stepped over the writhing broodmother. The foam mass shifted in its mouth, the monster's eyes rolling back in panic as it struggled to breathe through its nose. Dale ignored it. He walked to the center of the room, where the spider lay tangled in its own silk and Dale's earlier containment tape.

"Stay still," Dale said. His voice was flat. Procedural.

*You intend to kill me?* the spider transmitted. The thought arrived directly in Dale's mind, cold and wet.

"No," Dale replied.

He opened his Field Guide. The interface flickered in the air before him, translucent and glowing with Authority blue.

He scrolled past the *Rattus profundus* entry, past the beetle data, and landed on *Arachnida architectus*.

The spider.

He tapped the entry.

*Weakness Identified: Structural Dependency.*

Dale nodded. He pulled a marker from his pocket.

Red ink.

Sharp tip.

He drew a circle on the spider's abdomen.

Then he drew a second circle.

The spider shrieked. A psychic ripple that made Dale's teeth ache.

*You are violating consent protocols!*

"I am violating your structural integrity," Dale corrected. "And I am doing it for the benefit of the building."

He looked at the floor.

The basement was unfinished. Concrete slab. Cracked.

The cracks radiated from beneath the spider. The monster's legs were anchored into the concrete.

Not just resting on it.

Embedded. The chitin had fused with the foundation. The spider was not an infestation in the house.

It was a load-bearing column of the house.

Kill the spider, and the house collapses.

Kill the broodmother, and the spider dies of shock.

Save the spider, and the broodmother escapes.

The Authority's exam was a logic trap. A test of interpretation, not combat.

Dale uncapped his second spray can. This one was different.

Industrial-grade solvent.

Designed for removing hardened adhesive from subflooring. Class I chemical. Safe for humans in ventilated spaces.

Deadly for organic adhesives.

He sprayed the spider's legs.

The solvent hissed. The connection between chitin and concrete dissolved.

*Stop! You will kill me!*

"I am separating the structural component from the organism," Dale said. He checked his watch. Forty seconds.

He sprayed faster. The solvent ate away at the biological bond. The spider's legs twitched, pulling away from the concrete. The monster screamed in psychic agony, a sound like metal tearing.

The broodmother thrashed against the barrier. The foam in its mouth cracked. A trickle of black blood ran down its chin.

Dale moved to the next leg.

Twenty seconds.

The spider was half-detached.

Its body sagged, pulling on the remaining anchors.

The floor above groaned. Dust fell from the ceiling.

A crack widened in the wall.

*If you sever me completely, the structure fails,* the spider whispered. *The house falls. The broodmother escapes into the neighborhood.*

Dale paused. He looked at the ceiling. Then at the spider.

A loophole.

The Authority's rule stated: *Eliminate the broodmother without killing the structural component.*

It did not say the structural component had to be the spider.

It did not say the structural component had to remain in one piece.

It only required that the component survive the elimination event.

Dale pulled out his clipboard.

He wrote a new code.

*Structural Modification: Temporary Load Transfer.*

He tapped the Field Guide. He selected the *Arachnida architectus* entry and dragged it into the *Tools* section.

This was not permitted. The Field Guide was for identification, not equipment.

The Authority's notification flickered. *Warning: Field Guide misuse. Cross-contamination risk.*

Dale ignored the warning. He targeted the spider's silk.

The monster spun webs to reinforce its body. The silk was stronger than steel cable. It was part of the spider. Part of the structure.

Dale sprayed solvent on the spider's abdomen. The creature convulsed, spraying fresh webbing in a panic.

Dale caught the strand.

He wrapped it around the joist above.

Then he wrapped it around the spider's remaining legs.

Then he wrapped it around the broodmother's neck.

The spider was now a pulley.

The broodmother was the counterweight.

The joist was the anchor.

Dale stepped back.

"Pull," Dale ordered.

The spider, trapped in its own silk, jerked. The tension snapped.

The broodmother, gagged and choking, lurched forward.

The silk tightened. The spider's legs, still partially fused to the concrete, acted as a lever.

The broodmother slammed into the spider.

The impact shattered the spider's exoskeleton.

The spider died.

But the silk held.

The broodmother's neck snapped.

The spider's body broke, but its silk remained intact, anchoring the dead monster's weight to the joist. The concrete floor, freed from the living spider's pull, settled back into equilibrium.

The broodmother died.

The structural component—the silk and the fused concrete fragments—survived.

*Time remaining: 2 seconds.*

*Objective Complete.*

*Thoroughness Rating: 8.4.*

*Penalty: Minor structural damage to foundation. Repair required.*

*Certification Level II: Passed.*

*New Protocol Unlocked: Structural Bypass (Class III).*

The golden barrier dissolved.

The room went silent.

Dale stood in the center of the debris. Dust coated his face. The broodmother's body lay twisted in the corner, dead. The spider's broken husk hung from the joist, a grotesque chandelier of chitin and silk.

He wiped his hands on his coveralls.

Then he heard the door open.

Not the basement door. The front door.

Footsteps.

Heavy. Confident.

Not the stumbling gait of a neighbor fleeing panic.

The rhythmic *clack-clack-clack* of polished shoes on tile.

Dale turned.

A man stood at the bottom of the basement stairs.

He wore a suit. Not a hazmat suit.

Not tactical gear.

A charcoal three-piece suit, tailored to perfection.

His tie was a shade of crimson that matched the Authority's notification alerts. His face was pale, angular, and expressionless.

He held a cane. The head of the cane was carved ivory. In the shape of a skull.

"Mr. Kowalski," the man said. His voice was smooth.

Like oil on a blade. "You passed."

Dale did not lower his clipboard. He kept it raised, a shield of paperwork. "Who are you?"

"I am the Reviewer." The man stepped into the room. He did not look at the dead broodmother. He looked at Dale. "I am the one who decides if your certification is valid."

Dale's grip on the clipboard tightened. The Authority did not send human reviewers. The Authority was a system. A bureaucratic entity that communicated via notifications.

This man was not a system.

"You're not in the manual," Dale said.

"The manual," the Reviewer said, "is a suggestion.

A guideline for the unlicensed. For those who fight without understanding." He walked past Dale. He kicked the spider's husk.

It swung gently.

"You fight with understanding. You weaponize the rules.

I admire that."

The Reviewer picked up the wrench Dale had dropped. He examined it. "Crude tool. Effective application."

"I follow protocol," Dale said.

"Protocol is a cage," the Reviewer said. He tossed the wrench aside. It clattered against the concrete.

"You passed the exam, Mr. Kowalski.

But you did not pass the interview."

Dale's stomach tightened. There was no interview. There was only the practical exam.

"What interview?"

"The Authority does not just license technicians.

It licenses *partners*." The Reviewer turned. His eyes were wrong.

Not black. Not white. They were empty.

Hollow sockets where light simply ceased to exist. "You have a high Thoroughness Rating. 9.2.

You see the nest.

You see the vectors. You see the code violations in reality itself."

The Reviewer smiled. It was a thin, cruel expression.

"We have a backlog, Mr.

Kowalski. A global backlog.

And we have a shortage of qualified personnel.

Specifically, personnel who are willing to bend the rules of physics to maintain the rules of bureaucracy."

Dale took a step back. His hand moved to his pocket.

His spray cans were empty.

He had one wrench.

One clipboard.

"I am a pest control technician," Dale said. "I am not a partner."

"You are now," the Reviewer said. He pulled a card from his breast pocket. It was black.

Embossed with gold foil. The Authority's seal.

But the text was different.

*Senior Auditor. Clearance Level: Omega.*

"This is a job offer," the Reviewer said. "Not a certification. A job.

You work for us directly.

No more local permits. No more municipal oversight.

You go where the rifts are.

You clean them. You get paid in *Real Treatment Points*. Points that can buy you anything.

Safety. Immunity.

Power."

Dale looked at the card.

He looked at the dead broodmother.

He looked at the broken spider.

He thought about the code violation he had written at the duplex. K-7. Structural entry point.

He thought about the screaming tenant dragged into the walls.

He thought about the next six weeks. The next six months. The next ten years.

"Real Treatment Points?" Dale asked.

"Unlimited," the Reviewer said.

"And the penalty for failure?"

"Erasure," the Reviewer said.

"But not of your certification. Of your existence.

You become part of the infestation. A permanent entry in the Field Guide of the damned."

Dale stared at him.

The offer was absurd. It was illegal. It was insane.

It was exactly what he had been looking for.

"I need to check the references," Dale said.

The Reviewer laughed. It was a dry, rasping sound.

"There are no references.

Only results. Your first assignment is in Chicago. A Class V infestation.

A hive mind.

It has taken over a hospital. Three hundred patients.

One queen."

Dale's blood ran cold.

Three hundred patients.

One queen.

He could not treat this with spray cans. He could not treat this with duct tape.

He would need the Authority's full support. He would need the Omega clearance.

Or he would need to run.

"I have to submit a report," Dale said.

"Submit it now," the Reviewer said. He pointed at Dale's clipboard.

Dale looked at the blank page.

He uncapped his pen.

He wrote the header.

*Incident Report: Class IV Sub-basement Elimination.*

He wrote the details.

He wrote the outcome.

He wrote the name of the Reviewer.

He signed it.

Then he tore the page off.

He held it out.

The Reviewer took it. He looked at the signature. He looked at Dale.

"You didn't ask for the contract," the Reviewer said.

"I didn't sign it," Dale said.

The Reviewer's smile widened. It reached his empty eyes.

"No," the Reviewer said. "You didn't. You signed a *request for consultation*.

That is a different form. A different liability."

He folded the paper. He placed it in his pocket.

"You are now on probation, Mr.

Kowalski.

If you refuse the Chicago assignment, your certification is revoked. You become unlicensed.

And in a world with Rifts, an unlicensed man is just meat."

The Reviewer turned. He walked up the stairs.

Dale did not follow.

He stood in the silence.

He looked at his clipboard.

The pen was still in his hand.

He wrote one more line.

*Contamination Source: Internal.*

He looked at the door.

The Reviewer was gone.

The front door was closed.

Dale walked to the basement door. He opened it.

The hallway was empty.

But on the kitchen table, where the screaming tenant had been dragged away, sat a suitcase.

Black. Leather. Heavy.

Dale walked to it.

He opened it.

Inside was a new Field Guide. Bound in leather. Pages black. Ink silver.

And a key.

A single brass key.

Tagged: *Chicago. Mercy General. Ward 9.*

Dale closed the suitcase.

He picked it up.

He walked to the front door.

He opened it.

The sun was setting. The neighborhood was quiet. The crack in the street had been filled with quick-drying cement.

Dale stepped outside.

He did not look back at the duplex.

He walked to his van.

He opened the back doors.

He placed the suitcase on the floor.

He sat in the driver's seat.

He started the engine.

He did not go home.

He drove toward the interstate.

Toward Chicago.

Because the Authority had just raised the stakes.

And Dale Kowalski had just lost the right to refuse the job.
