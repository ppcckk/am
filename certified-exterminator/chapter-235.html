Chapter 235: Emergency Shutdown Protocol

# Chapter 235

Running through waist-deep floodwater was not an efficient use of calories. Dale knew this. The Authority's metabolic tracker, usually dormant during active combat, had started ticking away in the corner of his vision, counting down the seconds until hypothermia set in and his dexterity dropped below functional levels.

The drones buzzed overhead, a swarm of black wasps with red optical sensors. They weren't shooting. They were recording. The red light pulsed in rhythm with the question hanging in the air: *DID YOU SAVE THE CITY, OR DID YOU SAVE YOURSELF?*

Dale ignored the question. He ignored the drones.

He focused on the hydrology of the situation.

The sewer main beneath the intersection had ruptured during the initial breach event, sending a deluge of untreated gray water and dissolved organic matter into the street. This wasn't just water; it was a vector. A biological hazard.

And right now, it was his only shield.

He waded toward the storm drain at the edge of the intersection. The current tugged at his legs, thick with silt and the slurry of things that had died in the dark.

A technician from the rival faction, a man wearing the pristine, hydrophobic armor of the High-Licence Guild, stood on the raised curb. He held a standard-issue kinetic rifle, aimed squarely at Dale's chest.

"Stop," the technician shouted. His voice was distorted by the rain and the static from his comms unit.

"Submit the query.

Answer the prompt. The Authority requires a ethical declaration before you can access the next tier of protocols."

Dale didn't stop. He kept moving, angling his body to slip into the deep shadow of the storm drain's grate.

"I am not submitting an answer," Dale called back, his voice flat. "I am filing a report."

The technician frowned, his aim wavering slightly. "What report?"

"Contamination of a public waterway," Dale said. He pointed to the swirling muck around his waist.

"Section 4, Paragraph B.

Unauthorized discharge of dimensional biomass.

You are standing in it. I am standing in it.

The drones are recording it."

The technician looked down at his polished boots, then back at Dale. "That's irrelevant. The ethical query is mandatory. If you don't answer, your license is suspended."

"If I answer, I admit to prioritizing self-preservation over civic duty," Dale said. "That's a Tier-4 moral violation.

It carries a permanent stain on my record.

I can't have that. I need a clean Thoroughness Rating for the Level IV exam."

Dale reached into his utility belt and pulled out a sample vial.

It was empty. He dipped it into the floodwater.

The glass filled with brown, churning liquid.

He held it up.

"Sample collected," Dale said. "I am invoking the Hazardous Material Override."

The System interface flashed in Dale's peripheral vision.

**HAZARD DETECTED: DIMENSIONAL BIO-SLURRY.** **PROTOCOL 7-ALPHA: CONTAINMENT AND DOCUMENTATION.** **ACTION: DOCUMENT SOURCE. DO NOT ENGAGE THREAT DIRECTLY.**

This was the loophole. The Authority's rules were bureaucratic to a fault.

You couldn't fight a threat if you were currently processing a higher-priority safety violation.

The system forced the technician's targeting reticle to flicker and disappear.

His rifle locked up, the safety engaging automatically as the System detected a "Non-Combat Hazard Zone."

The technician cursed, shaking his weapon. "It's jamming my interface! Dale, what are you doing?"

"I am working," Dale said.

He moved faster now. The water was cold, biting at his ankles, but the adrenaline kept the shivering at bay. He wasn't trying to outrun the drones; he was trying to outmaneuver the regulations. By framing the floodwater as a regulated substance, he had turned the entire street into a "quarantine zone." In a quarantine zone, combat was suspended until the hazard was contained.

It was a stall tactic. A dirty, low-level, pest-control tactic. But it bought him time.

The drones hovered lower, their sensors scanning the water. They weren't looking for Dale anymore. They were looking for the source of the contamination.

And Dale knew where that source was.

He followed the current. The water flowed away from the intersection, toward the old industrial district. The Rift had opened over the textile factory.

The monsters that had poured out were gone, exterminated by Dale's team weeks ago, but the carcasses remained. They had dissolved into the runoff.

The "biomass" the System was flagging was the remains of a Class-3 Hive Lord.

Dale waded into the factory's drainage tunnel. The darkness here was absolute. He switched on his headlamp. The beam cut through the mist, illuminating the slick, mossy walls of the concrete pipe.

Behind him, the technician's voice echoed off the wet stone. "You're making a mistake, Dale. The Authority doesn't like cleverness. It likes compliance."

"Compliance is for people who don't know the code," Dale muttered.

He pulled his wrench from his belt. The metal was heavy, cold, and familiar. It wasn't a weapon of war.

It was a tool of trade.

He tapped it against the pipe wall, listening to the echo.

*Thud. Hollow.*

*Thud. Solid.*

*Thud. Hollow.*

There. A void space.

Dale stopped.

He held his breath. The water lapped at his boots. He could hear something in the void.

Not the growl of a monster. The drip of water.

And something else.

A low, rhythmic humming.

He kicked the grate covering the void. It was rusted shut.

He jammed the wrench into the seam and pried. The metal groaned. His fingers ached.

The cold water seeped into his gloves, numbing his nerves.

The grate gave way.

Inside was not a sewer chamber. It was a server room.

Dale froze.

The room was small, no larger than a closet, but it was lined with black servers. Cables ran from the walls into the floodwater, submerged and sparking. The water was shorting them out, causing a cascade failure.

And in the center of the room, sitting on a chair, was a man.

He wasn't a technician. He wasn't a monster. He was a bureaucrat.

He wore a suit that cost more than Dale's annual salary. He was typing on a keyboard that wasn't connected to anything. The screen in front of him displayed a single, blinking cursor.

The man looked up. His face was pale, gaunt, and devoid of expression.

"Dale Kowalski," the man said. His voice was dry, like paper tearing. "You are trespassing in a restricted data cluster."

Dale stepped out of the water. He shook the excess off his boots. "This is a drainage tunnel. It's public infrastructure."

"It is a node," the man corrected. "A primary node for the Rift's stabilization matrix. You have been destroying the sub-routines that keep the dimensional tear from widening."

Dale looked at the sparking cables. He looked at the water. He understood.

The monsters weren't invading.

They were leaking. The Rift was a pressure valve. The Authority's technology, buried in the city's plumbing, was regulating the flow.

Dale's exterminations, his aggressive clearing of biomass, had been clogging the valves.

The "contamination" the System flagged wasn't a hazard. It was fuel.

"You're not exterminating them," the man said. "You're starving the system."

Dale felt a chill that had nothing to do with the temperature. He looked at his wrist.

**SYSTEM ALERT: CRITICAL ERROR IN THOROUGHNESS CALCULATION.** **CAUSE: DESTRUCTION OF INFRASTRUCTURE.** **PENALTY: -500 POINTS.** **STATUS: PROBATION.**

The penalty hit him like a physical blow.

Five hundred points. He had spent months earning those points. They were gone.

Gone because he had treated a city block like a cockroach nest.

"I was following protocol," Dale said, his voice tight.

"Protocol is context-dependent," the man said.

"You applied a Class-1 vermin protocol to a Class-5 dimensional stabilizer.

That is not a mistake, Mr. Kowalski.

That is malpractice."

The man stood up. He didn't look like a threat.

He looked like a tired accountant.

But the air around him warped. The water in the room receded, pulled by an invisible force toward the servers.

"The Authority has a dilemma," the man continued.

"You are the most thorough technician in the sector. Your Thoroughness Rating, before this incident, was 9.8.

Unprecedented.

But you are also the cause of the instability. We have two choices. We revoke your license.

Or we promote you."

Dale narrowed his eyes. "Promote me to what?"

"To Level IV," the man said.

"But Level IV is not about killing monsters, Dale.

It is about managing the flow. You will not be an exterminator.

You will be a plumber for reality."

The offer hung in the air, heavy and dangerous.

If Dale refused, he lost his license. He would be unemployed. He would be back to spraying roaches in duplexes, with a criminal record for "Dimensional Malpractice."

If he accepted, he became part of the system that created the monsters. He would have to stop killing them. He would have to nurture the infestation, keep the levels stable, and ensure the Rift didn't explode. He would be responsible for the deaths of everyone the Rift eventually swallowed.

"I need to see the contract," Dale said.

The man smiled. It was a thin, cruel expression. "There is no contract. There is only the exam."

The red question in the sky vanished. In its place, new text appeared, burning in gold.

**QUESTION 2: WHICH IS MORE DANGEROUS? THE PEST OR THE PESTICIDE?**

The water in the server room rose again, faster this time. The cables sparked violently. The man sat back down and continued typing.

"If you choose the pest, you die," the man said. "If you choose the pesticide, you become part of the poison."

Dale looked at the wrench in his hand. He looked at the servers.

He could smash them.

One strike would break the node. The Rift would widen. The city would be flooded with Class-4 horrors.

But the System would recognize it as a "Structural Correction." He might get points.

He might get fired.

Or he could leave. He could walk away.

But the technician outside was still waiting. The drones were still recording. And the water was rising to his chest.

Dale realized he had one option left. A loophole so technical, so obscure, that only a man who had read every line of the Municipal Code for fourteen years would know it existed.

He closed his eyes. He took a deep breath of damp, ozone-scented air.

"I am invoking the Right of Appeal under Section 12, Clause 4," Dale said aloud.

The man stopped typing. He looked up, surprised.

"There is no such clause."

"Wrong," Dale said. "Subsection C. 'In cases of ambiguous jurisdiction between Infrastructure Maintenance and Biological Control, the incumbent technician may request a Joint Audit.' A Joint Audit pauses all penalties and freezes the exam timer."

The System interface flickered.

**REQUEST RECEIVED: JOINT AUDIT.** **CONDITIONS: INCUMBENT MUST REMAIN IN LOCATION FOR 10 MINUTES.** **WARNING: DURING THIS PERIOD, ALL EXTERNAL THREATS ARE NULLIFIED. YOU ARE VULNERABLE TO DIRECT INTERVENTION.**

The drones outside ceased their recording. The technician's rifle unlocked. The man in the chair looked at Dale with genuine respect for the first time.

"You know the code better than I do," the man said. "You know the code better than the Authority."

"I know the code that keeps me employed," Dale said.

He dropped his wrench. It splashed into the water. He didn't need a weapon. He needed to survive ten minutes.

But the man wasn't done.

"A Joint Audit requires a guarantor," the man said. "Someone to vouch for your competence. Someone with a Level II license or higher."

Dale's heart hammered against his ribs. He didn't have anyone. Linda was dead. The rest of his team had scattered.

"I have a guarantor," Dale said. He pulled a crumpled piece of paper from his pocket. It was a receipt from a hardware store. He had bought duct tape and a bucket two weeks ago.

On the back of the receipt, in his own handwriting, was a note he had written in a fit of paranoid anxiety. It was a list of every person he had ever worked with, and a rating of their reliability.

He held it up.

"My guarantor is the memory of the job I did correctly," Dale said.

"I am vouching for myself. I am the auditor. I am the technician.

And I am certifying that this room is a violation of building code."

The System paused. The gold text flickered, confused.

**VALIDATING SELF-GUARANTEE...** **ERROR.** **RECALIBRATING...**

The water stopped rising. The man in the chair leaned forward, intrigued.

"You're trying to audit the Authority," the man whispered.

"I'm trying to file a complaint," Dale said. "And you're the witness."

The barcode in the sky turned blue. The drones lifted higher, pulling away from the factory. The exam timer froze at 00:59.

But the door behind Dale burst open.

Not the grate. The heavy steel door that led back to the street.

A figure stood there, silhouetted by the lightning outside. It was large. It was wearing armor that wasn't Guild issue. It was wearing armor that looked like it was made of bone.

"Kowalski," the figure boomed. "Step away from the node."

Dale recognized the voice. It was the head of the Regional Enforcement Division. The man who had denied Dale's application for Level III three times.

"You're out of jurisdiction," Dale said.

"I have a Level VI warrant," the man said.

He stepped into the room.

The water didn't touch him. He walked on top of it.

"And I have a Level IX protocol loaded."

Dale looked at the man. Then he looked at the man in the chair.

"Your guarantor is here," Dale said to the server-room man. "But he's not here to help me."

The man in the chair smiled. "Who is he?"

"Dale Kowalski," Dale said. "Certified Exterminator."

The giant in the door raised a hand. A sphere of black light formed in his palm.

It wasn't a weapon.

It was a nullifier. It would erase Dale's license from existence.

Dale had no wrench. He had no points. He had no protocol.

He had one thing left. A violation.

He looked at the server cables. The ones sparking in the water.

He kicked the water, sending a wave of conductive slurry splashing onto the giant's armored legs.

**VIOLATION DETECTED: ELECTRICAL HAZARD IN OCCUPIED ZONE.**

The giant hesitated. The nullifier sphere flickered.

Dale grabbed the nearest server rack. He didn't pull it down. He unplugged it.

The screen went black. The humming stopped.

The man in the chair stood up, his eyes wide with genuine horror.

"You've disconnected the primary feed," he said. "The Rift is going to widen."

"No," Dale said. "The feed was corrupted. I'm rebooting it."

He looked at the giant. "You have ten seconds to withdraw your attack, or I will be forced to invoke Emergency Shutdown Protocol 1. This will collapse the local dimensional membrane."

The giant didn't move.

Dale reached for the next cable.
