Chapter 13: Obstruction and Consent

# Chapter 13

The number was dropping too fast. Gerald watched it tick down in the upper-left corner of his interface: 71.4. 70.8. 69.2. Each decrement corresponded to a specific violation the auditor had identified, and he could see them scrolling in the audit log like a waterfall of citations he hadn't filed.

He was in Corridor 7. The one that connected the fire-rated storage alcoves to the central hub.

It was eleven feet wide.

That should have been enough. The code required six feet of clear egress width for any corridor serving more than four entities simultaneously. He'd designed it at eleven because he'd been thorough.

Because he always designed things with margin.

But the auditor wasn't citing width. It was citing obstruction.

He could see it now. The spawned entities.

His own minions.

They were bunched at the junction where Corridor 7 met the hub, bottlenecked by the load-bearing pillar he'd installed to satisfy the structural code category. The pillar was necessary. Without it, his Compliance Rating would have been lower still.

But it created a pinch point. And the pinch point was trapping his own entities.

Egress obstruction. A violation type he'd never encountered. He'd dealt with fire safety, electrical grounding, structural load, ventilation. He'd never had to think about whether his dungeon could evacuate itself.

68.1, and he tried to move the pillar.

He couldn't.

It was load-bearing. Moving it would collapse the ceiling panel above it, which would trigger a structural violation that was worse than the egress one.

He'd done the math in two seconds. Forty years of building inspection taught him to calculate collapse patterns like a chess player calculating mate in three.

He couldn't fix this physically. Not before the audit completed. The countdown in the corner read four minutes and twelve seconds.

So he needed to fix it procedurally.

Gerald pulled up the variance request form. He'd read about variances in the System documentation during week two, filed them away in the mental cabinet where he kept things that were theoretically useful but practically irrelevant.

A variance allowed a Core to argue that a specific violation should not apply because of material hardship or unnecessary burden.

It required a hearing. Both parties had to be present. Both parties had to argue.

He filed the variance request. The System processed it in three seconds. A hearing was scheduled. The countdown paused at three minutes and forty-one seconds.

The opponent appeared.

Not a person.

A projection. A tall, angular figure made of pale light, with a face like a building permit stamped in negative space.

The auditor.

Gerald hadn't seen one before. The Inspector General's agents didn't typically appear in person. They just audited.

But a hearing required presence.

"Core Hutchins." The auditor's voice sounded like a filing cabinet opening. "You have requested a variance on violation code 7.4.1, egress obstruction, detected in Corridor 7. State your basis."

"The pillar is load-bearing," Gerald said.

"Removing it would create a structural violation.

The obstruction is the lesser violation. I'm requesting a variance on grounds of material hardship.

The alternative remedy is worse than the violation."

The auditor tilted its head. "The System does not recognize comparative hardship as a basis for variance. You must demonstrate that compliance is impossible."

"Then I'll demonstrate that your audit is invalid."

The auditor went still. "Explain."

Gerald pulled up his inspector's interface. Not the Core interface.

The other one, and the one that had been with him since before he became a Core. The one that let him inspect any structure within line of sight. It worked on dungeons now.

It had always worked on dungeons.

He'd just never pointed it at an auditor before.

"You're a structure," Gerald said.

"You have a physical form. You're projecting into my dungeon. That means you have an egress path.

The one you entered through."

The auditor said nothing.

Gerald walked to the corridor entrance.

The one the auditor's projection had materialized from, and he looked at it. He looked at the width.

He looked at the angle of the walls. He looked at the distance from the projection point to the nearest exit, and performed this task as he had in ten thousand buildings before.

Not with drama. With the flat, mechanical efficiency of a man running his eyes along a measurement tape.

"Your projection is entering through a corridor that's four feet wide," Gerald said.

"That's below the six-foot minimum for any structure housing more than one entity. You're an entity. Your projection is an entity.

And there's a second entity here.

Me. So you're in an egress violation."

"That is not applicable to auditors. Auditors are exempt from—"

"Exempt from what?" Gerald interrupted. "Show me the exemption. File it. Cite the code section."

The auditor paused.

A long pause. Gerald could feel the System searching for the exemption the auditor had cited. Searching.

Not finding.

"There is no exemption," Gerald said.

"You didn't check.

You don't check. You just audit.

That's why your own compliance rating is—"

He read the number. He'd been expecting it to be high. Auditors should be exemplary.

It was 62.3.

"Your Compliance Rating is lower than mine," Gerald said. "You're auditing me for egress obstruction while standing in an egress violation yourself. If this hearing is adversarial, I move to disqualify you on grounds of standing. You can't cite a violation you're currently committing."

The auditor's projection flickered. The filing-cabinet voice came back, lower now. "The Inspector General's office is not subject to the Compliance Rating system."

"Then why do you have one?"

Silence.

Gerald pressed. "You have a rating.

The System is showing it to me right now.

If you're exempt, the rating shouldn't exist. If it exists, you're not exempt. If you're not exempt, you're in violation.

If you're in violation, you lack standing to audit me."

The auditor's projection went rigid. It began to dissolve slowly.

Not all at once.

From the edges inward. Like a document being erased.

"Variance granted," the System announced. "Violation 7.4.1 cured. Compliance Rating recalculated."

The number ticked back up. 69.2. 70.1. 71.4. 72.0.

Gerald exhaled. His hands had stopped shaking somewhere during the argument. He didn't notice until now.

The auditor was gone. The projection had fully dissolved. Gerald was alone in Corridor 7, surrounded by his own confused minions who were still bottlenecked at the pillar but at least no longer being judged for it.

He sat down on the floor. His back against the wall.

The wall was rated for fire, and he appreciated that.

Small comforts.

He had about ninety seconds of peace before the notification arrived.

It wasn't from the auditor. It was from the System's standard enforcement layer. A new citation.

Filed by the Inspector General's office. Not a variance.

Not an audit.

A citation. Direct and immediate.

Gerald opened it.

VIOLATION 12.9.3: UNPERMITTED OCCUPANCY.

Location: Evidence Room, Sub-Level 2.

Details: A Core-class entity designated PETRA, shell-class, amber, is present within the dungeon structure without a registered occupancy permit. Per System Code 12.9, any Core-class entity residing within another Core's dungeon for more than 72 hours without a permit constitutes an unpermitted occupancy.

Penalty: Demolition of affected structure.

Gerald read it twice. Then a third time.

Demolition.

Not a debuff. Not a stat reduction.

Demolition.

The System would physically destroy the Evidence Room. Every wall. Every shelf.

Every cage.

Including the cage with Petra in it, and he looked at the timestamp on the citation. It had been filed eleven minutes ago. During the hearing. While he was arguing about egress.

Someone had reported Petra. Someone had looked at his Evidence Room and filed a complaint.

He pulled up the complaint record. Filed by: Inspector General's Office, Agent K-7.

K-7. The auditor.

The auditor hadn't just lost the hearing, which had retaliated.

Not with a counter-citation. With a demolition order.

And it had filed it before the hearing ended, so it was already in effect. The 72-hour clock on Petra's presence had started when Gerald moved her into the Evidence Room. That was yesterday.

He was past the window, and he couldn't file a variance. Variances were for structural impossibility. An occupancy permit wasn't a structural issue. You either had one or you didn't.

He couldn't appeal.

Appeals required a hearing, and he'd just used his hearing on the egress violation; the System allowed one hearing per audit cycle. His cycle wasn't over.

The audit was still technically open, and he had one option. He could get Petra a permit.

He pulled up the occupancy permit application. It required a structural assessment confirming the host dungeon could support the additional Core without compromising integrity.

A consent signature from the occupying Core.

A consent signature from the host Core. A filing fee.

He had the structural assessment. He'd done it himself. His dungeon could support Petra. He could sign for himself.

But the consent signature from the occupying Core.

Petra was a shell Core. Amber.

Flickering.

Barely conscious.

She'd been Keth's. Keth had been destroyed.

Petra had survived as a remnant. She wasn't fully coherent. She wasn't fully present.

Could she sign?

Gerald walked to the Evidence Room. The cage was there.

Petra's light pulsed.

Slow. Irregular. Like a heartbeat that wasn't sure it wanted to keep going.

He set the permit application on the table beside the cage. He watched the light.

"Petra," he said. "I need you to sign this."

The light pulsed. Once. Twice.

He waited.

The light pulsed again. And then, faintly, a System prompt appeared above the cage. A signature field. A small, amber glow that looked almost like a hand reaching for a pen.

It touched the field.

The System accepted it.

PERMIT APPROVED. OCCUPANCY REGISTERED.

Gerald stared at the approval.

Then he stared at Petra. The light was dimmer now.

Dimmer than before, and the signature had cost something. She'd spent a portion of her remaining coherence to sign.

She was still there. Barely.

Then a second notification. Not from the System.

From outside.

From the dungeon's perimeter.

Someone was at his entrance. Someone with a Core signature he recognized.

Keth's signature.

Not Keth. Keth was dead. But something was wearing Keth's signature like a coat.

Something that had been in his dungeon. Something that had access to his filing system.

Gerald opened the perimeter view.

Standing at his entrance was a figure. Small. Compact.

Built like a filing cabinet.

Its Core signature read: K-7.

The auditor. Outside his dungeon. In the physical world. With a demolition order that had already been filed and a permit that had already been approved.

It was here to inspect the permit. To verify the signature. To confirm that Petra was coherent enough to have consented.

And Gerald knew, with the bone-deep certainty of a man who had spent forty years watching inspectors tear apart buildings, that the auditor was going to find the signature invalid. Because Petra wasn't coherent enough to consent. And the auditor knew that. And it had come here to prove it.

The figure raised its hand. Knocked on the door.

Gerald looked at Petra. The light pulsed. Waiting.

He looked at the door, and he opened it.
