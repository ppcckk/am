Chapter 266: Unmarked Exits

# Chapter 266

The floor was gone.

Gravity remained. That was the first thing Gerald Hutchins noticed.

The second thing he noticed was the silence.

Not the absence of sound, but the absence of code. No hum of ventilation fans. No tick of thermal expansion in the steel beams.

Just the dry, dead air of a space that had been erased from the municipal record.

Gerald hit the ground. He didn't scream.

He landed in a crouch, knees bent, back straight, which was an instinct drilled into him by thirty-one years of stepping off unstable scaffolding. His suit pants tore at the knee. He ignored it.

Above him, the ceiling was a flat, grey plane. No exits. No emergency pull-cords.

The man in the grey suit stood on the edge of the void where Gerald's dungeon used to be. He looked down with the detached curiosity of a man reading a parking ticket.

"Mr. Hutchins," the man said. His voice echoed strangely, bouncing off walls that shouldn't exist. "You are in violation of Spatial Continuity."

Gerald looked up. He adjusted his tie. It was crooked. He fixed it.

"I am not in a dungeon," Gerald said. His voice was dry. Cracked. "I am in a void."

"Correct," the man said. "And you are unlicensed."

Gerald stood up. His joints popped.

The sound was loud in the empty space, and he checked his pockets.

The clipboard was gone. The pen was gone.

But the badge remained, clipped to his lapel. The metal felt cold against his chest.

"Where is the Inspector General?" Gerald asked.

"Pending review," the man said. He checked a watch that had no hands.

"The system is currently processing your dissolution.

It should take approximately three minutes. During this time, you are technically an anomaly. You do not exist.

Therefore, you have no rights.

"

Gerald looked around. The void was not black.

It was a dull, static grey, like the screen of a television with no signal.

Faint outlines of shelves floated in the distance. A bookshelf, suspended by nothing. A desk chair, hanging upside down.

This was not a dungeon. This was a rendering error.

"You made a mistake," Gerald said.

"I did not," the man said. "Clause 19, Subsection Z.

Moral Hazard.

You prioritized the preservation of biological assets over structural neutrality.

You saved the library. You saved the people.

The System requires cores to be neutral entities. By acting, you became a hero. Heroes are not infrastructure.

Infrastructure does not choose. Infrastructure supports.

"

"Infrastructure also fails," Gerald said.

"If the support fails, the building collapses. If the building collapses, the people die. Therefore, the support must prioritize the structural integrity of the life within it.

That is not morality.

That is load-bearing logic.

"

The man tilted his head. A small crack appeared in his grey suit. Not fabric. Reality.

"That is a novel interpretation," the man said. "But it is not code."

"It is physics," Gerald said. "And physics is the ultimate code."

He walked toward the floating bookshelf.

His feet didn't touch the ground. He drifted. The friction was low.

He pushed off the air, using a hand gesture to stabilize himself. He had spent years teaching contractors how to use leverage.

He applied it here, and he reached the bookshelf. It was a standard Oakhaven model, three feet wide, four feet tall. He ran a hand along the spine of a book. It was *Structural Analysis of Load-Bearing Walls, Vol 4*.

Of course.

"Hey!" The man shouted from the edge. "Stay in your designated zone!"

Gerald ignored him. He pulled the book from the shelf. It didn't fall. It stayed suspended in the air where he left it.

"What are you doing?" the man asked.

"Inspecting," Gerald said.

"You have no authority."

"I have eyes," Gerald said. "And I have a clipboard in my memory."

He opened the book. The pages were blank. No text. Just white space.

Gerald frowned, and he flipped through them. White.

White. White.

"Where is the text?" Gerald muttered.

"It is a placeholder," the man said, drifting closer. "The System has deleted the narrative content of your dungeon because you are no longer a Core. The story is gone. Only the geometry remains."

"Geometry is not story," Gerald said. "Geometry is structure. And structure has rules."

He closed the book. He looked at the shelf supports.

Brackets.

Two inches thick. Standard steel.

"The brackets are too thin," Gerald said.

The man blinked.

"What?"

"For the load weight of the books," Gerald said.

"These are hardcovers. Reference volumes.

Heavy.

The brackets are rated for softcover paperbacks. This is a failure point.

"

"The books are not real," the man said. "They are data."

"Data has mass," Gerald said. "In a System, data has weight.

You know this.

You've cited cores for excessive digital storage.

You've fined cores for bloatware that slows down load times. That is weight.

"

The man's eye twitched.

"I am not going to argue physics with a dissolved entity," the man said.

"You don't have to argue," Gerald said. "You just have to agree."

Gerald pushed off the bookshelf, drifting toward the next floating object. A desk. A lamp. A rug.

He was looking for patterns.

In his thirty-one years, Gerald had learned that every building had a skeleton. Even invisible ones. Even voids.

The void was not random. The objects were arranged in a grid. A perfect grid. Three feet by three feet.

Gerald stopped at the center of the grid. He hovered there. He looked at the man.

"You're not in the grid," Gerald said.

The man smiled. It was a thin, paper-cut smile.

"I am the auditor. I stand outside the system."

"No," Gerald said. "You're in it. You're in the negative space."

The man's smile faded.

"Careful, Mr. Hutchins."

Gerald raised his hand. He pointed at the floor.

"Where is the egress?" Gerald asked.

"There is no floor," the man said.

"There is a conceptual floor," Gerald said. "You defined it when you brought me here. You said, 'The ground beneath Gerald's feet dissolved.' Dissolution is a transition.

A transition has a source and a sink. Where is the sink?

"

The man looked at his briefcase. He opened it. The document inside fluttered.

"Clause 19," the man repeated. "Moral Hazard."

"Clause 19 is a trap," Gerald said. "It's a trap for cores who think they are heroes. But it's not a trap for inspectors."

"I do not understand."

"You wrote the code," Gerald said.

"You wrote the rules. You know that every rule has a loop. A loophole.

You can't plug every hole.

Not unless the system is static. But the System is dynamic.

It grows.

It changes. It spawns.

"

"It spawns dungeons," the man said.

"It spawns problems," Gerald corrected. "And problems require solutions. You created a solution called 'Moral Hazard' to punish cores that acted like heroes. But what happens when a core acts like an inspector?"

The man paused. The grey static around them rippled.

"What happens?" the man asked.

"Nothing," Gerald said. "Because an inspector does not save.

An inspector cites, and an inspector does not choose to save the library.

An inspector identifies a violation in the library's foundation and orders the evacuation.

"

Gerald pointed at the floating desk.

"That desk is in a non-compliant zone," Gerald said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"It's blocking the primary egress route," Gerald said. "If the ceiling collapsed, no one could get out. That is a fire code violation. Section 4.2.1."

"I am not a fire marshal," the man said.

"You are a Compliance Officer," Gerald said. "You are bound by the same codes. You cited me for Moral Hazard.

But by keeping me here, in a void with no exits, you are committing a Safety Code Violation. You are trapping a sentient entity in an unventilated, unlit, unmarked space.

"

The man's hand went to his throat.

"That is... a stretch."

"It is not a stretch," Gerald said. "It is precise language. I am citing you."

"You cannot cite me," the man said. "I am not a Core."

"You are a System agent," Gerald said. "System agents are subject to the System. If they are not subject, they are not part of the code. If they are not part of the code, they are illegal."

The static screamed. It was a high-pitched whine, like a feedback loop.

The man stepped back. He fell. Or rather, he ceased to be stable.

"You are disrupting the audit," the man said. His voice was distorted.

"I am auditing the audit," Gerald said.

He reached out. He grabbed the edge of the floating rug. He pulled.

The rug didn't move. But the grid moved.

The floor shifted. Three inches to the left.

The man stumbled, and he caught himself on the air, but his briefcase opened. Papers flew out. They didn't scatter.

They froze in mid-air.

Gerald looked at the papers. They were not documents. They were blueprints.

He recognized them, and they were the blueprints for the Ashford Municipal Library.

But they were wrong.

The load-bearing walls were missing. The foundation was labeled 'Optional'.

"This is not the library," Gerald said.

"It is the potential library," the man said. He was struggling to stay upright. "The version of the library that exists if you had not intervened. The version where you let the structure fail because you were 'neutral'."

Gerald felt a coldness in his stomach. Not fear. Nausea.

"You're showing me the alternative," Gerald said.

"I am showing you the consequence," the man said. "If you had followed the code strictly, the library would have collapsed.

The people would have died.

The System would have rated your dungeon 'Efficient'. Because you did not waste resources on saving people.

"

Gerald looked at the blueprints. He traced the lines with his finger.

"The code is wrong," Gerald said.

"The code is absolute," the man said.

"The code is broken," Gerald said. "And I have the authority to fix it."

The man laughed. It was a dry, rattling sound.

"You have no authority. You are nothing."

"I am the Inspector," Gerald said. "And I have found a violation in the System itself."

He pointed at the blueprints.

"Clause 19 defines 'Moral Hazard' as acting to preserve life over structural integrity. But it does not define 'Structural Integrity'."

The man stopped laughing.

"It is defined," the man said.

"Not clearly," Gerald said. "Ambiguity is a violation.

Section 1.1.4.

'All definitions must be unambiguous to prevent exploitation.' Clause 19 is ambiguous, which does not specify what constitutes 'structural integrity'. Is it the building?

Or the occupants? If the occupants are part of the structure, then saving them is preserving structural integrity. If the occupants are not part of the structure, then the library is not a building.

It is a trap."

The static turned red.

The man's suit unravelled. Threads of grey light floated away.

"Stop," the man said. "You are causing a cascade failure."

"I am causing a citation," Gerald said.

He raised his hand. He mimed writing. He mimed stamping.

*Cited.*

The word hung in the air. It wasn't spoken. It was felt. A pressure.

The man's briefcase snapped shut.

It exploded. Shards of plastic flew outward, but they didn't hit Gerald. They hit the grid.

And where they hit, the grid broke.

Cracks appeared in the void. Black lines.

Gerald walked toward them. He stepped into the crack.

It hurt. It felt like walking through fire.

But Gerald had worked in asbestos abatement, and he knew how to walk through danger. Slow. Deliberate.

Head down.

He stepped through.

The other side was different.

It was not a void. It was a room.

A small, dim room. Stone walls. A single iron door.

Gerald stood in the center of the room. He was breathing hard. His suit was tattered. His badge was bent.

He looked around.

There was no man in a suit.

There was a desk. On the desk was a ledger.

Gerald walked to the desk. He opened the ledger.

The first page was blank, and the second page had a single line.

*Auditor: Void.* *Status: Deleted.* *Core: Hutchins, Gerald.* *Status: Pending Reinstatement.*

Gerald slammed the ledger shut.

"Reinstatement?" he muttered.

A voice came from the iron door.

"Mr.

Hutchins," the voice said.

It was female. Sharp.

Professional.

Gerald turned.

The door opened, and a woman stood there. She wore a suit identical to the man's, but her posture was different. She was taller. She held a clipboard.

"Who are you?" Gerald asked.

"I am the Deputy Inspector General," she said. "And I have a question for you."

She stepped into the room. The floor beneath her feet was solid stone. Real stone.

"You cited the Auditor," she said.

"I cited the violation," Gerald said.

"You cited the System," she said.

"I cited the lack of clarity," Gerald said.

The woman looked at him. She checked her clipboard.

"The System has reviewed your argument," she said. "It is... compelling. However, it is also dangerous."

"Why?"

"Because if you are right," she said, "then every dungeon core has been operating under a flawed interpretation of neutrality. If we accept your definition, then every core that has ever tried to protect its inhabitants was right. And every core that has ever tried to kill intruders was wrong."

"That seems correct," Gerald said.

"It is not correct," the woman said. "It is chaotic. The System requires order. You have introduced chaos."

Gerald straightened his tie.

"Order is not the same as compliance. Sometimes, compliance requires breaking the old order to build a new one."

The woman looked at him. She took a pen from her pocket.

"Mr. Hutchins," she said. "You have created a new subclass of violation."

"What is it?"

"Jurisdictional Overreach," she said. "You have stepped outside your dungeon. You have entered the Administrative Layer. And you have filed a report against a Superior Officer."

Gerald felt the floor tilt again.

"That is a capital offense," the woman said.

Gerald looked at the iron door. Behind her, he could see a corridor.

Long.

Dark. Lined with doors that had no numbers.

"I did not file a report," Gerald said. "I issued a citation."

"There is a difference," the woman said.

"Not in this code," Gerald said.

The woman smiled. It was not a polite smile. It was a predatory one.

"You will be held here," she said. "In the Administrative Layer. While we determine if your citation is valid."

"How long?"

"Until the appeal is heard."

"When is that?"

The woman checked her watch. This one had hands. They spun backward.

"The Inspector General is currently auditing the Eastern Sector," she said. "He will not be available for six months."

Gerald looked at the door. Then he looked at the ledger on the desk.

He picked it up.

"Six months," Gerald said.

"Yes."

"Is there a library?"

"There is a waiting room," the woman said. "It has no books. Only forms."

Gerald opened the ledger again. He tore out the page that said *Status: Pending Reinstatement*.

He folded it. He put it in his pocket.

"I will need a pen," Gerald said.

The woman sighed. She handed him a pen.

"You are making things difficult, Mr. Hutchins."

"I am making things clear," Gerald said.

He walked past her. Into the corridor.

He did not look back, and he knew the rules. He knew that if you follow the code, you can walk anywhere. But only if you know where the exits are.

And he had a feeling that in this place, the exits were not marked.

He would have to measure them himself.
