Chapter 1: Served and Glowing

# Chapter 1

Gerald Hutchins knelt on the concrete floor of Suite 4B and pressed his tape measure against the gap between the support column and its base plate. The gap was three millimeters. The tolerance was one.

He pulled his clipboard from his back pocket and wrote the number down in the column marked VIOLATION. The contractor, a thick-necked man named Dale who wore a tool belt like a fashion accessory, stood behind him with his arms crossed.

"It's within tolerance," Dale said.

"It's not."

"Come on, Hutchins. Three mil. You're telling me the whole thing's gonna come down because of three mil?"

Gerald stood up. His knees cracked. He was fifty-four years old and his knees had been cracking for six years, which was not relevant to the citation but which he noted internally because everything was relevant when you were trying to keep a building from falling on people.

"The tolerance on a load-bearing column base plate is one millimeter," he said. "You're at three. That's a Class B violation. I'm red-tagging this unit."

Dale's face went red in a way that had nothing to do with structural engineering. "You're red-tagging the whole floor because of three millimeters?"

"Yes."

"Over my dead body."

Gerald looked at him. "That would not change the outcome."

He peeled the red tag from his clipboard and zip-tied it to the column. Dale swore, kicked a bucket, and walked out.

Gerald watched him go, then turned back to the column and photographed the base plate with his phone.

The photo would go in the file. The file would go to the city. The city would send a letter.

Dale would fix it or he wouldn't.

Either way, the record was clean.

Gerald moved to Suite 4C, and the contractor had removed a load-bearing wall here; he could tell by the header beam, which was undersized for the span. He measured it.

Four inches by six inches where it should have been six by twelve. He wrote that down too.

Suite 4D had a fire suppression issue.

The sprinkler heads were spaced too far apart.

Suite 4E had an electrical panel with no clearance. Suite 4F was fine.

Gerald liked Suite 4F. Suite 4F had been built by someone who understood that walls were not decorative.

By the time he reached Suite 4G, he had four citations and one red tag. The building was called the Meridian, a nine-story mixed-use project on the east side of Ashford.

Gerald had been inspecting it for three weeks.

The general contractor had been calling him a "career bureaucrat" in increasingly creative ways. Gerald had not responded to any of them because responding would not change the number of millimeters in the gap.

He was writing up the electrical panel violation in 4E when his phone buzzed.

Not a text.

Not a call. A vibration so deep it felt like the phone was trying to crawl into his thigh pocket.

He pulled it out, and the screen was black except for a line of text in white, sans-serif font:

**SYSTEM INITIALIZATION SEQUENCE COMPLETE. COMPLIANCE CORE ASSIGNED.**

Gerald stared at it. He tapped the screen.

The text did not change, and he tried to close the app.

No app existed. The text was burned into the display like a notification that had no notification.

He put the phone away and looked at the electrical panel. The clearance was still insufficient. He finished the citation.

His phone buzzed again.

**COMPLIANCE RATING: 47/100. STATUS: NON-COMPLIANT. DEBUFFS ACTIVE.**

"What debuffs?" Gerald said aloud.

The air in Suite 4E felt thicker. He registered this as he did all room details: as data. The air was thicker, which meant ventilation was below spec, which meant the building's mechanical systems were not functioning at capacity.

He checked his notes. The HVAC rough-in had been approved two weeks ago.

Someone had changed something.

His phone buzzed a third time.

**STRUCTURAL INTEGRITY: 31%. PHYSICAL DAMAGE RESISTANCE: -69%. FIRE SAFETY: 22%.

FIRE DAMAGE RESISTANCE: -78%.

ELECTRICAL SAFETY: 40%. LIGHTNING DAMAGE RESISTANCE: -60%.

VENTILATION: 28%.

REGENERATION RATE: -72%.**

Gerald set down his clipboard. He looked at the ceiling.

The ceiling was a ceiling, which was drywall over steel studs with mineral wool insulation.

It was not a dungeon ceiling, and did not have glowing runes or stalactites or a sense of ancient malevolence.

It was a ceiling that had been installed by a subcontractor named Kowalski who used too few screws.

He picked up his clipboard again. He was going to finish the electrical citation. The System could wait.

It could not wait.

The floor beneath him dropped. Not much.

An inch, maybe two.

The building groaned, but not as buildings usually do. Gerald felt it in his teeth. His coffee cup slid off the workbench and shattered on the concrete.

The lights flickered, then went out, then came back on in a color that was not white, which was the color of a monitor set to a slightly wrong temperature.

Gerald did not run.

Running was not a response to a structural anomaly. Running was what you did when the building was already falling, and this building was not yet falling. It was, however, doing something it had no business doing.

The walls were shifting. Not settling.

Shifting.

The drywall in Suite 4E was bulging inward in a way that suggested the space behind it was no longer the space behind it.

His phone buzzed again.

**DUNGEON FORMATION IN PROGRESS. CORE LOCATION: INSPECTOR UNIT. YOU ARE THE CORE.**

"I am not the Core," Gerald said.

**YOU ARE THE CORE.**

"I am a municipal building inspector. I have a parking validation in my glove compartment."

**YOU ARE THE CORE. COMPLIANCE RATING: 47/100.

YOU MAY UPGRADE STRUCTURE TO IMPROVE RATING. UPGRADES REQUIRE RESOURCES. RESOURCES ARE OBTAINED THROUGH DUNGEON FUNCTION.**

Gerald looked at the wall. The drywall had a seam in it now.

The seam was not in the original construction drawings, and he could tell because he had approved the construction drawings. He had stamped them. He had initialed every page.

The seam was not on any page he had initialed.

He pulled a utility knife from his tool belt and cut the drywall along the seam. Behind it was not the mechanical chase he had approved.

It was a corridor.

Stone walls. Damp. The air coming off it was cold and smelled like copper and something organic.

Gerald put his knife away. He pulled out his phone and took a photo.

He would add this to the file, and the file was the file.

The file was how you remembered things.

The corridor extended about forty feet before it turned left. Gerald did not go down it. He was not going to go down an unauthorized corridor in a building that had not passed inspection.

That was not how this worked. You did not explore a space that had not been permitted.

You cited it, and he walked back to the stairwell; the stairwell was where he had left it, which was good. The stairs were concrete with metal nosing.

The handrail was continuous.

The landing lights were working. This was a compliant stairwell and he was glad about it.

On the third floor, he passed a window, and the view outside was wrong. The street was still there. The cars were still there.

But the sky was the wrong color. It was the color of the monitor light inside the building.

A pale, sickly amber that had no business being in a November afternoon.

People were running.

Not all of them. Most of them were standing still, looking at their phones.

Gerald understood that. He had been looking at his phone too.

He made it to the ground floor. The lobby had changed.

The tile floor was the same tile he had approved, and the ceiling was the same ceiling. But the elevator bank had multiplied. Four elevator doors stood where two had been.

The fourth door had no call button.

The third door opened onto a staircase that went down into darkness.

Gerald took the stairs up. Not the dark ones. The ones he had inspected.

He went back to Suite 4E, and the electrical panel was still there. The clearance was still insufficient. He finished the citation, signed it, and date-stamped it.

Then he sat down on the floor because his knees were bad and because he needed to think.

He thought about the Compliance Rating.

Forty-seven out of one hundred.

The debuffs were listed. Fire damage resistance down seventy-eight percent.

That meant if something in this building caught fire, the structure would take nearly double the damage it should. That was not a game mechanic. That was a physics problem.

That was a load calculation, and he thought about the upgrades.

The System said he could upgrade structure to improve rating. He knew how to do that. He had been doing it for thirty-one years.

You fixed the violations.

You brought things up to code. The System was asking him to do the thing he had always done.

He pulled out his phone. The notification was still there. He tapped on it.

A menu appeared.

It looked like a spreadsheet. Categories down the left side: STRUCTURAL, FIRE SAFETY, ELECTRICAL, VENTILATION, PLUMBING, ACCESSIBILITY. Percentages down the right.

The numbers were red.

Gerald felt something he had not felt in years. He felt like he was at work.

He started with STRUCTURAL. The category listed nine violations. Seven of them were things he had already cited in his inspection. The other two were things the System had generated: a load-bearing wall that had been replaced with a decorative arch, and a foundation crack that ran the full length of the building's north side.

He could fix the foundation crack, and he knew how.

He could not fix it with a citation. He needed materials.

The System said resources were obtained through dungeon function. He did not know what dungeon function meant. He did not know what a dungeon was.

He knew what a foundation crack was. He knew what it meant if you left it.

His phone buzzed.

**NOTICE: ADJACENT CORE DETECTED. RANGE: 200 METERS. COMPLIANCE RATING: 12/100. STATUS: CRITICALLY NON-COMPLIANT.**

Gerald looked at the number. Twelve.

He had never seen a building at twelve, and he had seen buildings at thirty. He had seen buildings at thirty-five and he had called the mayor's office and the fire marshal and the structural engineering department and he had stayed on site until the building was red-tagged and the tenants were evacuated and the crane was brought in to take it down floor by floor.

Twelve was not a building. Twelve was a pile of rubble with a permit.

He looked at the adjacent Core's details.

The System listed its violations. Forty-three were present.

No fire suppression.

No egress routes. No ventilation. No structural support on the third and fourth floors.

Electrical wiring exposed and ungrounded. Plumbing cross-connected with the storm drain.

Gerald felt his stomach turn. Not with fear. With the specific nausea he got when he saw a ground-fault interrupter bypassed with a penny.

He pulled out his citation pad. The real one. The paper one. He had carried it for thirty-one years because paper did not glitch and paper did not have a battery and paper did not tell you that you were a dungeon Core when you were a building inspector.

He wrote the first line. He wrote it as he always did.

**CITATION NO. 2024-1147.

PREMISES: ADJACENT CORE, 200M RANGE.

VIOLATIONS: 43. SEVERITY: CRITICAL.

STATUS: SERVED.**

He held the pad up and the System recognized it. The text glowed. The adjacent Core's Compliance Rating dropped from 12 to 8.

Gerald looked at the number. Eight.

He turned the page.
