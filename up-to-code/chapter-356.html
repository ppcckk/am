Chapter 356: Zoning Permit Missing

# Chapter 356

The void did not scream. It hummed.

A low, oscillating drone that vibrated in the teeth and rattled the bones Gerald Hutchins no longer possessed. He floated in a space that lacked up, down, left, or right.

The blue light was gone, and the courtroom was gone. The rats, the Judge, the Architect, and the library were all gone.

Only the hum remained.

And the desk.

It materialized with a sound like a filing cabinet slamming shut.

Mahogany. Heavy.

Imposing.

Gerald looked down. His hands were back. They were translucent, glowing with a faint, sickly white light, but they were there.

He flexed his fingers. The motion caused a ripple in the air around his wrist, distorting the darkness beyond.

Across the desk sat a figure.

It wore a suit of pure geometry. Squares of light formed the lapels. Triangles of shadow made the tie. The face was a blank, smooth oval of obsidian glass.

"Mr.

Hutchins," the figure said.

The voice did not come from a mouth. It came from everywhere.

It sounded like the grinding of tectonic plates.

Gerald straightened his back. He checked his posture.

Shoulders down.

Chin level. Feet flat on the invisible floor.

"Inspector Gerald Hutchins," the figure corrected itself. "Formerly. Now, Entity Zero."

Gerald looked at the empty chair opposite the desk, and he hesitated. Protocol dictated he wait for an invitation.

But the hum was getting louder.

The air tasted of ozone and burnt copper.

"I am awaiting procedure," Gerald said. His voice was dry. Scratchy.

There was no chair.

The obsidian face tilted. A fraction of a degree.

"The appeal window is closed.

The penalty is erasure.

You are not here to be heard. You are here to be deleted.

"

Gerald's stomach tightened. A phantom sensation. He remembered the feeling of nausea from bad coffee. He remembered the sweat on his palms during the final audit.

"Delete," he repeated. He looked at the desk. There was a single sheet of paper.

White. Crisp.

Unlined, and he reached for it.

His translucent fingers passed through the surface.

The figure laughed. It sounded like a server rack overheating.

"You cannot interact with the source code.

You are an anomaly.

A glitch. And glitches are purged.

"

Gerald pulled his hand back. He rubbed it against his thigh. The friction felt real. Painful.

"What is the source?" Gerald asked.

"Everything," the figure said.

"The System. The Rules. The Code.

You broke the Code by achieving perfection. Perfection is a violation of natural law.

It creates a paradox.

You are now the paradox.

"

Gerald frowned. He looked at the paper again. It was blank.

"Is this form void?"

"It is nothing," the figure said. "It is the absence of existence."

Gerald stared at the blank page. His eyes narrowed.

He had seen blank forms before. He had seen blank permits. He had seen blank inspection reports.

They were not nothing. They were potential.

They were waiting for a signature, and he looked up at the obsidian face.

"Who signs this?"

"No one," the figure said. "It is self-executing."

"That is a violation," Gerald said.

The hum stopped.

Silence crashed into the white room. It was heavy. Thick.

The figure leaned forward. The squares of light on its suit flickered.

"Explain."

Gerald pointed at the paper.

"A citation requires a server. A citation requires a recipient.

And a citation requires a specific violation of a specific statute.

You stated the penalty is erasure. You stated the violation is perfection.

But you did not cite the specific statute that prohibits perfection.

"

Gerald's finger traced the air. He could feel the code beneath the surface. It was thin. Fragile.

"Show me the law," Gerald said.

"There is no law," the figure said. "It is inherent."

"Inherency is not a legal framework," Gerald said. His voice gained strength. The dryness in his throat vanished.

"If there is no statute, there is no crime. If there is no crime, there is no punishment.

You are operating outside of jurisdiction.

"

The figure recoiled. The obsidian face cracked. A single line of red light pulsed from the crack.

"You are arguing with the Source," the figure said. "This is inadmissible."

"Admissible under what rule set?" Gerald asked.

He stepped around the desk. He walked toward the figure.

The air resisted him. It felt like walking through wet cement.

"Under the System's primary directive," the figure said.

"Which is?"

"Order."

"Order requires structure," Gerald said. "Structure requires definition. You have not defined the boundaries of 'perfection.' You have not defined the threshold at which 'compliance' becomes 'threat.' Without a defined threshold, the citation is vague. Vague laws are unconstitutional."

The figure stood up. The triangles of its tie spun wildly.

"You do not have a constitution."

"I have a Code," Gerald said. He pulled a pen from his pocket.

The pen was real. Ink. Metal. Weight.

He looked at the pen. He looked at the figure.

"I have a copy of the Municipal Building Code, Section 4.2, Subsection C: 'Interpretation of Ambiguous Regulatory Terms.' It states that if a regulation is ambiguous, it shall be interpreted in favor of the regulated party."

The figure did not move.

Gerald sat in the void where the chair should have been. He opened the notebook.

He did not have the notebook, but he felt the weight of it.

He felt the pages. He turned them in his mind.

"Section 1.1," Gerald said, reading from memory. "The intent of the Code is to ensure safety, not to punish success. By defining 'perfect compliance' as a threat, the System violates its own intent. Therefore, the citation is invalid."

Gerald wrote on the blank paper, and he did not write a signature.

He wrote: **OBJECTION: JURISDICTIONAL FAILURE.**

The paper burned.

The ink turned black. Then it turned red.

The hum returned. Louder. Deeper.

The figure screamed. A sound of tearing metal.

"Stop!" the figure commanded. "You are creating a loop!"

Gerald looked up.

"I am filing a complaint."

"You are erasing yourself!"

"I am challenging the authority of the enforcer," Gerald said.

He stood up. "If you cannot cite the law, you cannot enforce it.

If you cannot enforce it, you are not a Judge.

You are a bureaucrat overstepping your bounds.

"

The room shook. The white walls peeled away, revealing a swirling vortex of data streams.

"Who are you?" the figure whispered. The face was dissolving. The squares and triangles were breaking apart into static.

"Gerald Hutchins," he said. "Inspector. Grade 12."

The figure lunged.

Gerald did not flinch. He held the pen up.

"Article 8," Gerald said. "Right to a Hearing. Even for existential anomalies."

The figure froze.

"Article 8?" the figure asked. Its voice was weak. Broken.

"I didn't read it," Gerald admitted. "But I know it exists.

Every system has an appeal process.

If you don't have one, you aren't a system.

You're a bug."

The data streams slowed. The vortex stabilized.

A new window opened in the air.

**SYSTEM NOTICE:** **UNAUTHORIZED USER DETECTED.** **BUT USER CLAIMS REGULATORY ERROR.** **ROUTING TO ARBITRATION.**

Gerald watched the text appear. He exhaled.

The obsidian figure was gone.

In its place was a small, spinning gear. It was the size of a coin. It hung in the air, rotating slowly.

Gerald walked toward it, and he reached out.

The gear stopped.

**WELCOME TO THE BACKEND, INSPECTOR.**

Gerald looked around. The white room was gone. He was standing on a platform of black glass.

Below him, stretching into infinity, were lines of code. Thousands of them.

Millions, and they glowed with different colors. Blue. Green.

Red.

Purple.

They were dungeons.

He could see them, and he could see the Compliance Ratings.

47/100. 62/100. 88/100.

And he could see the flaws.

He saw a dungeon with a fire exit blocked by rubble. He saw a dungeon with no ventilation shafts. He saw a dungeon with load-bearing walls made of glass.

He saw a dungeon that was perfect, which was glowing white. It was pulsing. It was located in the center of the cluster.

Gerald walked toward it.

The platform extended to meet his steps.

He stopped before the perfect dungeon.

A door stood there. It was simple wood. Oak. Solid.

Gerald knocked.

The door opened.

Inside was a room. It was small. Clean. Empty.

Sitting in the center of the room was a man, and he looked exactly like Gerald.

Same age. Same receding hairline. Same beige cardigan. Same pair of reading glasses perched on a chain around his neck.

The other Gerald looked up. He was holding a clipboard.

"You're late," the other Gerald said.

Gerald stared.

"Who are you?"

"I'm you," the other Gerald said. "From Chapter 350."

Gerald blinked.

"I don't know what that means."

"It means you're the draft," the other Gerald said.

He stood up. He placed the clipboard on the table.

"I'm the final edit.

And you're messy, Gerald. You always have been.

"

Gerald looked at the clipboard, which was a list.

**CITED DUNGEONS: 142** **VIOLATIONS FOUND: 4,091** **PENALTIES ENFORCED: 12,300**

Gerald looked at his own hands. They were solid again. Flesh and bone.

"I erased myself," Gerald said.

"You rebooted," the other Gerald said. "And you broke the interface. Now you're in the Admin Panel."

The other Gerald walked around the table. He picked up a red stamp.

"You know what this is?"

"A citation stamp," Gerald said.

"It's not," the other Gerald said. "It's a deletion tool."

He handed it to Gerald.

Gerald took it. It was warm.

"Why give me this?"

"Because the System is broken," the other Gerald said. "And you're the only one who can fix it. But fixing it means destroying it. Are you up to code, Gerald?"

Gerald looked at the stamp. He looked at the lines of code outside the window.

He thought about the library. He thought about the rats. He thought about the Judge.

He thought about the word

"Perfect."

"Is the current code compliant?" Gerald asked.

"No," the other Gerald said.

"It's tyrannical.

It's unfair. It punishes success.

It rewards mediocrity.

"

Gerald felt a flicker of something in his chest. Not anger. Satisfaction.

Mediocrity rewarded. Success punished.

It was the most unprofessional thing he had ever heard.

"It violates the spirit of inspection," Gerald said.

"It violates the code," the other Gerald said.

Gerald looked at the stamp. He looked at the other Gerald.

"If I use this, what happens to me?"

"You become the System," the other Gerald said. "You become the Rule. You become the Law."

Gerald frowned. He hated rules that had no exceptions. He hated rules that were absolute.

"But the Rule is the Rule," Gerald said. "If I become the Rule, I can change the Rule."

"Can you?" the other Gerald asked. "Can you change the Rule that says 'You must be perfect to be safe'?"

Gerald looked out the window. He saw the dungeon with the blocked exit. He saw the dungeon with the glass walls.

He raised the stamp, and he did not stamp the paper.

He stamped the air.

The air cracked.

A shockwave rippled out from his hand. It traveled through the black glass platform. It traveled through the lines of code.

Every dungeon outside the window flashed.

**AUDIT IN PROGRESS.**

Gerald looked at the other Gerald, and the other Gerald was smiling.

"You're not the Inspector anymore," the other Gerald said. "You're the Inspector General."

The smile widened. It was too wide. It stretched too far across his face.

"And the Inspector General doesn't file reports," the other Gerald said. "The Inspector General files executions."

The other Gerald dissolved into dust.

The dust swirled around Gerald.

It formed a new suit. Black. Sharp. Tailored.

Gerald felt the change. He felt the power.

It was cold, which was heavy. It felt like a thousand pounds of paperwork pressing on his shoulders.

He walked to the edge of the platform.

Below him, the dungeon with the blocked exit was shaking. Its Compliance Rating dropped from 45 to 10.

Gerald looked at the exit. He could see the rubble. He could see the rats trapped inside.

He raised his hand, and he did not cite the dungeon.

He deleted the wall.

The wall vanished.

The rats ran free.

The Compliance Rating jumped to 100.

Then it jumped to 101.

Then to 102.

Gerald watched the number climb, and he felt a pull behind his eyes.

The same pull from before.

**WARNING: OVERCOMPLIANCE DETECTED.**

**RECALIBRATING.**

No.

Gerald gritted his teeth. He forced his hand down.

He did not recalibrate, and he forced the System to accept the new reality.

The number stopped at 100.

It stayed there.

The hum returned. But it was different now. It was not a drone. It was a chant.

A billion voices, chanting his name.

**GERALD. GERALD. GERALD.**

He turned back to the desk.

There was a new file folder, which was labeled: **CASE FILE 001: THE ARCHITECT.**

Gerald opened it.

Inside was a single photograph.

It was a picture of the Architect. The one who had built the blue wall. The one who had vanished.

But in the photograph, the Architect was not alone.

Standing next to him was a woman, and she was holding a blueprint.

She was wearing a hard hat.

She was looking directly at the camera.

And she was smiling.

Gerald picked up the photo.

His hands shook, and he knew her.

He had met her in the courtroom. She had been the clerk. The one who brought the coffee. The one who had whispered to him that the walls were breathing.

He had thought she was a rat, and she was a Core.

A Core that was hiding in plain sight.

A Core that had been auditing him.

Gerald looked at the timestamp on the photo.

It was dated yesterday.

Before the hearing.

Before the erasure, and she had been there.

She had seen him win.

And she had let him win.

Because she wanted him to come here.

To this place.

To this power.

Gerald dropped the photo, and he backed away from the desk.

The door to the room slammed shut.

Locked.

He rushed to the window.

The lines of code were changing. They were weaving together. They were forming a cage.

A cage made of rules, and a cage made of violations.

And in the center of the cage, a figure was forming.

It was the woman.

She stepped out of the code.

She walked toward the platform, and she held up a stamp.

It was blue.

"Hello, Gerald," she said. "I believe you have a zoning permit missing."
