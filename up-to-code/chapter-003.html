Chapter 3

The parking garage smelled like urine and ozone. Gerald noted both. Urine meant water intrusion at the base slab. Ozone meant something electrical was running hot and ungrounded. He was twenty meters inside the structure before Core-9902's voice came through the concrete.

"You're early."

"I'm on time." Gerald's shoes squeaked on the epoxy-coated floor. He kept walking. "Your entry point is a Class B egress door blocked by a collapsed column. I'm using the service ramp instead."

A pause. "The column is load-bearing."

"It was load-bearing. Now it's a pile of rubble sitting on top of a load-bearing wall. Which means the wall is carrying it. Which means the wall is overloaded by roughly forty percent. Which means your entire second floor is a collapse hazard."

Silence. Then: "That's not in the checklist."

Gerald pulled out his pad. "I know what's in the checklist. I'm telling you what's in the building."

He reached the second level. The garage had been converted into a dungeon six years ago, but the bones were still visible. Original concrete columns. Original rebar grid. Original painted stall numbers. Someone had carved corridors between the columns and filled the spaces with dirt floors and torch sconces. The effect was a medieval crypt poured inside a JCPenney.

Gerald didn't care about the aesthetic. He cared about the load path.

He stopped at a junction where three corridors met. The ceiling above was a thin concrete slab, maybe four inches. The floor below was original garage slab, six inches. The columns between them were original, eighteen-inch square, spaced at twenty-four feet on center.

Twenty-four feet on center. For a parking garage, that was fine. For a dungeon with active monsters moving around, with cave-ins and explosions and whatever else these things did, that was a span that needed intermediate support.

He typed into his pad: *Your column spacing is wrong for your current occupancy classification.*

Core-9902's voice came from everywhere. "Occupancy classification?"

"The System assigns you one. I can see it in the audit overlay. You're listed as Group R-2, residential. That's what a parking garage gets reclassified to when it's converted to habitable space. But you're not residential. You're a high-traffic combat environment with active kinetic loads. That's Group A-3, assembly. Assembly requires column spacing of sixteen feet maximum for spans over twelve feet."

Another pause. Longer this time.

"You're saying my dungeon is in violation of its own structural code."

"I'm saying your dungeon is classified as something it isn't. That's a Change of Use Without Permit. Section 10.4. It's not on your checklist because your checklist is for the category you were assigned. You need a different checklist. You need an A-3 checklist."

Gerald could feel Core-9902 thinking. The air pressure in the corridor shifted slightly, like a held breath.

"If I reclassify," Core-9902 said slowly, "I lose compliance points. My current rating is 87. If I reclassify to A-3 and my column spacing fails, I drop to..."

"Sixty-three. Maybe sixty."

"That's not survivable for the Inspector General."

"No. It's not."

Gerald kept walking. He was mapping as he went, the way he'd done for thirty-one years. You walk the perimeter. You note the deficiencies. You write the report. The report is the weapon.

He reached the third level. Here the conversion was more thorough. Someone had installed a sprinkler system. It was connected to a water main that ran under the garage's original drainage trench. The pipes were PVC.

Gerald stopped.

"Your sprinklers are PVC."

"They're rated for—"

"They're not rated for anything. PVC softens at 140 degrees. A fire in a dungeon with combustible materials will hit that in ninety seconds. Your sprinklers will melt before they activate. That's not a violation. That's a death sentence for anyone inside."

He typed it. *Sprinkler material non-compliant. Fire code, Section 4.2. Severity: critical.*

Core-9902 went quiet.

Then: "You're writing a citation."

"I'm taking notes."

"You're writing a citation. You're in my dungeon. You're writing a citation. I can feel the System flagging it."

Gerald looked up. The audit overlay in his peripheral vision showed a small amber icon pulsing near Core-9902's core location. A citation draft. Unfiled. Unserved. But drafted.

"I'm taking notes," Gerald said again. "Whether it becomes a citation is a separate question."

"It's not separate. The System knows you're drafting. It's already applying a provisional reduction to my Compliance Rating. Three points. Pending service."

Gerald felt something cold move through his chest. He hadn't known that. He hadn't known that drafting a citation had immediate mechanical consequences.

"I didn't know that," he said.

"No. You didn't. And now you've cost me three points and you're still standing in my dungeon, and I want to know why."

Gerald sat down on a concrete ledge. He put his pad on his knee. "Because you called me here to trade information. You offered me a checklist. I offered you knowledge of the citation mechanic. But you wanted to see my face when I realized what Section 7.3 actually says. You wanted to watch me be confused. You wanted me to be the confused one. You're a Core with six years of infrastructure and a rating of 87, and you wanted to watch a man be confused because it makes you feel like you're still in control."

The air pressure shifted again.

"That's not what this is."

"It's what this is. And I'm not confused. I'm inspecting. Those are different things." Gerald tapped his pad. "Here's what I know. You have a structural violation that will kill you during the audit. You have a fire code violation that will kill your occupants. You have a classification mismatch that means your entire compliance framework is built on the wrong assumptions. And you have forty-seven hours to fix all of it."

"And you have four hours."

"I have four hours. And I have nothing. No walls. No systems. No code compliance at all. My rating is probably zero."

"Probably negative."

Gerald almost smiled. Almost. "Then we're both in trouble. And the only way out is if I help you fix your violations, and you help me understand the checklist. That's the trade. That's always been the trade."

Core-9902 was silent for a long time. The torches flickered. Somewhere below, Gerald heard the grinding of stone. A golem, maybe. Or something worse.

"Fine," Core-9902 said. "But you're not leaving until I've seen your notes. All of them."

"That's reasonable."

"It's not reasonable. It's necessary. If you leave with those notes, you can cite me from range. I need to know what you've written."

Gerald looked at his pad. He looked at the amber icon still pulsing near Core-9902's core. Three points. He'd already cost Core-9902 three points just by being thorough.

He turned the pad around.

Core-9902 read the notes. Gerald could tell because the torches dimmed, then brightened, then dimmed again. The Core was processing.

When it finished, it said: "The sprinkler violation. You're right. I need to replace the PVC with CPVC or metal. I can do that in six hours if I spawn a water elemental to do the plumbing."

"Good."

"The column spacing. I can't fix that. Not in time. I'd have to demolish and rebuild the second floor."

"No. You don't. You need to reclassify to A-3, then apply for a variance. Section 10.4 has a variance provision for existing structures. You cite the original construction date, the change-of-use date, and the impossibility of retrofit. The System grants a provisional variance for existing structures that predate the current code."

Silence.

"That's not in the checklist either."

"No. It's not."

Gerald stood up. "You owe me a checklist. The real one. Not the one you prepared for me. The one you actually use."

Core-9902 projected it onto the air between them. A dense grid of requirements, cross-referenced, color-coded. Gerald scanned it in four seconds. His eyes moved the way they always moved across a plan set: top to bottom, left to right, looking for the thing that didn't match.

He found it on line forty-three.

"You're using the wrong fire load calculation for your third level. You're calculating based on storage occupancy. You have monsters spawning there. Monster biomass has a different combustion coefficient than stored goods. You're underestimating your fire load by a factor of three."

Core-9902's torches went out. All of them. Every light in the dungeon died simultaneously.

Gerald stood in perfect darkness.

Then the lights came back. Dimmer. Red.

"You know too much," Core-9902 said. Its voice was flat now. Stripped of the performative confidence from before. "You walked in here with nothing. No dungeon. No walls. No defenses. And you've already found enough violations to drop me below sixty. And you're doing it by reading my building like it's a building. Because that's what it is. That's what all of us are. We're buildings. And you're an inspector."

"I am."

"And if I let you leave with what you know, you could do this to every Core within range."

"Yes."

The red lights pulsed. Gerald could feel the System humming around him, the way you can feel a transformer buzzing in a wall. Something was changing. The audit overlay in his vision shifted. A new icon appeared. Not amber. Red.

It was his own icon. His own Compliance Rating.

It read: 4.

Gerald stared at it. Four. He had a rating of four. He hadn't built anything. He hadn't even chosen a location for his dungeon. And the System had already assigned him a rating. Four. Below the dissolution threshold of ten.

"You're being audited," Core-9902 said. "Right now. Right here. Because you're inside my dungeon, the System is treating you as an adjacent structure. You're being scored on my compliance."

Gerald's stomach dropped. He looked at the number again. Four. Not a rounding error. Not a display glitch. Four.

Then he understood.

The System's adjacency clause. Section 14.2 of the Dungeon Compliance Framework. A structure without its own defined footprint inherits the compliance score of the nearest registered structure. Gerald had no footprint. No walls. No foundation. He was a floating set of eyes and a clipboard inside someone else's building.

Which meant his score wasn't his.

It was Core-9902's.

"Wait," Gerald said.
