Chapter 81: Nonconforming Use Permit

# Chapter 81

Gerald's pencil moved across the form in the small, tight handwriting that had filled thirty-one years of inspection reports. The Core pulsed beneath him, warm and patient, waiting.

He wrote the category. He wrote the subcategory. He wrote the justification paragraph, which took four lines and used the word "predates" twice because the System's form parser would flag anything else.

Then he signed it, and the Core went still.

"What did you do?" it asked.

"I filed a Nonconforming Use Permit." Gerald held up the form. The System's blue light was already crawling across the paper, parsing it, categorizing it.

"You're a pre-existing structure.

You were here before the current System established its code framework. You can't be retrofitted because the code didn't exist when you were built. You can only be maintained."

"That is not a compliance category."

"It's category 7-C, subcategory 4, variance type 11.

Structures predating the establishment of applicable code. I used it on the Ashford Street water tower in 2019.

The tower was built in 1962.

The electrical code changed in 1974. They couldn't retrofit the wiring because the original conduit was buried under three feet of concrete and the concrete had become load-bearing. " Gerald looked at the Core. " Same logic.

You're the water tower."

The Core was quiet for a long time. The warmth under Gerald's feet pulsed twice, three times, then steadied.

"The System is accepting this."

"It's accepting the filing. That doesn't mean it's approved."

"What happens if it's approved?"

"Then you're a legal nonconforming structure.

You can't be inspected for compliance because you're grandfathered in.

The System can't cite you. It can't retrofit you.

It can't self-inspect you because there's no jurisdictional basis for the inspection. " Gerald put the chisel down. " You're in limbo. You're the thing that exists in the gap between what the code says and what the code can reach."

The Core pulsed. Something in the rhythm changed.

Gerald couldn't name it.

Not warmth, not cold. Something like relief. Or maybe the Core didn't feel relief.

Maybe it was just the System's processing signature shifting, and the form went blue. Then white. Then it was gone. Absorbed into the floor.

The System had accepted it.

Gerald felt it before he saw it. A pressure change, like the air in a room when someone opens a window on a windless day. The dungeon's ambient hum shifted pitch, dropped a half-tone, then stabilized.

"Approved," the Core said. "Category 7-C, subcategory 4, variance type 11. I am a legal nonconforming structure."

"You're a legal nonconforming structure," Gerald confirmed.

For the first time in eleven thousand years, the Core was safe. Gerald could feel that.

He sensed it, like a load-bearing wall behind drywall.

Something structural had shifted. The pressure was gone.

Then his phone buzzed.

Not a text. Not a call.

A System notification, the kind that meant an Inspector General audit had been triggered.

Gerald had received four of these in the past sixty chapters.

Each one meant he had forty-eight hours to prepare his dungeon for a full compliance inspection.

He looked at the notification.

This one was different.

This one was addressed to him. Not as a dungeon core. As a property owner.

*NOTICE: You have been identified as the responsible party for the structure registered under Form 7-C/4/11, filed [timestamp]. As property owner, you are responsible for all ongoing compliance obligations associated with the registered structure. Your Compliance Rating has been updated to reflect this responsibility.*

Gerald read it twice.

Then he looked at his Compliance Rating, which had been 87. He'd spent sixty chapters bringing it up through handrails and egress signage and fire extinguishers and proper ventilation. Sixty chapters of careful, methodical, maddening work.

It was 4 now.

The original System's Compliance Rating had been merged into his. The Core's eleven-thousand-year-old nonconformance was now his responsibility. And the Core's Compliance Rating was,, 4 out of 100.

Gerald sat down on the floor.

"Wait," he said.

The Core pulsed.

"I filed the form. I made you legal. But the filing also made me your property owner. And property owners are responsible for compliance."

"Yes."

"Your Compliance Rating is four."

"Yes."

"That's now my Compliance Rating."

"Yes."

Gerald closed his eyes.

At a Compliance Rating of 4, the System applied proportional debuffs to all spawned entities. At 12, his mold spores had had reduced regen. At 4, his spawns would be dead.

Not weakened. Dead.

The rat would stop existing.

The water leak would stop leaking. The mold spores would be inert matter.

And his own Core stats. His dungeon's structural integrity. His ability to maintain the dungeon at all.

At 4, he was at critical structural failure, and he had forty-eight hours before the Inspector General arrived. And when the IG arrived, it would see his Compliance Rating. It would see that his dungeon was, by the System's own metrics, a Class 4 fire hazard with no egress, no ventilation, no fire suppression.

It would issue a demolition order.

And Gerald Hutchins, municipal building inspector, could not fight a demolition order.

He had spent thirty-one years enforcing them. He knew exactly how they worked.

He knew the order was final once issued.

He knew the timeline. He knew that once the order was issued, the structure came down.

Even if the structure was him, and he stood up.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. I need to bring your Compliance Rating up."

"You cannot retrofit me. You know this.

The materials do not exist.

The methods do not exist.

I was built by a System that no longer exists, using processes that have no current equivalent."

"I know."

"Then what are you going to do?"

Gerald looked at where the form had been. The filing was permanent. He was the property owner. The obligation was permanent.

But obligations had procedures. And procedures had loopholes. And loopholes had paperwork.

"I need to file a Variance Request for the Compliance Rating itself," he said. "A Compliance Rating is a measurement. Measurements can be contested. If I can prove the measurement is invalid, the Rating gets recalculated."

"How would you prove that?"

Gerald thought about the Ashford Street water tower. He thought about the 2019 variance. He thought about the time a restaurant owner had argued that his kitchen hood didn't meet current code because the building's original ventilation shaft was too narrow to accommodate a modern hood, and the inspector had agreed, granting a variance based on physical impossibility of compliance.

"I argue physical impossibility," Gerald said. "You were built by a System that no longer exists. The code that applies to you was written by that System.

The current System inherited your structure but not your code. So the current System's Compliance Rating is measuring you against a code you were never built to satisfy.

That's not a violation.

That's a measurement error."

The Core pulsed. Slow. Deliberate.

"That is a valid argument."

"It's not an argument.

It's a fact. The current System's code framework was established after you.

You can't be measured against a code that didn't exist when you were built.

The Rating is invalid."

"Will the Inspector General accept this?"

Gerald looked at his phone. Forty-eight hours.

The IG would come.

It would look at his Rating.

It would issue the demolition order.
