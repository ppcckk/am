Chapter 24: Hacksaw and Audit

# Chapter 24

The sprinkler head was wrong.

Gerald held it up to the light filtering through his ventilation shaft and rotated it ninety degrees. The threading was coarse.

The discharge pattern was conical, which was designed for a room with a ceiling height of nine feet or more. His main corridor topped out at seven feet four inches. A conical discharge pattern at that height would create a wet-floor condition that would itself constitute a slip-and-fall violation under the same code he was trying to satisfy.

He set the sprinkler head down on the workbench he had built from a collapsed shelf bracket and a plank of pine.

He picked up another. This one was fine-threaded with a flat discharge plate.

Correct for the ceiling height, and he set it aside.

A notification chimed.

*New Citation filed against your dungeon. Citing: absence of required emergency lighting in Corridor 2-B. Debuff applied: All spawned entities -15% speed. Duration: 72 hours.*

Gerald looked at the timestamp. Four minutes after the last one.

*New Citation filed against your dungeon. Citing: absence of required handrail on east staircase. Debuff applied: All spawned entities -10% health. Duration: 72 hours.*

Three minutes.

*New Citation filed against your dungeon. Citing: absence of required occupancy signage at primary entrance. Debuff applied: All spawned entities -8% health. Duration: 72 hours.*

Two minutes.

His Compliance Rating was 2. He could feel it in the dungeon.

The leak in the north corner had slowed to a trickle; the water was sluggish, as if cited.

His single rat was lying on its side near the east wall, breathing in long shallow pulls.

The mold spores had stopped spreading. Everything in his dungeon was dying at the speed the System said it should die, which was slower than it should die, which was the whole point.

Gerald opened his binder to the appeal form, and he had filed one appeal already, on the first Citation, and won it. That had bounced his rating from 2 to 4 and reversed the health debuff.

But the new Core had not stopped. It had filed six more Citations in the time it took Gerald to fill out the appeal paperwork, attend the Hearing, argue the corridor designation technicality, and return to his workbench.

The Hearing had gone well.

Gerald had stood in the System's abstract Hearing space, a featureless grey room with two podiums, and stated for the record that Corridor 2-B had not yet been designated as a "primary corridor" under Form 7-C, and therefore the fire suppression requirement cited did not apply to a corridor that the System had not yet classified as primary. The Hearing adjudicator, a faceless figure that the System rendered as a clipboard with legs, had reviewed his documentation and ruled in his favor. The Citation was void.

The debuff reversed.

But voiding one Citation did not stop the next one, and the new Core was citing him faster than he could appeal. The appeal window was twenty-four hours per Citation, but the new Core was filing one every two to four minutes.

Gerald could not physically attend twenty-four Hearings in a day.

The System would not allow it. He had checked. The Hearing queue was limited to one active appeal per Core at a time.

So the Citations stacked. The debuffs stacked. His rating fell.

Gerald closed the appeal form. He opened a fresh page in his binder and wrote: "Strategy: stop being cited."

He thought about that for thirty seconds. Then he crossed it out. It was not a strategy. It was a wish.

He needed to cite back, and he opened his range map; the System displayed all registered Cores within his operational range as colored dots. The Undercroft was a large red dot to the northwest, pulsing slowly, still under the debuff from Gerald's earlier Citation. Three other dungeons were scattered across the map in various shades of orange and yellow, each with their own Compliance Ratings visible if Gerald focused on them.

The new Core was a small green dot. Bright green. The color the System assigned to Cores with a Compliance Rating above 80.

That was impossible. A Core that had registered four minutes ago could not have a Compliance Rating above 80.

It had no dungeon, which had no corridors, no rooms, no ventilation, no fire suppression, no egress signage.

A brand-new Core was, by definition, in violation of every code category simultaneously. Its rating should have been in the single digits.

Gerald focused on the green dot. The System displayed the Core's name.

MARCUS HUTCHINS.

Gerald stared at it. He stared at it for a long time. The notification chimes kept coming. His rat stopped breathing for a moment and then started again.

He closed the range map. He opened it again. The name was the same.

Marcus Hutchins. His son. Twenty-three years old.

A barista. A person who had called Gerald's apartment twice in the last three years, both times to ask for money, both times after drinking.

A person who had once told Gerald, at a Thanksgiving dinner that had gone badly, that following rules was "the coward's way of being right."

His son had become a dungeon Core. And his son was citing him.

Gerald sat down on the floor of his main corridor. The concrete was cold. His back ached. He was fifty-four years old and he had a Compliance Rating of 2 and his son was killing him with paperwork.

He thought about what Marcus knew about code. The answer was: nothing. Marcus did not know what Form 7-C was.

Marcus did not know the difference between a primary corridor and a secondary corridor.

Marcus had not read the code. Marcus had not read anything.

But Marcus had read the System's Citation interface, which was enough. The System made it easy. You selected a category.

You selected a violation. You pressed submit.

The System checked whether the violation was technically valid.

If it was, the Citation went through.

If it was not, the Citation was rejected and the Core was fined for frivolous filing.

Marcus was not filing frivolous Citations. Every one of them was valid. Gerald's dungeon genuinely lacked fire suppression, emergency lighting, handrails on the east staircase, and occupancy signage.

Marcus was not making things up. He scanned Gerald's dungeon like the System did, locating gaps and submitting.

That made it worse.

Not better. Because it meant Marcus could keep going. He could keep finding gaps until Gerald's rating hit zero and the Inspector General shut the dungeon down.

Which would mean Gerald stopped existing.

Not died. Stopped existing.

The System would dissolve his Core and redistribute his dungeon footprint to the nearest adjacent Core.

The Undercroft would get his 4,000 square feet.

Gerald stood up. He walked to the workbench. He picked up the correct sprinkler head.

He measured the pipe stub he had already run along the corridor ceiling. He cut a length of copper pipe with the hacksaw he had spawned from a crafting recipe he had unlocked at Rating 50, back when he still had a Rating of 50.

The pipe was thin and the saw was slow and his hands shook because everything in his dungeon was slowed by debuffs, including his own physical functions.

He worked, and he did not think about Marcus. He thought about pipe threading.

He thought about the proper torque for a 3/4-inch fitting. He thought about the discharge pattern and the ceiling height and the wet-floor liability.

A notification chimed. He ignored it.

Another. Ignored.

Another. He ignored it and kept threading the pipe.

When the sprinkler head was installed, he ran a test. Water came out.

It spread flat against the ceiling and rained down in a fine mist that covered the corridor floor without pooling.

No slip hazard. No standing water. He noted the installation time: forty-seven minutes.

He added it to his schedule and crossed out the line item.

Rating: 5, and the Citations kept coming. He had not installed the emergency lighting yet.

He had not installed the handrail on the east staircase.

He had not put up occupancy signage. Those were all in his schedule, but they were scheduled for hours fourteen, nineteen, and twenty-three. He did not have hours fourteen, nineteen, and twenty-three.

He had a window that was closing, and he needed to cite Marcus back.

He opened his range map again.

He focused on the green dot.

He tried to pull up Marcus's dungeon details. The System returned a line: "No structures registered.

No corridors registered. No rooms registered. Core footprint: 0 square feet."

A zero-square-foot dungeon. Marcus had registered but had not built anything.

He had no corridors to inspect.

No ventilation to check. No egress routes to evaluate. Nothing was citable.

Gerald sat with that.

He mulled it over, as he did with unsolved problems. A dungeon with no structures had no violations.

That was the rule.

You could not cite a thing that did not exist.

But.

Gerald opened his binder to the back. He had photocopied the System's Core Registration Terms.

All forty-seven pages, and he had read them twice.

He had underlined passages. He had written margin notes in his small, cramped handwriting.

He found page nine. Section 3.2: Post-Registration Compliance Window.

"Within six (6) hours of Core Registration, all registered Cores shall establish a minimum functional footprint of not less than 500 square feet, inclusive of at least one (1) corridor with a minimum width of 36 inches and a minimum ceiling height of 6 feet 8 inches. Failure to meet the minimum footprint threshold within the compliance window shall constitute a Class 1 Registration Deficiency."

Gerald looked at the timestamp on Marcus's registration. It was four hours and eleven minutes ago.

Marcus had one hour and forty-nine minutes before his six-hour compliance window expired. And he had a zero-square-foot footprint. He had built nothing. He had registered and immediately started citing Gerald instead of building his own dungeon.

Gerald checked the citation form. He selected the category: Registration Compliance. He selected the violation: Failure to establish minimum functional footprint within required window. He filled in the evidence: Core footprint 0 square feet, time since registration 4 hours 11 minutes, required window 6 hours, projected deficiency at 6 hours 0 minutes.

The System flagged his submission. "Citation premature. Violation has not yet occurred.

Target has 1 hour 49 minutes remaining in compliance window. Citation cannot be filed for a violation that has not yet occurred."

Gerald stared at the rejection notice.

Of course. You could not cite someone for something they had not done yet. The violation had to be active.

You could not cite a building for a fire that had not started.

But, and he went back to Section 3.2. He read it again. And this time he read the next sentence.

"Cores who have not established a minimum functional footprint within the compliance window shall be assessed a Class 1 Registration Deficiency. Cores who have established a footprint below minimum dimensions within the compliance window shall be assessed a Class 2 Registration Deficiency. Cores who have not initiated construction within the first two (2) hours of registration shall be assessed a Class 3 Registration Deficiency."

Class 3. Initiated construction. Not completed. Initiated.

Gerald checked the timestamp again. Four hours and eleven minutes since registration.

The two-hour window had passed two hours and eleven minutes ago.

Marcus had not initiated construction. He had not placed a wall. Not a corridor.

Not a room. Not a square foot of anything.

Gerald selected the citation form.

Category: Registration Compliance.

Violation: Failure to initiate construction within required window. Evidence: Core footprint 0 square feet at 4 hours 11 minutes post-registration.

Required initiation window: 2 hours. Time since required initiation window expired: 2 hours 11 minutes.

He pressed submit.

The System accepted it.

*Your Citation has been filed against Core: MARCUS HUTCHINS. Citing: failure to initiate construction within required post-registration window. Debuff applied: Core registration status suspended pending remediation. Duration: until remediation completed.*

Gerald exhaled.

The green dot on his range map flickered. It turned grey.

Not red.

Grey. The color the System assigned to a Core whose registration was suspended. A suspended Core could not file Citations.

A suspended Core could not spawn entities, and a suspended Core was, in the System's taxonomy, a filing that had been set aside pending review.

The notification chimes stopped.

Gerald's rating held at 5.

It did not go up. The Citations already filed against him were still active. Their debuffs were still applied.

But no new ones were coming, and he picked up his hacksaw.

He started cutting pipe for the emergency lighting conduit.

He had sixty-seven hours and forty-three minutes to get his rating from 5 to 40. It was possible.

Barely. If he worked without stopping. If he skipped sleep.

If he prioritized the high-value fixes first, and he was halfway through the second conduit run when a new notification appeared. It was not a Citation. It was not a Compliance Audit notice. It was a Hearing summons.

*Core MARCUS HUTCHINS has filed an appeal of your Citation. Hearing scheduled: immediate. You are summoned to attend.*

Gerald set down the hacksaw. He wiped his hands on his shirt. He walked to the center of his main corridor and let the System pull him into the Hearing space.

The grey room. Two podiums. The clipboard with legs.

Marcus was not there. The System rendered Core appellants as a generic figure unless the Core chose to display its identity. Marcus had chosen not to display.

"State your grounds for appeal," the clipboard said.

Gerald waited.

Marcus's voice came from the empty podium, which was not a System-modulated voice. It was a real voice. Young.

Slightly slurred.

"I'm appealing because you're my dad and you're an idiot."

The clipboard paused. "This is not a valid ground for appeal. State a structural or procedural ground."

Marcus was quiet for a moment. Then: "Fine.

Your citation says I failed to initiate construction within two hours.

But the registration terms say 'initiated construction.' I filed a Form 1-A. I filed a Form 1-B. I filed a Form 3-C.

I filed paperwork. Paperwork is construction.

You can't build a building without filing the permits first.

You taught me that.

You literally taught me that at Thanksgiving when you made me help you file the variance request for the Henderson property."

Gerald felt something cold move through him. Not anger. Recognition.

Marcus was using Gerald's own argument against him. Gerald had spent thirty-one years insisting that the paperwork was the work. That the filing was the foundation.

That you could not build until you had filed. And Marcus had listened.

He had not understood the code, but he had understood the principle.

And now he was standing in a Hearing space and using Gerald's own professional philosophy to argue that his Citations constituted construction.

The clipboard turned to Gerald. "Respond."

Gerald opened his mouth, and he closed it. He thought about Form 1-A.

A permit application was not construction.

It was a request for permission to construct. The System's registration terms used the word "initiated." Initiated meant started. Started meant broke ground.

Filed paperwork was not breaking ground.

But Marcus had not broken ground.

Marcus had not placed a block.

And Marcus was arguing that the filing was the breaking of ground. And Marcus was quoting Gerald.

And the System was listening.

"Your Honor," Gerald said, and then stopped. The System's adjudicator was not a judge. It was a clipboard. But he had said "Your Honor" because it was what you said, because it was the correct form of address, because Gerald Hutchins did not improvise.

He tried again. "The registration terms define 'initiate' as the physical placement of the first structural element. Form filings are administrative, not structural. The distinction is in Section 1.4, definitions subsection, page three."

The clipboard turned a page. "Confirmed. Section 1.4. 'Initiate: to place the first physical element of a structure within the registered footprint.' The appeal is denied."

Marcus's voice came again. "Then I'll appeal again."

"You have one appeal per Citation. You have used it."

"Then I'll file a new Citation against you and you'll have to appeal that one."

Gerald looked at the empty podium. He could not see Marcus. He could not see his son. But he could hear him, and the voice was still slightly slurred, and the voice was still twenty-three, and the voice was still the voice that had called twice in three years to ask for money.

"You're suspended," Gerald said. "You can't file Citations while your registration is suspended."

"Then I'll fix the suspension. I'll place one block. One block is enough to un-suspend. And then I'll keep citing you."

The clipboard looked at Gerald. "The appellant's statement is noted.

The appeal is denied.

Your Citation stands. The Hearing is concluded."

The grey room dissolved.

Gerald was back in his corridor. His rating was 5.

His rat was breathing.

His sprinkler was installed. His emergency lighting was not.

A new notification appeared.

*Core MARCUS HUTCHINS has remediated its Registration Deficiency. Construction initiated: 1 structural element placed.

Registration status restored.

New Citation filed against your dungeon.

Citing: absence of required fire extinguisher in main corridor. Debuff applied: All spawned entities -12% health.

Duration: 72 hours.*

Gerald looked at his rating.

3, and he looked at his schedule. He looked at the pipe he had cut but not yet installed. He looked at the sprinkler head he had mounted. He looked at the east staircase that had no handrail.

He picked up the hacksaw.

The next notification came in ninety seconds. Then another. Then another.

Marcus had placed one block. And now he was citing Gerald at the same rate as before, maybe faster, because he had learned the categories. He had learned that the System would accept any citation that matched a real gap; Gerald's dungeon was full of gaps.

Gerald cut pipe. He did not look at the notifications.

He counted them.

Fourteen in the first ten minutes. His rating was dropping faster than he could fix things. The math was simple and the math was bad.

He would hit zero in six hours, and he set down the hacksaw. He opened his binder.

He turned to a fresh page.

He wrote: "Option 1: Fix faster. Not possible. Option 2: Cite Marcus back.

Not possible. He has one block and no other violations.

Option 3:"

He stopped writing. He stared at the page.

Option 3, and he thought about the Inspector General.

The audit was coming. The audit would review his entire dungeon.

It would find every violation. It would shut him down. Unless his rating was above 40 when the audit arrived.

He thought about the Hearing he had won. The corridor designation technicality. He had argued that a room did not legally exist until he filed the form that designated it. The System had accepted that.

He thought about Marcus's one block. One structural element placed. That was the minimum. That was the floor.

And then he thought about something he had read once, in the back of the registration terms, in a footnote so small he had almost missed it. A footnote about adjacent Core footprints. About what happened when two Cores' footprints overlapped. About what happened when a Core expanded its footprint into space that another Core had already claimed.

He looked at his range map. He looked at the grey dot that was Marcus's one block.

It was small, which was close. It was inside Gerald's operational range.

And it was inside Gerald's dungeon footprint.

Marcus had placed his one block inside Gerald's 4,000 square feet, and he had registered a Core and placed his first structural element inside another Core's dungeon. That was not a violation Gerald could cite. That was a violation the Inspector General would cite.

Gerald took his pen and started a new document. Not a Citation.

A complaint.

A formal complaint to the Inspector General, requesting an expedited audit of both Cores, on the grounds of footprint encroachment.

If the Inspector General came early, it would review both dungeons. Marcus's dungeon would fail immediately.

One block.

Zero corridors.

Zero ventilation. Rating: single digits.

The Inspector General would shut Marcus down.

But Gerald's dungeon would also be reviewed. And Gerald's rating was 3.

Gerald's hand shook. He wrote the complaint anyway. He wrote it in the careful, cramped handwriting of a man who had filled out Form 11-B four thousand times and had never once been asked to fight a dragon.

He pressed submit.

The System accepted it.

*Complaint received. Expedited audit scheduled: 4 hours 30 minutes.*

Gerald looked at his rating. 3, and he looked at his schedule; he had four and a half hours to get from 3 to 40.

He picked up the hacksaw.
