Chapter 158: Rebuild the World

# Chapter 158

The sledgehammer arc was a perfect semicircle, heavy with the promise of immediate structural failure. The woman in the hard hat did not swing it like a barbarian; she swung it like a demolition expert who had already calculated the load-bearing capacity of Gerald's ribcage.

She expected the bone to give. She expected the Core to flinch.

Gerald did not flinch. He did not even blink. He simply tilted his head three degrees to the left, a motion so precise and bureaucratic that the hammer's handle passed through the space where his skull had been a microsecond before. The impact struck the concrete floor behind him, sending a spiderweb of cracks racing toward the fluorescent lights.

Dust plumed. The smell of ozone and pulverized cement filled the air.

The woman paused, breathing hard. Her eyes, magnified by safety goggles, widened behind the glass.

She looked at the crater she had made, then at Gerald.

He stood amidst the debris, his suit jacket rumpled, holding the white letter in one hand and his clipboard in the other. He looked less like a dungeon core and more like a middle manager who had been inconvenienced by a late bus.

"Section 4.2, Subsection C," Gerald said. His voice was dry, lacking the echo that usually accompanied magical combat. "Unpermitted use of impact tools within a residential zone. That is a Class B infraction."

The woman lowered the sledgehammer. Her knuckles were white.

"You're not a boss," she said. "You're not even a monster."

"I am an inspector," Gerald replied. "And you are obstructing a public right-of-way."

He stepped over the rubble.

The floor of the neighbor's dungeon was pristine, tiled in white ceramic that gleamed under the harsh lights. It was clean.

Too clean.

The kind of clean that hid things. The kind of clean that required a permit for daily maintenance that no one filed.

Gerald looked at the walls. No handrails.

No ADA-compliant ramps, and the electrical outlets were ungrounded and spaced too far apart for standard equipment.

It was a sterile nightmare, a dungeon built for speed and lethality, not for safety.

"The blood," Gerald said, pointing at the dark stain near the baseboard. "It is not leaking from the pipes. It is leaking from the foundation."

The woman's jaw tightened.

"It's a feature."

"It is a liability," Gerald corrected. "And it is a violation of the Structural Integrity Act."

He raised his clipboard. A new window popped into his vision, glowing with the amber hue of a municipal warning.

**CITATION DRAFTED: NEIGHBORING CORE (ID: 9942-S)** **VIOLATION: Structural Compromise / Biohazard Misclassification** **PENALTY: Immediate Drain of 50% Mana Reserves** **APPEAL WINDOW: CLOSED (Flagrant Negligence)**

Gerald didn't wait for her to speak. He didn't wait for her to attack. He simply tapped the screen.

The air in the room shuddered. The fluorescent lights flickered, buzzing at a frequency that made teeth ache. The woman stumbled back, clutching her chest as if the air itself had turned to lead. Her sledgehammer clattered to the floor, heavy and useless.

"What did you do?" she gasped.

"I cited you," Gerald said. "For negligence."

She tried to summon a spell, a burst of fire or lightning, but the mana channels in her dungeon had been throttled. The System, the great bureaucratic engine that ran their world, had obeyed Gerald's command. The blood on the floor receded, drawn back into the cracks like a tide going out, leaving the tiles pale and spotless.

The dungeon's defense mechanisms were offline. The traps, the monsters, the guardians—all of them were paralyzed by the sheer weight of the legal precedent Gerald had established.

Gerald walked past her. He walked to the center of the room, where a single door stood. It was steel, painted green. The paint was chipped.

He ran his hand along the frame. Cold. Metal.

"Who sent you?" he asked, not turning around.

"The Inspector General," she said, her voice trembling. "She doesn't like irregularities."

"Irregularities?" Gerald turned.

"A dungeon that floods with blood is not an irregularity. It is a health code violation of the highest order. And this..." He gestured to the ceiling.

"No fire suppression system.

No emergency lighting. In a fire, everyone in this room dies.

That is not a dungeon.

That is a death trap with a mana bar.

"

The woman slumped against the wall.

"You're killing us."

"I am regulating you," Gerald said. "There is a difference."

He pushed open the green door.

Beyond it lay a corridor that stretched into darkness. The air grew colder, thicker.

The smell of copper returned, sharper now, mixed with something ancient and rotting.

Gerald's HUD flickered, warning him of low visibility, but he ignored it.

He had done this inspection a thousand times. He knew how to navigate a dark basement without tripping over a loose cable.

He knew how to avoid the wet spots. He knew how to ignore the things that scuttled in the periphery until they stepped into the light.

He walked down the corridor. The floor was slick.

His shoes slid, but he corrected his stance, shifting his weight to the balls of his feet.

A habit. Thirty-one years of walking on wet concrete had made his balance perfect.

Ahead, the corridor branched. Left was dark. Right was lit by a single, flickering bulb. Gerald chose left.

A shape detached itself from the shadows. It was tall, gaunt, wrapped in bandages that had long since turned gray. It held a scythe that looked less like a weapon and more like a tool for harvesting wheat.

"Gerald Hutchins," the figure rasped. The voice sounded like dry leaves skittering on pavement. "The Auditor."

Gerald stopped, and he adjusted his glasses.

"I am a Building Inspector.

The term 'Auditor' implies a financial review.

I am conducting a safety review.

"

The figure laughed, a dry, rattling sound.

"You think rules save you?

The System is old, Gerald, which is tired.

It does not care about code. It cares about balance.

"

"Balance is a code," Gerald said. "Section 1.1: Equilibrium of Forces. If you overload one side, the structure collapses.

You have overloaded this dungeon with blood magic. It is structurally unsound.

It will fail.

And when it fails, it will take the city block with it.

"

The figure stepped closer. The scythe swung in a lazy arc.

"Come and try to cite me."

Gerald raised his hand.

But he did not issue a citation. Not yet.

Instead, he pulled a small, metallic object from his pocket, which was a tape measure. A standard, five-meter retractable tape measure.

He clicked the release. The tape snapped out, hitting the wall with a soft *thwack*.

"Door width," Gerald muttered. "Eighty centimeters. Minimum requirement for egress is ninety. You are out of compliance."

The figure paused, confused.

"What?"

"Your exit is too narrow," Gerald said. "In the event of a fire, or a collapse, or a magical backlash, you cannot evacuate.

People will die.

Not because of your magic.

Because of your architecture.

"

The figure looked at the door. Then it looked at Gerald. For the first time, doubt flickered in its hollow eyes.

"You... you measure?"

"I inspect," Gerald corrected. "And you are failing."

He clicked the tape measure back. The sound was loud in the silence. *Click.*

The dungeon groaned. The walls vibrated. The System, sensing the violation, applied the debuff.

The gaunt figure staggered, its form becoming translucent. The scythe fell from its hands, dissolving into dust.

Gerald walked past it. He did not fight it. He did not cast a spell. He simply walked past a violation that the System had already condemned.

He reached the end of the corridor.

There was a room. Small. Circular.

In the center sat a throne made of bone and wire.

On the throne sat a small, withered creature that looked like a monkey wearing a suit. It held a ledger.

The monkey looked up. Its eyes were black pits.

"You are late," the monkey squeaked.

"I am on schedule," Gerald said.

"According to the calendar, it is 4:12 PM. The audit was scheduled for 4:00 PM. I am twelve minutes late.

This is your fault. The hallway was poorly lit, causing me to slow my pace for safety.

"

The monkey slammed its paw on the ledger.

"You are disrupting the Cycle!

The Inspector General sent us to test you. To see if you would break.

To see if you would become a tyrant.

"

"I am not a tyrant," Gerald said. "I am a bureaucrat. Tyrants have egos. Bureaucrats have forms."

He walked to the throne. He did not attack the monkey. He attacked the throne.

He ran his hand along the armrest. It was covered in sharp, jagged spikes.

Decorative.

Painful. Unsafe.

"Sharp edges within four feet of a seating area," Gerald noted. "Prohibited by the Accessibility Standards."

He pulled a small red pen from his pocket. He marked the throne.

The System registered the mark. A new window appeared.

**VIOLATION: Unsafe Furnishing** **CORRECTION ORDER: Remove spikes or pad immediately.** **PENALTY: Core Integrity -10%**

The monkey screamed.

It tried to leap from the throne, but the spikes dug into its fur, pinning it. It thrashed, but Gerald's citation held it in place.

The System enforced the correction.

The spikes retracted, smoothing into the bone, but the process was painful. The monkey shrieked, its mana draining as the dungeon's resources were diverted to fix the violation.

Gerald watched. His face was blank. His hand rested on his clipboard.

"This is not cruelty," he said to the thrashing creature. "This is compliance."

The monkey's eyes rolled back. It went limp. The throne was now smooth, safe, and boring.

Gerald turned away. He walked to the back of the room.

There was a small door there.

A hatch, and he opened it.

Inside was a space no larger than a closet. And in the closet, hanging from a hook, was a single, black book.

The Book of Codes.

But it was not the municipal code. It was older. Bound in skin that looked suspiciously like leather. The pages were yellowed, brittle.

Gerald reached in. He touched the spine.

The moment his fingers made contact, the world went white.

Not the white of light. The white of erasure.

He was pulled out of his body. Out of the dungeon. Into a void of pure, gray static.

And there, standing before him, was not the Inspector General. Not the woman with the sledgehammer. Not the monkey.

It was a man in a suit. He looked exactly like Gerald. Same age.

Same tired eyes. Same rumpled jacket.

But his tie was red.

"Hello, Gerald," the man said.

Gerald looked at his hands. They were still holding the book. But the book was gone. He was holding nothing.

"Who are you?" Gerald asked.

"I am the first one," the man said. "The first Inspector."

Gerald felt a chill that had nothing to do with temperature.

"There is no first one. The System is eternal."

"The System is a construct," the man said. "Built by someone who needed order. Who needed control.

I was that person.

For a time."

He stepped closer. His face was identical to Gerald's, but the expression was different. It was hungry. Desperate.

"You think you are shutting down dungeons," the man said.

"You are not. You are feeding them. Every citation you issue, every debuff you apply, it sends data back to the Source.

It teaches the System how to punish. How to break spirits.

You are not a hero, Gerald.

You are the hammer.

"

Gerald looked at the void. He looked at the red tie.

"I have a job to do," Gerald said.

"You have a trap to spring," the man corrected. "The Inspector General is not coming to audit you.

She is coming to *replace* you. Your Compliance Rating is too high. You are too efficient.

You are becoming the bottleneck.

"

The man smiled. It was a terrible smile. It was the smile of a man who had forgotten how to feel anything but duty.

"Look at your hands, Gerald."

Gerald looked down.

His fingers were turning gray. Flaking. Like dust.

Like mold.

"The mold was not dead," the man said. "It was waiting."

The gray spread. Up his wrist. Up his arm.

"You have one minute," the man said. "One minute to choose.

Keep inspecting, and you vanish.

Become part of the code. Forever. Or stop.

Let the chaos reign. Let the blood flow.

Let the dungeons burn.

"

Gerald stared at his dissolving hand. He felt no pain. He felt only the weight of the clipboard. He could still feel it.

If he let go, he would survive, and he would be free. He could walk away from this absurd, magical bureaucracy.

He could go home. He could drink tea. He could forget the screams.

But he was a building inspector, and he did not abandon a site.

He did not leave a hazard unchecked.

He looked at the man.

"I have not finished the inspection."

The man's smile faltered.

Gerald reached into his pocket. His hand was gray, crumbling, but he still had a pen. The red pen.

He pulled it out. He uncapped it.

He wrote on the air.

On the nothingness, and he wrote: **CITATION: Existential Threat to Municipal Integrity.**

The void cracked.

The man screamed. Not in pain, but in fury.

"You cannot cite *me*!" he shrieked. "I am the Source!"

"You are a structure," Gerald said. "And you are out of code."

The pen struck the air. The ink bloomed black, spreading like a stain. The void collapsed, folding in on itself. The man in the red tie lunged at him, but he was already fading, dissolving into the gray static.

Gerald fell, and he fell through the floor of the void, back into the closet, back into the dungeon, back into his body.

He hit the floor hard. The air rushed back into his lungs. The smell of blood and rot returned.

He gasped, coughing. He sat up.

He was in the small room. The monkey was gone. The throne was empty. The black book was gone.

But on the floor, where he had been standing, was a hole.

Not a hole in the floor. A hole in the *world*.

Through it, he saw the city of Ashford. But it was not the city he knew.

The buildings were twisted, melting, and the sky was the color of a bruise. And in the distance, rising from the library, was a tower of black glass that pierced the clouds.

A new notification flashed in his vision. Not amber. Not red.

**WHITE.**

**SYSTEM ALERT: CORE INTEGRITY FAILED.** **NEW STATUS: ARCHITECT.** **NEW OBJECTIVE: REBUILD THE WORLD.**

Gerald looked at his hands. They were whole. Pink. Solid.

He looked at the hole. He looked at the twisted city.

He picked up his clipboard. The paper was blank.

He uncapped his pen, and he wrote one word.

*Demolition.*

The hole widened.
