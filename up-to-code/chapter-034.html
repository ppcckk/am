Chapter 34: Self-Certification Petition

# Chapter 34

Gerald was on page thirty-one when he found the waste management section.

*Permit Application, Section 19: Biological Entity Waste Disposal Plan. Applicant must demonstrate adequate provision for organic waste generated by spawned entities within the dungeon footprint. Failure to provide a compliant disposal plan constitutes a Category 4 Violation (Environmental Hazard).*

He set his pen down. Looked at the three mold spores, still clinging to the eastern wall in a cluster roughly the size of a dinner plate.

Looked at the water leak, which he had patched with a System-generated sealant that the application had not mentioned was itself a Category 2 Chemical Hazard.

Looked at the rat. The rat was gone. It had been gone for six chapters, he thought, though he wasn't sure how to count chapters.

The rat had left through a gap in the handrail he'd since sealed.

He had no waste disposal plan, and he had no waste. His dungeon generated no waste; it consisted of four thousand square feet of mostly empty concrete and a few organisms producing negligible organic matter.

But the form did not ask whether waste existed.

The form asked for a plan. A plan for waste that did not exist.

Gerald understood this. Every municipal form he had ever filled out asked for plans for contingencies that might never occur.

Fire evacuation plans for buildings with no fire.

Flood mitigation plans for buildings in dry soil, and the form did not care about reality. The form cared about documentation.

He began writing.

The pen moved in the small, precise hand of a man who had filled out ten thousand forms and would fill out ten thousand more before his hands gave out. He described a waste management protocol for biological entities of negligible mass, and referenced his ventilation system, which could theoretically disperse airborne particulates below a certain threshold. He cited the mold spores by their System-registered spawn IDs, because the form required identification of all biological entities and their waste output projections.

He was projecting waste output for mold. He was a building inspector projecting waste output for mold.

Page thirty-two asked for an adventurer traffic flow analysis. Page thirty-three asked for a structural load assessment of the dungeon ceiling, which was also the library's floor. Page thirty-four asked him to list all adjacent property owners within a fifty-foot radius of the dungeon footprint.

Gerald stopped, and he knew the library's footprint. He had inspected the library himself, twice in the past year, because the library's board of directors kept filing complaints about the sub-basement. He knew the library's foundation extended to the north wall of the sub-basement and the south wall was the library's exterior.

He knew the east wall abutted the municipal parking garage, which was city-owned, and knew the west wall abutted the old storm sewer main, which was also city-owned.

Fifty-foot radius.

The parking garage was within fifty feet. The storm sewer main was within fifty feet. Both were city-owned, so he would list the City of Ashford as the adjacent owner.

That was straightforward.

But the form had a subsection.

*Per System Code 9.7.3(b): Adjacent property owners who hold registered Dungeon Core status must be listed separately and notified of all permit applications within their notification radius. Notification is automatic upon form submission. Objection window: twenty-four (24) hours.*

Gerald read it again. Then he pulled up his dungeon's footprint on the System interface.

He had mapped it himself, with his tape measure and a pencil, because the System's auto-generated maps were vague and he did not trust them.

His dungeon's western boundary was flush against the storm sewer main. The main was approximately eight feet in diameter; the sewer core, if one existed, would be registered against the main's infrastructure.

He checked the System's Core Registry. He had access to it because his Compliance Rating had crossed 60, which unlocked the public registry as a feature. He had not used it much. He had used it to look up the cores he had already cited, to verify their ratings before issuing Citations.

He searched for registered cores within the storm sewer main's coordinates.

One result.

*Registered Core: DRENCH. Status: Active.

Registered: 214 years ago.

Compliance Rating: 91.

Permit of Record: On file, Section 4, Subsection 12 (Subterranean Infrastructure Use).*

Two hundred and fourteen years. Gerald stared at the number. He had inspected buildings for thirty-one years, and none of them had ever been a defendant.

This core had been registered before the city of Ashford had a sewer system. Before the library had been built.

Before the parking garage.

Before the storm main had been laid.

DRENCH had a Permit of Record. DRENCH had been operating for two centuries under a permit that predated every structure above it.

Gerald looked at his own dungeon's western wall.

Eight feet of concrete separated his dungeon from DRENCH's territory, and the form asked about adjacent property owners within fifty feet.

Not eight feet.

Fifty feet, and he was within fifty feet of a two-hundred-year-old permitted dungeon.

He was obligated to name it.

He was obligated to notify it.

And once notified, DRENCH had twenty-four hours to object. An objection would trigger a boundary dispute Hearing.

Gerald had won Hearings before, and he had won them by proving structural non-compliance.

But DRENCH had a Compliance Rating of 91. DRENCH had a Permit of Record.

DRENCH had been operating for two hundred and fourteen years without a violation on file.

Gerald could not cite DRENCH for anything. He had looked.

He had checked the public registry's violation history.

Zero entries. Zero citations. Zero hearings.

Zero anything.

DRENCH was, by every metric the System tracked, a perfect dungeon.

Gerald sat back. His lower ached. The concrete floor had been cold for six hours. His knees ached in a manner that was not quite pain, but close.

He looked at the form.

Page thirty-four. The adjacent property owner section had two fields. One for the parking garage.

One for the storm sewer main, and he filled in the parking garage.

City of Ashford, Municipal Facilities Division.

He filled in the storm sewer main. City of Ashford, Public Works.

Then he scrolled down.

A third field existed. It was labeled *Additional Adjacent Core Owners (if applicable)*.

He could leave it blank. He could argue that the storm sewer main was city infrastructure and the core registered against it was not a separate property owner but a use of city infrastructure.

This was technically true, and the core was registered against the main, not against a parcel of land. It was infrastructure use, not property ownership.

But the form said *if applicable*. And he had found a registered core within the radius, and the form's language was clear: *must be listed separately and notified.*

He could leave it blank.

If the System caught him, it would be a filing error. A Category 1 Violation.

Minor.

A fine, maybe. His Compliance Rating would drop a few points.

If he filled it in, DRENCH would know he existed. DRENCH would know he was applying for a permit. DRENCH would have twenty-four hours to respond.

Gerald thought about what DRENCH would do. He thought about what any core would do when a new core appeared within fifty feet.

He thought about the goblin scout from the first weeks, and he thought about the mid-tier core he had cited in Chapter 19.

He thought about the three elite spawns that had died when their dungeon's fire exits failed during a Hearing-enforced debuff.

He filled in the field.

*DRENCH. Registered Core. Storm Sewer Main, Coordinates 44.712, -73.564.*

He scrolled to the bottom of the form. Page forty-seven. The signature line.

He signed. He hit submit.

The interface chimed.

*Permit Application Submitted.

Processing Time: 72 hours. Adjacent Core Notification: Sent to DRENCH. Objection Window: 24 hours.

Current Time Remaining: 24:00:00.*

Gerald watched the timer start counting down. Twenty-three fifty-nine. Twenty-three fifty-eight.

He waited.

The timer reached twenty-three fifty-seven. Twenty-three fifty-six.

Nothing happened.

The interface stayed quiet. Gerald watched it. He had expected an immediate response.

Cores were fast. The System processed their actions in real time.

If DRENCH was going to object, the objection would come quickly.

Twenty-three fifty. Twenty-three forty-five.

Nothing.

Gerald exhaled. Maybe DRENCH was dormant. Maybe it had been asleep for years. Maybe the notification had gone to an empty inbox.

Twenty-three thirty. Twenty-three twenty-five.

His interface erupted.

*NOTIFICATION: Objection Filed. DRENCH has filed a Boundary Dispute Objection against Permit Application #4471-A.*

*NOTIFICATION: Counter-Application Filed. DRENCH has filed a Encroachment Claim against Core #7714 (Gerald Hutchins), alleging that the applicant's dungeon footprint overlaps with permitted infrastructure registered under DRENCH's Permit of Record.*

*NOTIFICATION: Conditional Use Reclassification. Per System Code 9.7.3(f): Where a Conditional Use structure overlaps with a Permitted Structure, the Conditional Use structure is classified as Encroachment. Encroachment structures are subject to Immediate Demolition without Hearing or appeal. Owner must cease all construction activity within one (1) hour.*

Gerald read it four times.

His dungeon was not conditional anymore, which was an encroachment. An encroachment on a permitted structure.

And encroachments did not get Hearings. They did not get appeals. They got demolished.

One hour, and he had one hour to stop his own dungeon from being torn apart by the System.

He looked at the interface. One hour. Fifty-nine minutes remaining.

He looked at the counter-application.

DRENCH's claim was specific. It stated that Gerald's western wall encroached on DRENCH's permitted territory by three feet. Three feet.

Gerald had measured that wall himself, and he had measured it to the millimeter. His dungeon's western boundary was flush against the storm sewer main.

It did not overlap.

It could not overlap, because the storm sewer main was a physical structure and Gerald's concrete wall was on the other side of it.

Unless the storm sewer main was not where Gerald thought it was.

He pulled up his original measurements. He had measured from the library's foundation, which he knew to be accurate because he had inspected it himself. He had measured the sub-basement's dimensions. He had mapped his dungeon against those measurements.

He looked at DRENCH's permit coordinates. He looked at his own coordinates.

They overlapped by three feet.

Not because Gerald had measured wrong. Because the storm sewer main had been relocated.

The main had been moved three feet east during a municipal infrastructure project in 1987.

Gerald knew this because he had inspected the project, and he had signed off on it. He remembered the blueprints.

But the System's coordinates had not been updated. DRENCH's permit was registered against the original location of the main. The main had moved.

DRENCH's permitted territory had moved with it, because the permit was registered against the infrastructure, not the coordinates. The System updated infrastructure permits automatically.

DRENCH's territory had shifted three feet east when the main was relocated.

Gerald's dungeon was inside DRENCH's permitted territory. Not because Gerald had encroached. Because the ground had moved under him.

Fifty-six minutes remaining.

Gerald opened the permit application again. He scrolled to Section 14. Licensed Contractor.

He had left it blank because he could not find a way to fill it.

He had not had time. He had been on page thirty-four when he found DRENCH.

Now he looked at Section 14 with new eyes.

*Per System Code 9.7.3(d): A Licensed Contractor is any entity that has performed direct construction activity within the structure and holds a valid System-issued Contractor's License. The Owner may not serve as Licensed Contractor; the Owner must designate a Licensed Contractor who is distinct from the Owner.*

Distinct from the Owner.

Not a different person. A different entity. Gerald was the Owner.

His dungeon was the structure. The contractor had to be something else.

He looked at his spawn list. Three mold spores. A water leak. The rat was gone.

He looked at the water leak, which was a System-generated sealant.

It was not a spawn. It was infrastructure.

It could not be a contractor.

He looked at the mold spores. They were biological entities. They had not constructed anything. They had not performed construction activity.

He had no contractor. He had forty-seven minutes. He had no way to fill in Section 14.

Unless, and he scrolled to the bottom of the form again. Past the signature line. An addendum followed. A line of text in a smaller font than the rest of the form.

*Per System Code 9.7.3(d)(iii): In the event that no Licensed Contractor can be designated, the Owner may petition for Emergency Self-Certification. Emergency Self-Certification requires the Owner to demonstrate that the structure was built in compliance with all applicable codes and that no Licensed Contractor was available at the time of construction. Approval is at the discretion of the Inspector General.*

The Inspector General. The entity that audited all cores. The entity that Gerald had been avoiding since his Compliance Rating crossed 60. The entity that had not yet audited him but would, eventually, because all cores were audited.

Gerald looked at the addendum. He looked at the timer. Forty-six minutes.

He looked at his dungeon. Handrails to code.

Egress signage compliant.

Fire suppression operational. Ventilation adequate. Compliance Rating 74 and climbing.

He had built everything to code, and had never filed a permit because he had not known he needed to. No contractor had been available because there had never been a contractor. He had done it himself, alone, with a tape measure and a pencil and thirty-one years of knowing exactly what the rules required.

He opened the Emergency Self-Certification petition. It was three pages long. He began writing.
