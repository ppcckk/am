Chapter 75: Self-Evident Citation

# Chapter 75

Twenty seconds.

Gerald's mouth moved before his brain caught up. He had spent thirty-one years standing in doorways with a tape measure, and his body knew how to deliver a finding before his mind could second-guess it.

"Section 11.3.2, Subsection C. Constructive Egress Failure.

A pathway that presents to a reasonable occupant as an exit route constitutes a life safety violation regardless of whether a physical barrier blocks traversal.

Core 4471's eastern corridor terminates in a sealed alcove at the point where a reasonable occupant would expect to reach grade level. The violation is self-evident from the structure. No inspection required.

No prior citation required.

The condition exists by virtue of the build."

The Inspector General's pen moved across its clipboard. Gerald could not read what it wrote.

Core 4471's presence in the grey space pulsed. It had spent its thirty seconds. Gerald had spent twenty-three.

The Inspector General looked at both of them. Its face, if it had one, was the face of a man who had read every page of a codebook and found nothing interesting on any of them.

"Finding for Core 2891 on the consolidated matter.

The Constructive Egress Failure is upheld. Core 4471's appeal is denied. The original Citation stands.

The secondary Citation issued during the appeal also stands, as it was served by a Core with valid standing at the time of service."

Core 4471's presence contracted. In Gerald's awareness, he felt the distant network of that Core's spawns begin to degrade. Three corridors in its dungeon lost structural integrity simultaneously, and a spawn room that had been holding six elite entities went dark.

The Inspector General did not pause. Its pen kept moving.

"However. This adjudication establishes Code Precedent under System Rule 9.4.1. The finding of Constructive Egress Failure is now binding on all registered Cores within this jurisdiction. All Cores operating under this jurisdiction are hereby subject to audit under this precedent within one hundred and sixty-eight hours of this ruling."

Gerald felt the words land like a hand on his shoulder.

One hundred and sixty-eight hours. Seven days. Every dungeon in the region. Including his.

The Inspector General turned its attention back to Gerald.

Its clipboard was enormous.

Gerald's dungeon was four thousand square feet. The clipboard was larger than that.

Gerald had not known that was physically possible, but the grey space was not physical, and the clipboard was not a clipboard, and the Inspector General was not a man.

"Core 2891. You are now subject to audit under the precedent you established. I will conduct the audit now, as a courtesy, since you are present."

Gerald's pen stopped moving.

"Wait," he said. "I have a Corrective Order with eleven hours remaining. I have not completed my ventilation remediation. Under Section 3.1.4, a Core under active Corrective Order is exempt from concurrent audit until the Order is resolved or expired."

The Inspector General's pen paused. It looked at Gerald. The look was not hostile. It was the look of a man checking whether the other man had read the section correctly.

"Section 3.1.4. Exemption applies to audits initiated by the Core's own Compliance deficiency. It does not apply to audits initiated by Code Precedent established in a separate adjudication. You are not exempt."

Gerald opened his mouth. Closed it.

He knew the section, and he had read it four times during the Corrective Order process. He had read it in the context of his own violations, and had not read it in the context of someone else's ruling.

That was a reading failure. That was his fault.

"Proceed," Gerald said.

The Inspector General's pen touched the air between them.

The grey space peeled open like a page. Gerald's dungeon was visible from above.

All four thousand square feet of it, and the corridors he had measured to the inch. The handrails he had installed at thirty-four-inch height. The egress signage he had printed on the System's formatting template.

The pen traced a line through his dungeon. It stopped at the north corridor.

Gerald looked. The north corridor ran from the main entrance hall to a junction where it met the east corridor at a ninety-degree angle.

He had built it that way because the east corridor was the primary spawn path and he needed a buffer zone between the entrance and the first encounter point.

The junction was a T-intersection.

The north corridor continued past the junction for another twelve feet before terminating in a small alcove he used for storing replacement signage.

The pen circled the alcove.

"North corridor, terminal alcove. The pathway presents as an exit route. A reasonable occupant entering from the main hall would follow the corridor and expect to reach grade level at the terminus.

The terminus is a dead end. Constructive Egress Failure."

Gerald stared at the alcove.

Twelve feet. Storage for replacement egress signage. The irony was not lost on him.

He had stored egress signage in a space that violated egress code.

He felt the debuff land. It was small.

His dungeon's Compliance Rating dropped from 78 to 74.

The ventilation debuff he had been fighting for eleven hours got slightly worse. His water leak spawn, which he had been trying to upgrade into a proper drainage system, lost a point of efficiency.

Not catastrophic. Not fatal.

But the principle was the problem, and the Inspector General had cited him.

Gerald Hutchins had been cited by the System's Inspector General for a violation that existed because of a precedent he himself had created.

"You may appeal," the Inspector General said. "The appeal window is seventy-two hours. The hearing will be conducted in this space.

You will need to argue against the precedent you established. You may not argue the precedent is invalid.

You may only argue that your specific structure does not meet the precedent's criteria."

Gerald looked at his clipboard. He looked at the alcove. He looked at the twelve feet of corridor that led to nowhere.

He had built it wrong. Not by much. Not by a lot. But by the exact amount that made a reasonable person expect an exit and find a wall instead.

Thirty-one years of inspections. He had cited hundreds of buildings for this. He knew the language.

He knew the logic, and he knew that a twelve-foot dead-end corridor attached to a main circulation path was not a storage alcove. It was a trap.

He had built a trap in his own dungeon and put signs in it.

"Thank you," Gerald said. The words came out automatic. Polite. The voice he used when he handed a citation to a contractor who was going to be angry.

The Inspector General nodded. It folded itself into the grey space and was gone.

Gerald sat alone. His Corrective Order clock ticked. Eleven hours became ten hours fifty-three minutes. His Compliance Rating sat at 74, down from 78, and the ventilation debuff pressed against his spawn list like a thumb on a bruise.

He turned to a blank page and wrote the date at the top. He wrote the header.

APPEAL BRIEF. CORE 2891. CONSTRUCTIVE EGRESS FAILURE.

He stared at the header. He had to argue that his corridor was not an exit route. But it looked like one.

He had designed it to look like one, because he had not thought about what it looked like. He had thought about circulation flow and spawn buffer and storage access.

He had not thought about the experience of a reasonable occupant.

A reasonable occupant.

Gerald put his pen down, and he stood up in the grey space, which meant nothing, because he had no body. But the gesture helped.

He walked to where his dungeon's main entrance hall was visible from above; studied it with an adventurer's eye.

You come in. You see the main hall.

To your left, a corridor runs north, which is wide. It is lit. The floor is level.

There is a handrail on the right side, which suggests a grade change ahead, which suggests the corridor goes up, which suggests it goes to grade level.

There is no handrail on the left side. The handrail is only on the right. A reasonable occupant would think the corridor rises on the right and therefore the exit is to the right, which is to say, forward, which is to say, north.

They would walk north. They would pass the junction. They would continue for twelve feet. They would hit a wall.

Gerald felt sick. Not the metaphorical kind. The real kind. The kind that came from knowing you had done something wrong and that the wrongness was obvious and that you had not seen it because you were too busy making sure everything else was right.

He sat back down. He picked up his pen.

He could not argue the corridor was not an exit route. It was.

He had built it to be an exit route and then not made it one, which was the violation. That was exactly the violation.

But he could argue something else.

He wrote: "The Constructive Egress Failure precedent requires that a pathway present as an exit route to a reasonable occupant. Core 2891 submits that the presence of a handrail on the right side of the corridor constitutes an active signal to the occupant that the corridor is not a primary egress path but a secondary circulation route.

Under Section 10.2.1, handrails indicate grade transitions.

A grade transition indicates the corridor does not reach grade level at its terminus.

Therefore the corridor does not present as an exit route. It presents as a circulation route with a grade change."

He stopped. He read it back.

That was clever. That was the kind of argument he would have made as a defense attorney for a contractor who had a dead-end hallway with a handrail. He had made that argument before.

He had lost that argument before, because the inspector who cited the building had pointed out that a handrail does not indicate the absence of an exit. A handrail indicates a change in elevation.

A corridor can have a change in elevation and still be an exit.

He had lost that argument. He remembered losing it. He remembered the inspector's face.

But the Inspector General was not an inspector. The Inspector General was a judge. And a judge was bound by precedent, and the precedent he had established did not define the handrail as an indicator of anything.

The precedent defined the violation as a pathway that "presents to a reasonable occupant as an exit route." The handrail argument was not addressed in the precedent.

It was new.

Gerald wrote it in. He wrote it carefully. He wrote it in the language of someone who had lost this argument once and had spent eleven years thinking about why he had lost it and what he should have said instead.

He looked at the clock. Ten hours forty-one minutes on the Corrective Order. Seventy-one hours and forty-three minutes on the appeal window.

He had time. He had a plan. He had a handrail.

He also had a new problem.

The Inspector General had said "all registered Cores within this jurisdiction." Gerald had been auditing other dungeons for weeks. He had issued fourteen citations. He had a reputation.

Adventurers were starting to come to his dungeon because it was up to code, which meant it was safe, which meant it was boring, which meant it was easy, which meant it was popular.

His dungeon was popular because it was safe.

His dungeon was now cited for a safety violation.

If the appeal failed, his Compliance Rating would drop further. The debuffs would compound.

His spawn list would degrade, and the adventurers who came for the safety would find a dungeon that was less safe.

And if the appeal succeeded, the handrail argument became part of the precedent. That meant every dungeon in the jurisdiction that had a handrail on a dead-end corridor could use Gerald's argument to escape the Constructive Egress Failure finding.

He had just created a loophole, and a loophole that every other Core in the region would find within seven days.

Core 4471 was dead. Three of its elite spawns were gone. Its dungeon was degrading. But Gerald had just handed every other Core in the jurisdiction a defense against the exact citation that had killed them.

Gerald looked at his clipboard. He looked at the handrail argument. He looked at the fourteen citations he had already served.

He had won the hearing. He had saved his own dungeon. He had just given away the war.

His pen hovered over the page. He could add a qualifier.

A limitation.

A clause that narrowed the handrail defense so it only applied when the handrail met specific criteria. He could write it in three minutes.

But he would need to know what criteria; the criteria would need to be specific enough to survive a hearing. And he would need to be sure he was not creating another loophole he could not see.

He thought about his dungeon, and he thought about the handrail. Thirty-four inches.

Right side only.

Level floor.

He thought about Core 4471's eastern corridor. No handrail. Dead end.

He thought about the fourteen citations. He had cited three of them for Constructive Egress Failure.

The other eleven were different categories.

But those three cores would be waiting for the precedent to spread.

They would see the handrail defense within days.

Gerald put his pen down. He opened a new page. He wrote: "Draft Amendment to Code Precedent 9.4.1. Handrail Qualification Clause."

He had seventy-one hours to write it. He had fourteen dungeons that would test it. He had an Inspector General that was watching.

He started writing. He wrote slowly. He wrote carefully. He wrote like a man who had made a mistake and was trying not to make another one, which was exactly how he had written every citation for thirty-one years, and it had never once been enough.

This time the dungeon was reading them.
