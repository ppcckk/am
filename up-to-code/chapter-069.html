Chapter 69: Unauthorized Interaction

# Chapter 69

The pen moved.

Gerald wrote in the first box. Applicant designation. He wrote his Core Registration number. He wrote it the way he wrote it on every form he had ever filled out in thirty-one years of municipal service, which was in block capitals, with a slight leftward lean that his wife had once called "the look."

The pen was warm. The warmth was wrong. It was the warmth of an instrument that did not belong to him, and the System had already logged that as unauthorized interaction, and the penalty was queued, and the ten seconds were not going to wait for his handwriting.

He wrote the second box. Condition proposed.

He paused. The pause was not hesitation.

The pause was the pause of a man who had filled out four thousand variance requests and knew that the condition box was where variance requests died.

A condition that was too vague got rejected. A condition that was too specific got enforced against you. The condition had to be proportional.

It had to be reasonable.

It had to be the kind of condition that a person filing the request could actually meet without breaking something else.

Gerald wrote: Mechanical ventilation system installed to meet Section 4.2(b) of the Dungeon Environmental Code, with a minimum air exchange rate of 0.5 cubic meters per minute per occupied cubic meter.

He wrote the third box. Timeline.

Thirty days, and he wrote the fourth box. Reasonable alternative during the variance period.

He stopped.

This was the box that mattered. This was the box that made the variance legal or illegal. The alternative had to be something that reduced the harm the condition was meant to prevent.

If the condition was about ventilation, the alternative had to be about reducing the need for ventilation.

Gerald wrote: Spawn population reduced to fifty percent of maximum capacity. Occupant density lowered to meet current ventilation baseline.

He underlined "fifty percent." Then he underlined it again. The underlining was not necessary. He did it anyway.

The pen stopped.

Not because he stopped it.

The pen stopped because the ten seconds were over. The violation locked.

The penalty was no longer queued. The penalty was active.

[PROCEDURAL VIOLATION PENALTY APPLIED: ALL ACTIVE CITATIONS VOIDED] [PROCEDURAL VIOLATION PENALTY APPLIED: ALL PASSIVE RESISTANCES VOIDED]

Gerald felt it. That was the problem with passive resistances.

You did not notice them when they were there.

You noticed them when they were gone. It was like a draft you had lived with for so long that you only felt the cold when the window was closed.

His dungeon was exposed.

Every resistance he had built over sixty-eight chapters of careful compliance work was gone. Fire resistance, lightning resistance, physical damage reduction.

All of it.

Gone.

But the form was filed, and the notification came three seconds later.

[FORM 7 RECEIVED: CONDITIONAL VARIANCE REQUEST] [REVIEW STATUS: PENDING] [ACTION: PROCEDURAL VIOLATION PENALTY FROZEN PENDING VARIANCE REVIEW] [REVIEW WINDOW: 48 HOURS]

Gerald set the pen down. He set it down gently, as if the owner were watching.

The Inspector General's audit instrument was still in his dungeon. It had not moved.

It had not spoken, which had not left.

It was a tripod-mounted thing that looked like a surveyor's transit and a theodolite had mated in a dark alley, and it was pointed at Gerald's clipboard, which was pointed at the floor, which was pointed at Gerald's Core, which was pointed at nothing in particular because Gerald was looking at the notification.

He waited.

The IG did not move.

Gerald understood this. The IG was processing. The IG was reading the form.

The IG was checking the three boxes. Condition, timeline, reasonable alternative.

If all three were present and facially valid, the IG could not reject the variance.

Not on the spot. The variance had to go to review.

This was the loophole.

Gerald found it, as he had found every loophole in thirty-one years of municipal work. Not by being clever.

By being thorough.

He read the variance framework in the System's documentation as he read the Ashford Municipal Code: twice, once for comprehension and once for exceptions.

The exception was buried in Section 7.3(c) of the Administrative Procedures appendix, and a variance request, once filed and facially valid, entered a mandatory review period.

During that period, the action being appealed was frozen.

The IG could not enforce.

The IG could not reject. The IG could only accept, reject with cause, or assign a compliance auditor.

Accept. Reject with cause. Assign.

Three options. All of them required the IG to stop what it was doing and make a decision. The IG could not simply wait for the review window to expire, because the review window was not a grace period.

It was a procedural obligation. The IG had to act within forty-eight hours.

Gerald checked his dungeon clock. He had forty-seven hours and fifty-nine minutes.

The audit instrument rotated. Slowly. Like a lighthouse beam made of cold light. It swept across his dungeon and stopped.

[VARANCE REQUEST REVIEWED] [CONDITION: VALID] [TIMELINE: VALID] [REASONABLE ALTERNATIVE: VALID] [DECISION: VARANCE GRANTED] [PROCEDURAL VIOLATION PENALTY: SUSPENDED] [CONDITIONAL COMPLIANCE DEADLINE: 30 DAYS] [FAILURE TO MEET CONDITION: PENALTY DOUBLED AND ONE PASSIVE RESISTANCE PERMANENTLY FORFEITED]

Gerald read the last line twice.

Permanently forfeited.

Not voided. Not suspended. Forfeited.

The System was telling him that if he failed, he would lose a resistance he had earned, and he could not rebuild it.

Not in the same way. Not with the same code section.

The permanent resistances were tied to specific compliance milestones, and once forfeited, the milestone was consumed.

He had earned six resistances. Fire, lightning, physical, environmental, structural, and biological. If he failed, he lost one.

He did not get to choose which one. The System would choose.

Gerald made a note on his clipboard.

He wrote: "Install ventilation system. 30 days.

Budget: unknown. Materials: unknown.

Method: unknown."

He had no idea how to build a ventilation system. He was a dungeon core.

He was a sub-basement, and he did not have hands. He did not have a contractor. He did not have a supply chain.

He had a rat.

The rat was in the northeast corner, near the water leak. The rat was confused. The rat had always been confused. Gerald had been meaning to file a Form 11-B on it for three weeks and had not gotten around to it because the ventilation audit had consumed his attention.

Gerald looked at the rat. The rat looked at the wall.

He reduced his spawn population to fifty percent.

The water leak slowed. The mold spores went from three to one and a half, which was a strange visual but the System handled it.

The rat stayed, because the rat was not a spawn.

The rat was an unlicensed biological entity that had not been properly registered, which meant it was technically outside the population count.

Gerald noted this. He noted it, as he noted everything. He wrote it on the clipboard. He underlined it.

The audit instrument moved again.

[ADDITIONAL ACTION: COMPLIANCE AUDITOR ASSIGNED] [AUDITOR: CORE #4471] [AUDITOR STATUS: IN GOOD STANDING] [AUDITOR WILL CONDUCT ON-SITE VERIFICATION OF CONDITIONAL COMPLIANCE] [AUDITOR ARRIVAL: 72 HOURS]

Gerald stared at the notification.

Core #4471, and he knew that number. He had written that number on a Citation. He had written it on a Citation and he had served it, and the Citation had cited three fire exits that were blocked by decorative bone piles, and the debuff had been applied, and three elite spawns had died in a corridor that was too narrow to evacuate through, and Gerald had felt something about that that he had not examined because examining it would have required him to stop working, and stopping work was not permitted.

Core #4471 had appealed. The appeal had been granted. Gerald had seen the notification three chapters ago and had not processed it because he had been busy installing handrails.

The appeal was granted. Core #4471 was in good standing. And now the Inspector General had assigned Core #4471 as Gerald's compliance auditor.

This was not a coincidence. Gerald did not believe in coincidences.

He believed in procedural patterns.

The IG assigned auditors based on proximity and expertise.

Core #4471 was nearby. Core #4471 had been audited recently, which meant it was in the IG's active file.

Core #4471 had expertise in fire safety, because it had been cited for fire safety violations and had appealed them successfully.

Gerald's condition was ventilation. Ventilation was fire-adjacent. The IG had assigned the auditor with the most relevant expertise.

The auditor with the most relevant expertise was the Core that Gerald had effectively killed three of its children from.

Gerald looked at the clipboard. He looked at the notification. He looked at the audit instrument, which was still there, still pointed at him, still waiting.

He wrote on the clipboard: "Core #4471. Fire safety expertise.

Appeal granted.

Assigned as auditor. Arrival: 72 hours."

He underlined "72 hours."

Then he wrote: "Options."

He wrote one option, and he crossed it out. He wrote another option.

He crossed it out.

He wrote a third option and did not cross it out.

The third option was: "File a Form 7 against the auditor assignment."

He looked at the form. Form 7 was for conditional variance requests.

It was not for challenging personnel assignments, and he could not file a variance against an auditor.

A variance was about conditions and timelines and alternatives. It was not about who showed up.

But Gerald had been a building inspector for thirty-one years, and in thirty-one years he had learned that every form had a back. And the back of Form 7 had a section he had not used. A section that was not a condition, not a timeline, not an alternative. A section that was labeled "Recusal Request."

He had never used it. He had never seen it used. He had read it once, in the documentation, and had dismissed it as a vestigial field, the kind of thing that existed in the form template because the form template was a template and templates had fields that were not always used.

The recusal field read: "If the applicant believes the assigned compliance auditor has a conflict of interest, the applicant may request recusal by providing the following: (1) nature of conflict, (2) evidence of conflict, (3) proposed alternative auditor."

Gerald's hand moved. The pen was warm again. It was still the IG's pen.

The violation was still active. The penalty was still suspended.

The suspension was still conditional, and he wrote the nature of conflict. He wrote: "Auditor was previously cited by applicant for fire safety violations. Auditor's appeal was granted.

Auditor has direct adversarial relationship with applicant.

Auditor's objectivity is compromised."

He wrote the evidence. He wrote: "Citation #2024-0417. Appeal #2024-0417-A. Both logged in System records."

He wrote the proposed alternative, and he stopped.

He did not have a proposed alternative auditor. He did not know any other Core numbers. He cited dozens of Cores but did not memorize their numbers as he memorized code sections. He had been busy.

The pen stopped. Not because the ten seconds were up. Because Gerald stopped it.

He looked at the clipboard, and he looked at the recusal field. It was incomplete. A recusal request without a proposed alternative was facially invalid.

The IG would reject it. The auditor would arrive in seventy-two hours.

Core #4471 would walk into Gerald's dungeon, look around, and find a sub-basement with one and a half mold spores, a slow leak, and a confused rat.

It would find a dungeon that was failing its variance condition. It would find a Core that had no plan, no materials, no method.

It would report the failure. The penalty would double. One resistance would be permanently forfeited.

Gerald set the pen down, and he looked at the rat.

The rat looked at the wall.

Gerald picked up the pen again.

He turned to a fresh page on the clipboard. He wrote: "Ventilation. 30 days.

Materials needed: ductwork, fan unit, intake/exhaust vents, mounting hardware. Source: unknown."

He wrote: "Alternative: reduce occupant density further. Current: 50%. Required for current ventilation: 30%? 20%?"

He did the math. He did it slowly, as he did all math, on the back of a form, with a pencil that was not his. The math said that if he reduced his spawn population to twenty percent, his ventilation requirement would drop below his current baseline. He would meet the condition without installing anything.

But twenty percent spawn capacity meant his dungeon would be empty. Adventurers who entered would find nothing. The adventurer parties that had been coming to his dungeon, attracted by the rumor that it was somehow high-difficulty because everything was so aggressively up to code, would find a sub-basement with a leak and a rat.

They would leave. They would tell other adventurers to leave. His dungeon would become irrelevant.

And Core #4471 would arrive, see a dungeon at twenty percent capacity, and report that the variance condition had been met.

Gerald wrote: "Option A: Build ventilation. 30 days. Unknown materials. Unknown method. High reward, high failure risk."

He wrote: "Option B: Reduce to 20%. Guaranteed compliance.

Dungeon becomes empty.

Adventurers leave. Core #4471 reports compliance. Penalty voided.

Dungeon survives but becomes irrelevant."

He looked at both options. He looked at the recusal field, still incomplete.

He looked at the clock. Seventy-two hours until Core #4471 arrived.

He looked at the rat.

The rat sneezed.

Gerald turned to a fresh page. He wrote: "Form 11-B.

Unlicensed Biological Entity.

Species: Rattus norvegicus. Status: Unregistered. Action: Registration application."

He filed the form.

The System accepted it.

[RAT REGISTERED: SPAWN POPULATION +1]

The rat was now a spawn. The rat was now part of the population count. The rat was now subject to the fifty percent cap.

Gerald had just increased his spawn population by one. He had just made his compliance problem worse.

He looked at the notification. He looked at the rat. The rat looked at the wall.

And then the audit instrument moved, and it pointed at Gerald, and it said something that was not a notification. It said something in a voice that sounded like a filing cabinet opening.

"Your recusal request is incomplete."

Gerald looked up.

"Provide a proposed alternative auditor, or the recusal is void and the assignment stands."

Gerald looked at the clipboard. He looked at the blank space after "proposed alternative auditor."

He did not have a name. He did not have a number.

But he had thirty-one years of municipal knowledge, and he knew that every inspector in Ashford had a backup, and every backup had a number, and every number was in a directory, and the directory was in the System's personnel file, and the personnel file was accessible to any Core that had been audited.

Gerald had been audited. He had the file.

He turned the page.
