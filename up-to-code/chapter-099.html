Chapter 99: Two Inches Deficit

# Chapter 99

The measuring tape snapped back into its housing with a sound like a gunshot. Gerald Hutchins did not flinch. He had spent thirty-one years in municipal basements, and he had learned that sudden noises were usually the result of poor insulation or a neighbor’s unauthorized renovation. He adjusted his glasses, which were no longer physically attached to his face but existed as a permanent sensory overlay provided by the System interface, and looked at the ventilation shaft.

It was a four-inch gap in the ceiling, and the code required six inches for proper airflow in a residential-adjacent sub-basement. A two-inch deficit. A minor violation.

In the old life, a two-inch deficit meant a form. A stamp. A fifteen-minute walk to the planning office.

In the current life, it meant death.

"Time remaining: five hours, forty-two minutes," the System voice droned. It lacked the warmth of a human bureaucrat. It lacked the malicious glee of a demon. It sounded exactly like the automated phone tree at the Department of Motor Vehicles.

Gerald ignored it. He pulled his clipboard from the ether.

The clipboard was his anchor.

Without it, he felt the urge to dissolve back into the floorboards and let the mold take him. With it, he was an inspector.

He walked the perimeter of the library sub-basement. His dungeon was four thousand square feet of concrete, exposed rebar, and damp earth. The water leak in the northeast corner had been patched with a resin sealant he had synthesized using mana gathered from the confused rat, who had been cooperative once Gerald explained the concept of liability.

"Section 4, Sub-section C," Gerald muttered, his voice echoing in the damp air.

"Egress route width. Measured at thirty-two inches.

Required is thirty-six.

Deficiency: four inches. Severity: Moderate."

He marked it. A red checkbox appeared on his mental ledger.

Then he looked at the handrails, and he had installed them himself, using iron rods scavenged from the old boiler room.

He measured the height. Thirty-three inches.

The code said thirty-four.

A cold sweat broke out on his spectral form.

The audit wasn’t just about the dungeon’s current state. It was about the *history*, and the Inspector General wanted to see if he had been cutting corners from the start. And Gerald, who had never once cut a corner in his professional life, was terrified that the System would find the two inches of missing width in the ventilation shaft and revoke his core status for negligence.

He stopped. He looked at the ventilation shaft.

He could fix it. He could spend the mana he had saved up, expand the duct, and bring it up to code.

But the audit required the file to be submitted in six hours.

If he fixed it now, the record would show a violation that had existed for the last three months. The penalty for past violations was cumulative.

Gerald sat down on the damp concrete. He put his head in his hands.

"There is no way to retroactively fix a measurement error without falsifying the initial survey," he said. "And falsifying a survey is a Class A felony."

The rat squeaked. It was sitting on a stack of old library books that served as the dungeon’s 'reading nook,' a feature Gerald had added to improve the 'psychological comfort' rating of his tenants.

"I cannot lie to the Inspector," Gerald said. "But I can contextualize."

He stood up, and he walked to the center of the room; the air was thick with the smell of wet earth and ozone.

The System’s audit protocol was a scan.

It would read every inch of his dungeon, every spawn, every wall, every violation he had filed against others, and every violation he had filed against himself.

He had filed a self-citation for unauthorized entry into Kessick’s dungeon. That was in the file.

It was a minor violation, but it was there.

It proved he was honest.

It also proved he had been out of bounds.

"Context," he repeated.

He opened his interface. He navigated to the section labeled *External Interactions*. Seventeen entries existed.

Twelve citations against other dungeon cores. Four hearings won.

One loss.

And one self-citation, and he stared at the self-citation. *Unauthorized entry into adjacent dungeon corridor without Temporary Access Authorization.*

If the Inspector saw this, he would see that Gerald was a rogue inspector.

A vigilante. In any other context, this was a fireable offense.

But Gerald was not an employee, and he was a Core. And Cores had jurisdiction over their own boundaries.

Unless they violated the Treaty of Contiguous Borders.

Gerald frowned. He hadn’t read the Treaty.

He had only read the Building Code, and he opened his library.

The Treaty of Contiguous Borders was a PDF, because the System, in its infinite bureaucratic cruelty, had formatted all inter-core treaties as scanned documents from the 1970s.

He scrolled through the pages. His eyes skipped over the flowery language about "eternal enmity" and "blood oaths." He looked for the definitions.

*Definition 4b: Temporary Access Authorization (TAA). A TAA is granted upon mutual consent or in cases of imminent structural collapse threatening adjacent properties.*

Gerald froze.

Imminent structural collapse, and he looked at the wall shared between his dungeon and Kessick’s; it was a thick concrete barrier, but it was old. The rebar was rusting.

He had noted this in his initial survey. He had filed a report. *Shared wall integrity: 60%.

Recommendation: Reinforce.*

He had not reinforced it. He had cited Kessick for "negligent maintenance of shared load-bearing structures."

Kessick had ignored the citation.

Gerald looked at the date of his self-citation, and he had entered Kessick’s dungeon because he had heard a cracking sound. He had entered to verify if the structure was failing.

He opened the self-citation form. He deleted the line: *Violation: Unauthorized entry.*

He typed a new line: *Violation: None. Action: Emergency Structural Assessment under Exemption 4b.*

He hit Save.

The System flashed red. *ERROR. EXEMPTION NOT PRE-FILED.*

Gerald felt a physical lurch in his stomach. The System did not accept retroactive justifications. You had to have the paperwork before you did the work.

That was the rule, which was the law.

He looked at the clock. Five hours, fifteen minutes.

He couldn’t change the self-citation. It was locked.

He had to change the context of the audit itself, and he looked around his dungeon.

Three mold spores. One rat. A water leak.

Handrails. A fire extinguisher.

He looked at the spawn list. The mold spores were Level 1. The rat was Level 2. The water leak was a hazard, not a spawn.

The Compliance Rating was 12. That was abysmal. It was a death sentence.

But the audit wasn’t about the rating, which was about the *file*.

The Inspector wanted to see the compliance history. If the history showed a pattern of negligence, he was revoked.

If the history showed a pattern of obsessive adherence to code, even to his own detriment, he might be spared. Or worse, promoted.

Gerald picked up his pace. He checked the fire extinguisher.

It was expired, and he had renewed it last week, but the label was peeling; he peeled it off completely.

He checked the egress signage.

It was hand-painted. The System preferred digital signage.

He hated digital signage, which was flashy. It was loud.

He went to the northeast corner. The water leak.

He had patched it with resin.

The patch was holding, but it was messy.

It looked like a scar, and he scraped it off.

He watched the water begin to drip again. *Drip. Drip. Drip.*

He took his measuring tape. He measured the distance from the leak to the nearest electrical outlet. Two feet. The code required six feet.

He measured the distance from the leak to the handrail. One foot. The code required two feet.

He opened his clipboard. He filed a new citation against himself. *Violation: Active water leak creating slip hazard and electrical risk. Severity: Major.*

Then he filed another. *Violation: Electrical outlet within proximity of water source. Severity: Major.*

Then another. *Violation: Handrail obstruction by water accumulation. Severity: Minor.*

He kept filing. He filled page after page with his own failures, and documented every crack, every stain, every non-compliant inch of his own domain.

By the time he reached the fourth hour, he had filed forty-seven self-citations. His Compliance Rating dropped. 12 became 10. 10 became 8.

The rat looked at him with pity.

Gerald did not care. He was building a file. A file so thick with violations that it would choke the Inspector.

If the Inspector saw that Gerald had cited himself for every inch of non-compliance, it would prove one thing: Gerald Hutchins was not negligent. He was hyper-compliant. He was so obsessed with the rules that he punished himself for the System’s own failures to provide resources.

It was a gamble. A dangerous, stupid gamble.

The door to his dungeon—the only door, the one that led to the library sub-basement—banged open.

Gerald spun around. The air shimmered. A man in a suit stepped through.

The suit was grey, indistinguishable from the concrete. The man had no face.

Just a smooth patch of skin where features should be.

"Core Hutchins," the faceless man said. His voice was the same as the System voice, but deeper. More resonant. "The audit begins."

Gerald straightened his spine. He clutched his clipboard. "I have the file ready."

The faceless man did not look at the clipboard. He looked at the dungeon. He walked to the water leak. He dipped a finger in the puddle.

"You have allowed a major hazard to persist," the Inspector said.

"I have cited it," Gerald said. "Forty-seven times."

The Inspector looked at the file. He flipped through the pages. The pages fluttered in a wind that did not exist.

"You have cited yourself for lack of ventilation, lack of lighting, lack of safety barriers, and for the rat’s improper posture," the Inspector said.

"The rat’s posture is a code violation," Gerald said. "Section 12, Sub-section F. Animals must be kept in approved enclosures. The rat is free-roaming."

The Inspector stopped flipping. He looked at Gerald.

"Your Compliance Rating is eight," the Inspector said.

"It is eight," Gerald agreed.

"Most Cores with a rating of eight are dissolved within the first week."

"I know."

"Why are you still here?"

"Because I have filed the violations," Gerald said. "And I have corrected none of them, because to correct them without authorization would be a separate violation of the Permitting Code."

The Inspector paused. For a second, the air in the dungeon felt colder.

"You are claiming that the System’s lack of provided resources constitutes a violation of the Permitting Code?" the Inspector asked.

"I am claiming that the System’s refusal to issue me a Building Permit for my own existence is a violation of the Administrative Procedure Act," Gerald said. He had made that up. He wasn’t sure if the Administrative Procedure Act existed in the System. But he sounded confident.

The Inspector stared at him.

Then, the Inspector smiled. It was a terrible sight. The skin of his face stretched tight.

"Interesting," the Inspector said. "You have invoked a procedural loophole. The lack of a Core Permit is a structural defect. You cannot be cited for failing to meet code requirements if you were never authorized to build in the first place."

Gerald’s heart hammered against his ribs. He had guessed right.

"However," the Inspector continued, "the lack of a permit does not exempt you from the Safety Code. The Safety Code is universal. It applies to any structure, permitted or not."

The Inspector raised a hand. A red light appeared in the dungeon. It scanned the water leak.

It scanned the handrails.

It scanned the mold.

"Violation: Active water leak. Severity: Critical. Penalty: Immediate Core Damage."

Pain shot through Gerald. It felt like his bones were being snapped. He fell to his knees. The rat squeaked and hid behind a book.

"Violation: Inadequate ventilation. Severity: Critical. Penalty: Core Damage."

Gerald gasped.

His vision blurred. The System was hurting him. It was punishing him for the things he had cited himself for.

He had played himself.

"Stop," Gerald wheezed.

"The audit is not complete," the Inspector said.

Gerald looked at the file. The file was still there. The self-citations were there.

He realized what he had done. He had listed the violations, but he had not listed the *corrective actions*. He had not said, "I will fix this." He had only said, "This is wrong."

The System punished negligence. It did not punish awareness.

Gerald stood up. His legs shook.

"Corrective action," he said.

The Inspector looked at him. "Yes?"

Gerald looked at the water leak. He looked at the rat. He looked at the mold.

"Spawn," Gerald said. "I spawn a new entity."

The Inspector raised an eyebrow. "You have no mana. You have no resources."

"I have the rat," Gerald said.

He pointed at the rat. The rat froze.

"The rat is a biological entity. Under Section 9, Biological Entities are considered renewable resources if they are properly managed. The rat is currently in violation of the Noise Ordinance. I am citing the rat."

The Inspector blinked. "You are citing your own spawn?"

"Yes. And I am fining it. The fine is collected in mana. The mana is used to fix the water leak."

Gerald focused, and he used his Interface.

He targeted the rat. He applied a Citation. *Violation: Noise.

Fine: 10 Mana.*

The rat screamed. It glowed briefly. Then, a small pulse of energy flew from the rat to Gerald.

Gerald caught it. He used it.

He synthesized a small patch of concrete, and he threw it at the water leak. The leak sealed.

The Inspector watched. He did not move.

"Violation: Noise," Gerald said. "Fine: 5 Mana."

The rat squealed again. Gerald caught the energy. He used it to reinforce the handrail. The handrail straightened.

"Violation: Improper Posture," Gerald said. "Fine: 5 Mana."

The rat curled up. Gerald caught the energy. He used it to clear the mold.

He worked fast, and he cited the rat. He cited the mold.

He cited the water.

He cited the air. He fined everything. He used the fines to fix the dungeon.

It was brutal. It was ugly.

It was not how a dungeon core was supposed to operate.

Dungeon cores grew, and they spawned monsters. They built traps.

Gerald was auditing his own inhabitants.

The Inspector watched. The red lights stopped flashing. The pain in Gerald’s bones subsided.

The Compliance Rating ticked up. 8. 15. 22. 30.

The Inspector walked around the dungeon. He checked the new patch. He checked the handrail. He checked the rat, who was now trembling in a ball, having been fined six times in ten minutes.

"You have achieved compliance," the Inspector said.

"I have achieved compliance," Gerald said. He was breathing hard.

"But you have violated the Spirit of the Code."

The Inspector turned to Gerald. His faceless head tilted.

"Section 1. Purpose of the Code. The Code exists to facilitate growth.

You have stifled it. You have fined your own ecosystem into submission.

This is not inspection.

This is tyranny."

Gerald straightened. "I am an inspector."

"You are a tyrant," the Inspector said. "And the System does not reward tyrants. It rewards regulators."

The Inspector snapped his fingers.

The dungeon shook. The walls groaned.

"I am expanding the audit," the Inspector said.

"To include the neighboring cores. Kessick.

The Mold King.

The Sub-Basement Squatters. You have cited them. Now, I will audit your relationship with them."

Gerald’s blood ran cold.

"If I audit your relationships, and I find that your citations were issued without proper jurisdiction, or without proper notice, or without proper due process..."

The Inspector smiled again.

"...then I will revoke your Core Status for Fraudulent Inspection."

Gerald looked at his file. He had forty-seven self-citations. He had issued citations to Kessick. He had won the hearing.

But had he followed due process?

He remembered the Hearing. He had argued structural logic. He had won. But had he sent the Notice of Hearing in advance?

He had not. He had appeared in Kessick’s dungeon. He had served the Citation in person.

The System rules said: *Citations require the target to be physically present to 'serve'.*

It did not say the target had to be *aware* before the service.

Gerald had been sneaking around. He had been using the Treaty loopholes.

If the Inspector checked the timestamps, he would see that Gerald had entered Kessick’s dungeon *before* filing the citation.

He had committed trespassing. He had committed fraud.

Gerald looked at the rat. The rat looked back.

"I need time," Gerald said.

"You have none," the Inspector said. "The audit of your external interactions begins now."

The faceless man raised his hand. A new window opened in Gerald’s vision. It was a timeline. A timeline of Gerald’s movements.

Red dots appeared on the timeline. Trespassing. Unauthorized entry. Failure to notify.

One red dot became two. Two became ten.

The Inspector spoke. "Violation: Core Hutchins failed to provide 24-hour notice of Citation Hearing to Core Kessick."

Gerald felt a pull. A hook in his soul.

He looked at the timeline. He looked at the Inspector.

He had one second to think. One second to find a loophole.

He looked at the rat. The rat was still trembling.

Gerald opened his mouth.

"I appeal," he said.

The Inspector paused. "On what grounds?"

"On the grounds," Gerald said, his voice shaking, "that Kessick’s dungeon does not exist."

The Inspector stopped. "Clarify."

Gerald took a breath. He looked at the timeline. He looked at the red dots.

"Kessick’s dungeon," Gerald said. "Is a violation.

It is a structure built without a Foundation Permit.

Therefore, legally, it does not exist.

You cannot cite a non-existent structure. And therefore, I did not trespass, because I entered a void."

The Inspector stared at him. The air went silent.

Then, the Inspector laughed. It was a dry, rasping sound.

"Bold," the Inspector said. "But flawed. If Kessick does not exist, then Kessick’s spawns do not exist. If they do not exist, why have you been fighting them?"

Gerald felt the hook tighten. The System was pulling at his core.

"Because they are illegal," Gerald said. "And illegal entities are still subject to enforcement."

The Inspector’s faceless face leaned in.

"Prove it," the Inspector said. "Prove that Kessick has no Foundation Permit. Right now. Or I revoke you."

Gerald looked at his clipboard. He looked at the file.

He had never checked Kessick’s permit. He had only checked his own.

He opened a new tab. He searched the System Database. *Query: Core Kessick. Foundation Permit.*

The screen loaded. It spun.

It was loading.

The Inspector watched. The timer ticked down.

*3... 2... 1...*

The screen blinked.

*No Record Found.*

Gerald exhaled. He looked at the Inspector.

"No Permit," Gerald said. "Therefore, Kessick is a ghost structure. I did not trespass. I entered a void."

The Inspector stood still. He looked at the screen. He looked at Gerald.

Then, the Inspector’s face changed. The skin rippled.

"Correct," the Inspector said.

Gerald slumped.

"However," the Inspector added. "You have just opened a new problem."

"What problem?"

"If Kessick has no Foundation Permit, then Kessick is not a dungeon core. Kessick is a squatter."

The Inspector pointed at the wall.

"And you are an inspector of municipal property. You have jurisdiction over squatters."

The Inspector smiled.

"Go cite them, Hutchins. But this time, you are not citing a peer. You are evicting a parasite. And the System does not give you the right of appeal if you fail to evict."

The Inspector vanished.

Gerald stood alone in the damp, quiet dungeon. The rat squeaked.

Gerald looked at the wall. The wall that separated him from Kessick.

He looked at his clipboard, and he looked at the measuring tape.

He walked to the wall. He raised his hand.

And then, the wall crumbled.
