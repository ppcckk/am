Chapter 18

The hook end of the tape caught on a protruding aggregate chip and Gerald had to work it free twice before he got a clean reading. Fourteen inches from the reference mark. He noted it on the survey form the System had generated for him, a pale blue rectangle hovering at his left hand that updated each time he entered a measurement. Form 9-F. Emergency Injunction. Structural Provenance Certification.

He moved to the next reference point. The wall was poured concrete. Standard mix. He'd known that since day one, when he'd pressed his palm against it and felt the aggregate pattern like a fingerprint. The System had registered his dungeon footprint on a Tuesday in November. The concrete had been poured in August. He remembered because he'd been on a lunch break when the anomaly appeared in his reflection, and he'd still had mustard on his tie.

That was the thing about being a building inspector. You remembered the mundane details. You remembered them because the mundane details were the ones that mattered.

He pulled the tape to the third reference point. Fifteen inches. He noted it. Pulled to the fourth. Fourteen and a half. He noted it. Pulled to the fifth. Fourteen inches. Consistent. Good. The wall was uniform. Uniform meant original. Original meant his.

He worked his way along the south corridor, measuring at thirty-inch intervals. The System's form filled itself in as he went, each entry snapping into a column with a small click that only he could hear. The mana cost was minimal, maybe a tenth of a percent per measurement, but it was cumulative. He checked his reserve. Six percent. Four percent left.

The pulse in the wall was louder now. He could feel it through the tape's metal housing, a faint vibration against his fingers. Not a heartbeat. Something more like a water main under pressure. Steady. Insistent. Getting closer to the surface.

He reached the far end of the corridor and turned around. The wall stretched back the way he'd come, thirty feet of grey concrete with the faint blue glow of the System's survey grid overlaid on it. Everything looked normal. Everything was normal.

Except for the last eight inches.

He hadn't noticed it until he reached the reference point at the twenty-four-foot mark. The tape pulled to sixteen inches. He noted it. Pulled to the next. Sixteen inches. He noted it. Pulled to the next. Sixteen and a quarter. He noted it. Pulled to the next. Sixteen and a half.

He stopped.

The wall was thickening. Not at one point. Across the entire southern face. A gradual increase in depth that started at the twenty-foot mark and continued to the end of the corridor. He went back to the twenty-foot reference point and re-measured. Fourteen inches. Correct. He went to the twenty-two-foot mark. Fifteen inches. He went to twenty-four. Sixteen.

He walked the last six feet of the corridor and pressed his hand flat against the wall. The concrete was warm. Not hot. Warm. Like a pipe with hot water running through it. The pulse was right there, under his palm, steady and close.

He pulled his hand back. Wiped it on his trousers.

The wall had grown. Vellum's tissue was inside the concrete matrix, pushing outward, adding depth. The wall was no longer uniform. The first twenty feet were original. The last ten feet were a composite. Original concrete plus something else. Something growing.

He looked at his survey form. The System had flagged the discrepancy automatically. A small red icon appeared next to the measurements past the twenty-foot mark. The icon was a triangle with an exclamation point. The tooltip read: STRUCTURAL COMPOSITION DIVERGENCE DETECTED. PROVENANCE CONFIDENCE: 67%.

Sixty-seven percent. Not enough. The threshold for a valid provenance claim was eighty-five. He needed the wall to be demonstrably original, and Vellum's growth had contaminated it. The more Vellum grew, the lower his confidence score would drop. By the time the transfer window closed, it might be below fifty.

He sat down on the floor. The concrete was cold. His back ached. He was fifty-four years old and he had been sitting on floors for thirty-one years and his back still ached.

He thought about the problem.

The problem was not that the wall was his. The wall was his. He knew that the way he knew his own name. The problem was that the System could not prove it anymore, because the System's provenance algorithm relied on material uniformity, and Vellum had broken that uniformity from the inside.

Gerald pulled out his clipboard. The System had given him one when he'd registered as a Core. It was a standard municipal clipboard, the kind with the metal clip and the printed header that said CITY OF ASHFORD BUILDING DEPARTMENT. He'd kept it because the System had not offered an alternative.

He wrote on the back of a survey form in pencil. His handwriting was small and neat and slightly left-slanted, the handwriting of a man who had filled out thousands of inspection reports.

PROBLEM: Provenance confidence below threshold due to Vellum contamination. SOLUTION: Do not prove the wall is mine. Prove Vellum's addition is illegal.

He underlined the second line. He looked at it. He nodded once.

He flipped to a fresh page. He wrote:

CITATION BASIS: Unauthorized Structural Modification. AUTHORITY: System Building Code, Section 4.12.2 (Permit Chain of Custody). VIOLATION: Structural modification performed without registered permit, without inspection, without approval. SEVERITY: Class 2 (Material alteration of existing structure).

He stared at the words. His hand was shaking slightly. Not from fear. From the specific satisfaction of a man who has found the right form.

The Permit Chain of Custody was a mechanic he'd read about in the code but never used. It was a deep rule, buried in the structural compliance section, and it said something very specific: any structural modification to a registered footprint required a permit filed with the System, and the permit had to be approved before the modification took place. If a modification occurred without a permit, it was not just a violation. It was void. The modification did not legally exist.

Vellum had not filed a permit. Gerald had checked. He'd checked every morning since the pulse started. No permit. No filing. No approval.

Which meant the growth was illegal. Which meant the integration was built on an illegal foundation. Which meant the transfer request, which relied on integration percentage, was built on a violation.

He stood up. He walked to the twenty-foot mark. He measured the wall again. Fourteen inches. Original. He walked to the twenty-two-foot mark. Fifteen inches. Contaminated. He marked the transition point with a piece of chalk from his inventory. A thin white line on the concrete.

The boundary. Inside the line: his wall. Outside the line: Vellum's illegal addition.

He opened the Citation form. Form 7-A. Standard Citation. He filled it out with the precision of a man who had filled out ten thousand of them. Target: Vellum Core. Location of violation: South corridor, 22-foot to 32-foot marks. Nature of violation: Unauthorized structural modification. Code section: 4.12.2. Severity: Class 2.

He read it back. It was clean. It was airtight.

But he knew the rule. Citations had to be served in person. The target Core had to be physically present at the moment of service. Vellum's core was not in the corridor. Vellum's core was inside the wall. Inside the illegal addition.

He looked at the chalk line.

He looked at the wall beyond it.

He pulled out his mana gauge. Three percent.

He pulled out his inventory. The measuring tape. The clipboard. A pencil. A small bottle of chalk dust. A half-eaten granola bar from the library vending machine. A Form 9-F with a sixty-seven percent provenance score that was probably already dropping.

He looked at the wall. The pulse was right there. Six inches from the surface. He could feel it through the concrete, a slow push, like a fist pressing against a door.

He put his hand on the wall. The concrete was warm. The surface was slightly soft. Not wet. Soft. Like bread dough that had been left out too long.

He pressed harder. His fingers sank in. An inch. Two inches. The concrete gave way like mud.

He pulled his hand out. His fingertips were grey. Not dirty. Grey. The color of the wall's interior.

He wiped them on his trousers. He looked at the Citation form in his other hand. He looked at the soft wall.

He stepped through.

The space inside the wall was not what he expected.

He had expected tissue. Organic material. Something like the inside of a tree trunk or the interior of a fungal network. Something Vellum. Something alien.

Instead he was standing in a room.

It was small. Maybe eight feet by eight. The walls were smooth, dark, and dry. The floor was the same material. The ceiling was low. He had to duck slightly. The air was cool and still and smelled like nothing. Not like earth. Not like stone. Like nothing. Like a room that had never had a smell.

There was no Vellum here. No growth. No pulse. Just a room.

And in the center of the room, on the floor, was a core.

It was small. Smaller than Gerald's. Maybe the size of a grapefruit. It was dark grey, almost black, and it was cracked. A single fissure ran across its surface from top to bottom. It was not pulsing. It was not glowing. It was dead.

Gerald knelt beside it. He pulled out his inspection loupe from his inventory. He held it over the core and looked through it.

The core's surface was covered in System text. Tiny, dense, barely legible. He squinted.

COMPLIANCE RATING: 0/100. STATUS: TERMINATED. CAUSE OF TERMINATION: STRUCTURAL COLLAPSE. DATE OF TERMINATION: 1,247 DAYS AGO.

He looked at the date. He counted backward. 1,247 days. That was more than three years.

He looked at the core's registration number. He read it twice.

The registration number was not Vellum's. It was not his. It belonged to a Core that had existed here before him. Before the library. Before the municipal building. Before the city.

A dead Core. Buried inside his wall. Inside Vellum's illegal addition. Terminated for structural collapse.

Gerald sat back on his heels. His knees ached.

He looked at the Citation form in his hand. He looked at the dead Core.

The dead Core's footprint. He could see it. A faint outline on the floor, like a stain. A circle. The same shape as a dungeon footprint registration. And the circle was larger than his own. Much larger. It extended beyond the walls of this small room. It extended beyond the walls of his dungeon.

He had built his dungeon on top of a dead one. His registration had been issued for this space. But this space had already been registered. To someone else. Someone who had died here.

Which meant his registration was invalid. Which meant he had no legal standing to contest any transfer. Which meant Form 9-F was worthless. Which meant the Citation was worthless. Which meant Vellum could take his dungeon, and the System would support it, because the System's records showed that Gerald had never owned this footprint at all.

He looked at the dead Core. The cracked surface. The zero rating. The termination notice.

He looked at his own hands. Grey fingertips. Concrete dust under his nails.

He stood up. He ducked through the wall and back into his corridor. The chalk line was still there. The pulse was still there. The transfer window was still open.

He had four hours left. And no legal standing. And a dead Core in his wall that the System did not know about. And a Citation that was now a weapon he could not use.

He looked at the Citation form. Then he looked at the wall. Then he looked at the dead Core's registration number again.

He pulled out his clipboard. He flipped to a fresh page.

He wrote:

CITATION BASIS: Failure to Report Prior Occupancy. AUTHORITY: System Building Code, Section 1.3.7 (Footprint Disclosure Requirement). VIOLATION: Core failed to disclose prior registered footprint at time of registration. SEVERITY: Class 1 (Fraudulent registration).

He underlined the last line. He underlined it twice.

Class 1. The highest severity. Not a violation of structure. A violation of truth. A violation of the registration itself.

If he could prove that the System had issued his registration without knowledge of the prior footprint, then his registration was not invalid. It was fraudulent. And fraudulent registrations could be appealed. Not by the Core who filed them. By the Core who was harmed by them.

Vellum was not the harmed party. Gerald was.

He looked at the dead Core. He looked at the Citation form. He looked at the wall.

He could not serve this Citation on Vellum. He had to serve it on the System.

The System did not have a physical presence. The System did not have a Core. The System could not be served in person.

Unless.

He looked at the dead Core again. The registration number. The termination notice. The date.

The dead Core had been terminated by the System. For structural collapse. The System had audited it. The System had killed it. The System had been physically present at the termination.

The System's Inspector General. The same entity that would audit him in forty-two days.

The Inspector General had terminated this Core. The Inspector General had been here. The Inspector General had seen this footprint. The Inspector General had registered his own footprint over it without disclosure.

The Inspector General was the one who had committed the violation.

Gerald looked at the Citation form. He looked at the dead Core. He looked at his own hands.

He had four hours. He had two percent mana. He had a Citation that could only be served on an entity that was not here, had never been here, and did not have a physical form.

He folded the Citation form. He put it in his inventory.

He pulled out his measuring tape.

He went back to the wall.
