Chapter 220: Incubated Citation

# Chapter 220

Gerald's pen hovered over the parchment.

The ink tip dripped a single black tear onto the System interface. The drop sizzled against the glowing blue lattice of the Inspector General's authority shield. The sound was small, wet, and final.

The room remained dark.

The absence of light did not mean the absence of danger. Gerald knew this from three decades of inspecting basements.

Darkness hid water damage.

Darkness hid termites. Darkness hid the structural compromises that eventually brought down the ceiling.

He did not blink.

His ocular sensors, repurposed from the mold spores he had cultivated in Sector Four, adjusted to the low-light conditions.

He could see the outline of the Inspector General's form. It was rigid.

Statuesque.

The void where eyes should have been was swirling with static.

"Penalty applied," Gerald said.

His voice echoed in the empty chamber. It lacked the reverb of a proper auditorium. This space was too small. Acoustically non-compliant.

The Inspector General did not move. The debuff of *Stability Reduced by 50%* held him in place.

To move without support was to risk falling into the abyss of deletion.

The System enforced its own rules.

A structure that violated code could not support its own weight.

Gerald lowered the pen.

He stepped forward. The floor beneath his feet hummed with residual energy. The mandatory shutdown had severed the dungeon's connection to the upper realms.

No adventurers would enter. No loot would be claimed.

The dungeon was dead to the outside world, but it was alive to him.

He approached the frozen figure.

The Inspector General was not a man. It was a construct of pure administrative law, given form by the sheer volume of regulations it enforced. Gerald had seen similar constructs in dreams, usually involving tax audits or zoning boards that existed in other dimensions.

But this one had feet.

Gerald crouched. He pulled a tape measure from his utility belt.

The metal tape unspooled with a sharp *snap*. The hook caught on the edge of the Inspector General's boot. Gerald extended the blade. Twelve inches.

Standard issue.

He retracted the tape.

"Footwear compliance," Gerald muttered. "Shoe size exceeds standard municipal variance for public sector employees. Potential tripping hazard for subordinate agents."

He looked up.

The void eyes flickered. A low growl vibrated through the floorboards. It was not a threat.

It was a system error.

The Inspector General was trying to override the Citation with a higher-level administrative command.

Gerald felt the pressure against his core.

A headache bloomed behind his eyes, sharp and rhythmic. The mental strain of issuing a Citation to a Class-S entity was immense. It required him to hold the entire rulebook in his mind simultaneously.

He had to remember every sub-clause of the Fire Safety Act, every footnote on ventilation standards, every addendum regarding magical resonance.

He gritted his teeth.

*Section 14, Paragraph B, Subsection 3: Emergency Egress.*

He remembered. He always remembered.

The Inspector General's form flickerred. The static in the void eyes intensified. The figure was attempting to appeal. The System window appeared in Gerald's peripheral vision.

**APPEAL INITIATED.** **HEARING IN T-10 SECONDS.**

Gerald's hand tightened on his clipboard.

This was the loophole.

The System allowed appeals. It allowed the accused to argue that the citation was invalid due to contextual factors. The Inspector General could claim that the lack of fire suppression was intentional, designed to create a 'thermal hazard zone' for high-level adventurers.

If the Inspector General won the Hearing, the debuff would be reversed. Gerald would lose his standing. He might be demoted. He might be deleted.

But Gerald had read the fine print.

*Appeals must be based on structural logic.*

Not intent. Not strategy. Structural logic.

The timer hit zero.

The darkness vanished. The room flooded with white light. The Inspector General stood tall, the stability debuff still active but suppressed by the Hearing shield.

A new figure appeared in the center of the room. It was a small, floating orb of golden light. The Adjudicator. It had no face, no body, only a voice that sounded like paper tearing.

"Core Gerald Hutchins," the Adjudicator said. "You have cited Core IG-77 for Absence of Fire Suppression. The accused claims this is a tactical design feature, not a violation. Argue your case."

Gerald did not step forward. He did not raise his voice. He simply opened his clipboard.

"The accused dungeon is subterranean," Gerald said. "Depth: forty meters below the Ashford Municipal Library. Soil composition: heavy clay and sandstone. Drainage: inadequate."

The Adjudicator pulsed. "Relevant."

"Ventilation is reliant on natural thermal updrafts," Gerald continued. "The absence of active suppression systems creates a positive feedback loop. In a fire event, heat would rise, pulling more oxygen from the surface. The dungeon would act as a chimney."

Gerald paused. He looked at the Inspector General.

"The accused has not installed fireproofing materials on the support pillars. They are wood. Oak, specifically. Grade A."

The Inspector General shifted. The stability debuff forced his knees to bend slightly. He caught himself on a pillar.

Wood.

The Adjudicator's light turned orange.

"The code requires non-combustible materials for all load-bearing structures in subterranean environments," Gerald said. "The accused has violated Structural Code 9.2. Furthermore, the proximity of the oak pillars to the magical resonance crystals creates a risk of ignition via arcane friction."

Gerald tapped his pen against the clipboard.

"This is not tactical design.

This is negligence.

If a fire started, the dungeon would collapse within four minutes. The survival rate for occupants would be zero.

The survival rate for the Core would be zero."

The Adjudicator hummed. The sound was deep, resonant, like a church bell struck underwater.

"Argument accepted on structural grounds," the Adjudicator said. "However, the penalty of Mandatory Shutdown is disproportionate. The Core has a Compliance Rating of 98. The shutdown will cause economic damage to the regional System."

Gerald felt a spike of panic in his chest.

He hadn't anticipated the economic clause. The System cared about throughput.

Adventurers needed to loot.

Loot needed to be generated by functional dungeons. If Gerald shut down the Inspector General, the flow of high-tier loot would stop. The local economy would suffer.

The System would punish him for disrupting the flow.

He needed a compromise.

He needed a cost.

Gerald looked at the Inspector General. He looked at the cracks in the crystal walls. He looked at the oak pillars.

"I propose a modification," Gerald said.

The Adjudicator paused.

"Speak."

"The shutdown is too severe. I withdraw the Mandatory Shutdown."

A murmur rippled through the room. The Inspector General's void eyes narrowed. He had won. The big stick had been dropped.

"But," Gerald raised a finger, "I invoke the Remediation Clause."

The Adjudicator's light shifted to green.

"Explain."

"Section 22, Paragraph 4: The Core responsible for a critical violation must fund its own remediation. The accused must construct a fire suppression system. They must replace the oak pillars with stone. They must install ventilation shafts that meet current standards."

Gerald smiled. It was a thin, tight expression.

"And they must pay the fine in Mana."

The Inspector General roared. It was a sound of genuine rage, not just administrative displeasure.

To replace the oak pillars was to dismantle the core of his dungeon's aesthetic.

It was to admit fault. It was to spend centuries of accumulated Mana on boring, necessary plumbing.

"I will not," the Inspector General hissed. "I am the Law."

"You are a structure," Gerald said. "And structures fail."

He pointed to the ceiling.

"Look up."

The Inspector General looked up.

Cracks spiderwebbed across the ceiling. The magical resonance was destabilizing the rock.

The heat from the crystals was drying out the clay.

The dungeon was already failing.

It just hadn't collapsed yet.

"If you do not remediate," Gerald said, "I will file a Second Citation for Imminent Structural Failure. That citation carries no appeal. It carries immediate dissolution."

The threat hung in the air.

The Inspector General looked at the cracks. He looked at Gerald. He looked at the Adjudicator.

For the first time, the void eyes showed calculation.

He was weighing the cost of the fines against the risk of deletion.

"Fine," the Inspector General said. The word was dragged out, heavy with resentment. "I will pay."

The Adjudicator pulsed.

"Remediation ordered. Funds deducted. Hearing closed."

The white light faded. The room returned to darkness.

Gerald exhaled.

His hands were shaking. He tucked the clipboard under his arm. He needed to sit down.

He needed a glass of water. He needed to check his own blood pressure.

But he could not leave.

The Inspector General had not moved.

Gerald took a step back. Then another.

The Inspector General's head turned. The void eyes locked onto Gerald.

"You think this is over," the Inspector General said.

His voice was no longer the voice of the System. It was something older. Something darker.

"You think you have won because you followed the rules."

Gerald stopped.

"I followed the rules," Gerald said.

"The rules are a cage," the Inspector General said. "And you are shaking the bars."

The floor beneath Gerald's feet vibrated. Not with the hum of energy, but with the tremor of something moving underground. Something massive.

Gerald checked his map.

Sector Six. The deep foundations.

He had not inspected Sector Six. He had not inspected it because the map had been blank. He had assumed it was bedrock.

But bedrock did not move.

"What did you summon?" Gerald asked.

"I did not summon anything," the Inspector General said. "I merely removed the fire suppression. And now, the heat has reached the lower levels."

Gerald's blood ran cold.

The thermal feedback loop.

He had predicted it. He had used it to win the argument.

But he had not accounted for the immediate consequence.

The dungeon was still hot. The clay was still dry. And now, with the suppression removed, the heat was rising rapidly.

The vibration grew louder. Dust fell from the ceiling.

Gerald turned and ran.

He ran toward the exit. The corridor was narrow. The walls were sweating condensation. The air smelled of sulfur and burning wood.

He reached the door to the library sub-basement. It was locked.

He tried the handle. It was stuck. The wood had swelled from the humidity.

"Unlock," Gerald said. "System, unlock."

**LOCKED. REASON: INTERNAL PRESSURE DIFFERENTIAL. DOOR JAMMED.**

Gerald looked back.

The Inspector General was gone. The room was empty. But the floor was glowing red. A fissure opened in the center of the chamber, spewing steam and sparks.

The dungeon was cooking itself.

Gerald pounded on the door. The metal was hot. He pulled his hand back, hissing at the burn.

He needed a tool. He needed a lever.

He looked around. There was a fire extinguisher on the wall.

The Inspector General had cited it as missing, but it was there.

It was just empty.

The gauge read zero.

Gerald stared at the extinguisher.

He remembered the citation. *Absence of Fire Suppression.*

The System had enforced the citation. It had removed the functional extinguishers. But it had left the hardware.

He ripped the empty canister from the bracket. It was heavy, cold steel.

He swung it at the door.

The impact rang through the corridor. The door did not budge. The wood frame was warped. The latch was fused by heat.

He swung again.

The metal dented. The door shuddered.

The steam behind him was rising. The temperature in the corridor was climbing past sixty degrees. Then seventy. Then eighty.

Gerald's vision blurred. His core was overheating. He felt nauseous.

The physical symptoms of his flaw—the paralysis of improvisation—were kicking in. He wanted to file a report.

He wanted to measure the door's deflection.

He wanted to document the failure.

But the door would not open.

And the heat was coming.

He swung the extinguisher one last time. With all his strength. With the desperation of a man who had never been allowed to break a rule, only to find that the rules were trying to kill him.

The latch snapped.

The door burst open.

Gerald stumbled out into the library sub-basement.

The air was cool. The smell of old paper and dust replaced the smell of sulfur. He fell to his knees, gasping.

He looked back at the door.

The handle turned.

From the other side.

Gerald froze.

The door creaked open.

A hand reached through the gap. It was not the hand of the Inspector General. It was not human. It was made of bone and ash, fused together by intense heat.

The creature pushed the door wider.

It stepped into the library.

It was small. It was a rat.

But it was not the confused rat from the beginning.

This rat was the size of a dog. Its fur was singed.

Its eyes were pits of white fire.

It hissed, a sound like escaping steam.

Gerald scrambled back.

The rat looked at him. It tilted its head.

**SYSTEM ALERT: NEW ENTITY DETECTED.** **TYPE: PYRO-MORPHIC RODENT.** **THREAT LEVEL: EXTREME.** **ORIGIN: UNLICENSED BIOLOGICAL ENTITY.**

Gerald stared at the rat.

He had cited the rat once, years ago. Form 11-B. Unlicensed Biological Entity.

It had never been removed.

It had been hiding in the walls. And now, the heat had incubated it.

The rat lunged.

Gerald raised his clipboard.
