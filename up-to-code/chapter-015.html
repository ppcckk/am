Chapter 15

He didn't have the materials. He didn't have the time. He didn't have a contractor, because contractors in the System were Level 40+ entities that cost more mana than he had in his entire Core reservoir.

Gerald pulled up the Variance Provision.

He'd been digging through the code's appendix for twenty minutes when he found it, buried under a sub-section called "Conditional Use Exceptions for Non-Standard Structures." The language was dense, almost deliberately obscure, but Gerald had read municipal variance provisions so many times in his career that the syntax had become muscle memory. A variance wasn't a fix. It was a legal suspension. You weren't complying. You were asking permission not to comply, and the clock stopped while you asked.

The System's version worked like this: a Core could file a Conditional Use Variance on any single code category. Once filed, that category entered a "Pending Review" status. The Inspector General could not cite a category in Pending Review. The review period lasted forty-eight hours. During that window, other Cores within a two-kilometer radius could file objections. If an objection was filed, the variance was suspended and a Hearing was scheduled. The Hearing consumed additional time, but the category remained in Pending Review until the Hearing resolved.

Gerald read it twice. Then he filed on Accessibility. Then on Electrical.

Both accepted. Pending Review status confirmed. His accessibility score of 22 was now legally invisible to the audit. His electrical score of 58, same.

He had forty-eight hours of breathing room on those two categories. And he had seventy-two hours total. The math worked if he could fix fire safety and ventilation in the remaining window, because those were the categories he actually had the materials for. Fire exits were just door frames. Ventilation was a matter of widening two ducts and adding a second intake shaft. He could do it. He'd done it a hundred times in his career, just not with mana instead of lumber.

He got to work.

The first twelve hours were mechanical. He widened the fire exit frames on the east corridor, replacing decorative stone arches with proper egress doors that swung in the direction of travel. He added a second ventilation intake at the junction between rooms four and five, routing it to the surface vent he'd sealed three chapters ago. He widened the ductwork in the main hall from eight inches to fourteen.

His fire safety rating climbed from 41 to 67. Ventilation went from 73 to 81. Both above the violation threshold.

At hour fourteen, he stopped to eat. He didn't have food. He had a mana pellet that tasted like wet cardboard and sat in his stomach like a stone. He ate it anyway.

At hour nineteen, he was rerouting the electrical conduits away from the water lines when the notification hit.

**OBJECTION FILED. Variance Application #A-7741 (Accessibility). Objection by Core "Skarn." Hearing scheduled: 6 hours.**

Gerald stared at the notification. He'd never heard of Skarn. He pulled up the dungeon registry, which the System maintained as a public record, and searched by name.

Skarn's dungeon was three kilometers southeast. Mid-tier. About four hundred rooms. Torture-themed, which was distasteful but not illegal. His Compliance Rating was 74, which meant he was passing all categories but not excelling at any. He had filed objections before. The registry showed a history of them. Dozens. He'd objected to a Core called Thornwall's ventilation variance. To a Core called the Pale Engine's structural variance. Every objection had been filed at the last possible moment, within the final two hours of the comment window.

This wasn't a genuine concern about accessibility standards. This was harassment.

Gerald had seen this tactic before. Not in dungeons. In municipal zoning hearings. A developer would file a variance, and a competitor would file a frivolous objection specifically to force a hearing, because the hearing process took weeks and the developer's project permit expired in the meantime. The objection wasn't about the substance. It was about the delay.

Skarn was doing to him what a rival developer did to a housing project. And Gerald had sixty-one hours left on his audit clock. A hearing would eat six of them. Maybe more if the argument went long.

He had six hours to prepare for a hearing he hadn't asked for, about a variance he needed, against an opponent who was using the process as a weapon.

Gerald opened the Hearing interface. It was structured like a debate. Both Cores presented arguments. The System evaluated structural logic. The winner kept their variance. The loser got a small penalty to their Compliance Rating.

He pulled up the accessibility requirements again. Corridor width: minimum 36 inches. His corridors were 28. That was a violation. There was no arguing that. But variance hearings weren't about whether you violated the code. They were about whether the variance was justified. And Gerald knew, from fifteen years of municipal hearings, that justification had a specific structure.

You needed three things. First: a demonstrated hardship unique to the property. Not a general inconvenience. A specific, unavoidable constraint. Second: a minimum-impact modification. The variance couldn't be broader than necessary. Third: no public harm. The variance couldn't endanger others.

He drafted his argument.

Hardship: his dungeon was carved from natural limestone in a seismic zone. The bedrock was unstable. Widening corridors to 36 inches would require shoring costs that exceeded his Core's total mana budget by a factor of nine. The constraint wasn't preference. It was geology.

Minimum impact: he wasn't asking for a blanket exemption. He was asking for a 28-inch minimum, which still allowed passage for all standard hero classes. He included a table showing hero shoulder widths. Even the largest hero class, the Dreadnought, had a shoulder width of 26 inches. His corridors were 28. No hero would be physically blocked.

Public harm: none. The variance affected only internal circulation. It did not reduce egress capacity. His fire exits were now code-compliant. His occupancy load was 89, well within limits.

He submitted the argument. Then he sat back and waited.

The Hearing opened at hour twenty-five. Gerald entered the interface and found himself in a white void with a podium. Across from him was a representation of Skarn's Core. It looked like a black iron sphere wrapped in chains. A small, mean-looking thing.

Skarn's argument was brief. He claimed that the seismic justification was unverifiable. He claimed that Gerald had not provided geological survey data. He claimed that the hero shoulder-width table was irrelevant because future hero classes might be larger.

The System flagged the argument. It had merit. Gerald hadn't provided a geological survey. The System didn't know his dungeon was in a seismic zone unless he'd proven it.

Gerald pulled up his dungeon's environmental data. The System logged everything. He found the seismic reading from the day he'd first carved the east corridor. A 2.3 tremor. Minor, but logged. He submitted it.

Skarn's representative shifted. He tried a new angle. He argued that the variance was too broad because it applied to all corridors, not just the ones in the seismic zone.

This was the one Gerald had been waiting for. Because he'd anticipated it. He'd limited his variance request to the east and north corridors specifically. The south corridor, which sat on stable granite, he'd already widened to 36 inches during the fourteen-hour window. He submitted a room-by-room breakdown showing which corridors were covered by the variance and which were already compliant.

The System evaluated. The silence lasted eleven seconds. Gerald counted them.

**Variance granted. Accessibility category remains in Pending Review. Objection overruled. Appellant penalty: -2 Compliance Rating.**

Gerald exhaled. He had fifty hours left. He'd lost six to the hearing. He still had time to finish the electrical reroute, which was his remaining problem.

He was halfway through rerouting the third conduit when the second notification hit.

**OBJECTION FILED. Variance Application #A-7742 (Electrical). Objection by Core "Skarn." Hearing scheduled: 4 hours.**

Gerald closed his eyes. Four hours. He'd have forty-six hours left. He could still make it. Barely.

He opened the Hearing interface again. This time, Skarn's argument was more sophisticated. He claimed that Gerald's electrical variance was unnecessary because Gerald had already begun rerouting the conduits during the comment period. If Gerald was fixing the violation, he didn't need a variance. The variance was therefore a bad-faith filing, designed to delay the audit.

This was clever. It was also wrong, and Gerald knew exactly why. A variance wasn't a statement that you wouldn't fix the problem. It was a statement that you needed time to fix it. The System's own code said so. Section 14.7(b): "A Conditional Use Variance does not preclude corrective action during the review period. Corrective action during review is encouraged and does not invalidate the variance."

Gerald cited the section directly. He quoted it verbatim. Then he added: the rerouting was incomplete. Three of nine conduits still ran parallel to water lines. He couldn't finish in the remaining window without the variance. The variance was necessary precisely because he was mid-correction.

The System evaluated. This time it took six seconds.

**Variance granted. Electrical category remains in Pending Review. Objection overruled.**

Gerald had forty-two hours. He finished the electrical reroute in the next eight. His electrical rating climbed from 58 to 79. He was now passing every category except accessibility, which was shielded by the variance.

He allowed himself to rest. He sat in his main chamber, in the dark, and listened to the water drip through the new conduit routing. Everything was in order. Everything was code-compliant or variance-protected. The audit would come in thirty-four hours and he would pass.

At hour thirty-one, a new notification appeared. It was not an objection. It was something Gerald had never seen before.

**PRIORITY AUDIT INITIATED. Trigger: cumulative hearing activity exceeding threshold. Inspector General arrival: 2 hours.**

Gerald read it three times. The System's audit threshold. He hadn't known it existed. Two hearings in a single variance cycle had triggered an escalation. The IG wasn't coming in thirty-four hours. The IG was coming in two.

And his accessibility category was in Pending Review. Pending Review meant the IG couldn't cite it. But Pending Review also meant the category was unresolved. If the IG arrived during Pending Review, the variance would be evaluated on the spot. Not through a hearing. Through an inspection. The IG would look at his corridors, measure them, and decide whether the variance was valid.

Gerald had two hours. He couldn't widen his corridors in two hours. He couldn't fix accessibility. He could only make sure the variance held up under direct inspection.

He pulled up his seismic data again. He pulled up the hero shoulder-width table. He pulled up his room-by-room breakdown. He prepared everything.

Then he checked the dungeon registry one more time. Skarn's profile. The hearing history. The pattern of objections.

And he saw something he'd missed before. Skarn's first objection, three months ago, had been filed against a Core called "Hutchins, G."

Gerald's hands went cold.

He clicked on the registry entry for Hutchins, G. It was a dungeon Core. It had been shut down six weeks ago. A shutdown order. Compliance Rating dropped to zero. The System had dissolved it.

He clicked on the shutdown report. The violation that triggered it: Accessibility. Corridor width below minimum. The report cited Section 9.3(a). Gerald had written that citation himself. Six years ago. As a municipal inspector. He'd cited a small residential addition for non-compliant hallway width. The homeowner had appealed. The appeal had failed. The structure had been condemned.

The System had translated that condemnation into a dungeon shutdown. Because the System's code was Gerald's code. The same sections. The same thresholds. The same language.

Skarn had used Gerald's own citation history to find the exact violation that would destroy a Core built on Gerald's professional instincts. And Skarn had filed objections specifically to trigger the Priority Audit, forcing the IG to arrive while Gerald's weakest category was still exposed.

This wasn't harassment. This was targeted.

Gerald looked at his dungeon. His corridors were 28 inches wide. The IG would measure them in ninety minutes. The variance would be tested. And if the IG found that Gerald's seismic justification was insufficient, the variance would be revoked, the category would be cited, and the citation would stack with everything else.

He had ninety minutes to make his variance bulletproof. He couldn't widen the corridors. But he could do something else. Something he'd never done in his career, because it had always been illegal.

He could reclassify the corridors.

Section 11.2: "Corridors with a width of 24 to 30 inches may be reclassified as 'alcove passages' if they connect to a room with a turning radius of 60 inches or greater at both endpoints." Alcove passages were exempt from the 36-inch requirement. They were considered decorative connectors, not circulation space.

His corridors were 28 inches. That fell within the alcove range. His junction rooms had sharp ninety-degree turns with no turning radius. But he could add turning radius. He could carve out a 60-inch circular space at the end of each corridor. That was feasible in ninety minutes.

He started carving.

The System pinged again. A new notification. He ignored it. He kept carving. The stone gave way under his mana channel, slow but steady. He shaped the first turning radius. Then the second. Then the third.

At the fourth corridor, he checked the notification he'd ignored.

**COUNTER-CITATION FILED. Core "Skarn" has filed a Citation against Core "Hutchins, G." Alleged violation: Fraudulent Variance Application. Citation requires physical service. Server must be present at target dungeon entrance.**

Gerald stopped carving. A Citation required physical presence. Skarn was trying to serve him in person. Which meant Skarn was coming to his dungeon entrance.

But Gerald had one more trick. Section 4.1: "A Citation may be refused if the serving party is within a zone of active hero combat." Heroes were the only entities that could legally block a Citation. The System recognized hero combat as a force majeure exception.

He checked his hero tracker. There was a party of four Level 30 adventurers heading toward his dungeon entrance. They'd been circling for hours, looking for a way in. They were close. Very close.

Gerald made a decision. He opened his dungeon's front gate. He let the heroes in.

He would use them as a shield.
