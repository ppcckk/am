Chapter 42

The third support column was already bending when Gerald realized he had miscalculated the load distribution.

He pressed his palm against the concrete. It was warm. Not hot, not yet, but warm in the way a beam gets warm when it's carrying more than it was designed to carry. He could feel the vibration through his fingertips, a low hum that meant the molecular bonds were stretching toward their limit.

He pulled his hand back. Checked his notes. Ran the numbers again.

Twenty-two tons. He had applied twenty-two tons of simulated weight to the eastern corridor, and the corridor was holding. The columns were holding. The floor was holding. But the load was not distributing the way he had modeled it. The stress was traveling laterally instead of vertically, and it was pooling in a section of wall that Gerald had not reinforced because, according to his original structural plan, that section was non-load-bearing.

It was, in fact, load-bearing.

Gerald stared at the wall. The concrete was fine. No cracks, no spalling, no visible deformation. But his instruments, the ones the System had provided, were reading a stress concentration of 340 PSI in a wall rated for 200.

He pulled up the code. Scrolled to the structural section. Found the clause he had been looking for.

Adaptive Structural Tolerance. Section 14.7(c): "A structure that has demonstrated, through documented load-bearing performance, capacity exceeding its rated specification by a factor of 1.5 or greater shall be considered compliant with all structural requirements applicable to that category, regardless of original design parameters."

One point five. He needed to prove his dungeon could carry fifty percent more than its rated capacity. The eastern corridor was rated for fourteen tons. He needed twenty-one. He had applied twenty-two. The corridor was holding.

But the wall was not rated for this. The wall was not part of the corridor's load path in Gerald's original design. The wall was doing something Gerald had not planned for, and it was doing it without complaint, and it was doing it at 340 PSI when it should have failed at 200.

He turned off the load simulator. The hum died. The warmth in the concrete faded.

Gerald sat down on the floor. His clipboard was in his lap. He had not written anything on it in six hours. The page that said WHAT IS THE INTENDED STRUCTURAL FUNCTION was still there, still underlined twice, and below it he had written three lines of notes about load distribution that were now irrelevant.

The wall was not supposed to bear load. He had checked. He had measured. He had run the structural calculations himself, three times, using the System's own physics engine. The eastern corridor's load path ran through the columns, down to the foundation slab, and out to the bedrock. The wall was a partition. A non-structural element. It existed to divide space, not to carry weight.

And it had carried twenty-two tons without cracking.

Gerald pulled up his dungeon schematic. The System's schematic, the one that showed every element he had built, every modification he had filed paperwork for. He zoomed in on the eastern corridor. The wall was there, labeled Partition, Non-Structural, 8-inch concrete block.

He zoomed out. Looked at the whole dungeon. Four thousand square feet. He knew every inch of it. He had measured every wall, every column, every joint. He had filed a Form 7-A for every modification and a Form 12-C for every inspection.

He zoomed back in. Looked at the wall again.

It was not on the schematic.

Not the wall itself. The wall was there. But the load path was not. The System's schematic showed the load path going around the wall, through the columns, down to the slab. The schematic said the wall carried nothing. The schematic was wrong.

Or the wall was wrong.

Gerald stood up. He walked to the wall. He pressed his palm against it again. Cold now. Solid. He knocked on it. The sound was dense, uniform, no hollow spots. He pulled out his measuring tape. Eight inches, exactly. He checked the block dimensions. Standard.

He pulled up the code again. Scrolled to a section he had never needed before. Structural Anomalies, Section 9.1: "Any structural element that demonstrates load-bearing capacity inconsistent with its rated specification shall be flagged for investigation by the responsible Core. Failure to investigate within 72 hours constitutes a Class 3 violation."

Seventy-two hours. The Hearing was in thirty-one.

Gerald uncapped his pen. He wrote on the clipboard: LOAD PATH ANOMALY. EASTERN CORRIDOR. UNRATED WALL.

He underlined it once. Then he crossed out the underline and wrote it again without the underline. He was not underlining things anymore. Underlining was for emphasis, and emphasis was for arguing, and he was not arguing. He was investigating.

He pulled up his spawn list. Three mold spores, a water leak, a confused rat, and eleven adventurers currently in his dungeon's eastern wing who had wandered in looking for a "high-difficulty challenge" and were now sitting on a bench reading the egress signage because they could not figure out how to leave.

He did not need the adventurers. He needed the wall.

Gerald walked to his supply cache. The System gave him resources in bulk: concrete, steel rebar, copper wire, PVC pipe, fire-rated drywall, the standard municipal materials that any building inspector would recognize. He pulled out a core drill. A diamond-tipped bit. He walked back to the wall.

He drilled a hole. Quarter-inch diameter, eight inches deep. Straight through. He pulled the bit out and looked at the core sample.

Concrete block. Standard. Aggregate visible. No rebar.

He drilled a second hole. Six inches from the first. Same result.

He drilled a third. Same.

He drilled a fourth, lower, near the floor. Same.

He drilled a fifth, higher, near the ceiling. Same.

The wall was concrete block. Eight inches. No reinforcement. Rated for 200 PSI. Carrying 340.

Gerald sat down. He put the core samples on the floor in a row. He looked at them. He looked at the wall. He looked at the schematic.

He pulled up the System's physics engine. Not the code, the physics. The raw simulation layer. He had used it before for structural calculations, but only for his own builds. He had never queried it directly for a pre-existing element.

He typed the query. Load path analysis. Eastern corridor. Non-structural partition, 8-inch concrete block.

The physics engine returned a result.

Load path: THROUGH.

Gerald read it again. THROUGH. The load path went through the wall. Not around it. Through it. The System's own physics engine said the wall was load-bearing. The schematic said it was not. The schematic was Gerald's. He had filed it. He had stamped it. He had built to it.

The System's physics disagreed with Gerald's filing.

He pulled up another query. Load path analysis. All non-structural partitions in the dungeon.

The results came back. Every single one. Load path: THROUGH.

Every partition in his dungeon was load-bearing according to the System's physics. Every wall he had designated as non-structural was, in fact, carrying weight. The schematic was wrong. The physics were right. And the physics had been right the whole time.

Gerald stood up. His legs felt unsteady. He was not unsteady. He was fifty-four years old and he had been standing for fourteen hours and he was tired. That was all.

He pulled up the Hearing preparation screen. The Inspector General's thesis submission window was still open. He could request the thesis early, with a Form 19-F, and he had filed that form six hours ago. The response was pending.

He filed a second Form 19-F. Requesting the physics engine's raw load-path data for the Inspector General's dungeon.

The response came back immediately. The Inspector General's dungeon was a single room. One room, forty feet by forty feet, with no walls, no columns, no partitions. Just a floor and a ceiling and the space between them.

Load path: THROUGH. Everything. The floor, the ceiling, the air itself. The entire structure was load-bearing. There was no non-structural element in the Inspector General's dungeon because there was no structure to designate as non-structural. The room existed in a state of total compliance because there was nothing to be non-compliant with.

Gerald stared at the data. The Inspector General had designed a dungeon that could not be cited for structural violations because it had no structural elements to violate. A single room. No walls. No columns. No partitions. No load paths that could be miscalculated.

And Gerald had cited it.

He had cited a room that had no walls for having a wall that was not load-bearing. He had cited the Inspector General for a violation that did not exist.

Gerald sat down again. The clipboard was in his lap. The page said LOAD PATH ANOMALY. EASTERN CORRIDOR. UNRATED WALL.

He turned to a fresh page. He wrote: THE INSPECTOR GENERAL'S DUNGEON HAS NO STRUCTURAL ELEMENTS.

He wrote: I CITED A ROOM FOR HAVING A WALL.

He wrote: THE HEARING WILL BE ABOUT WHETHER A ROOM WITH NO WALLS CAN HAVE A WALL VIOLATION.

He wrote: IF I ARGUE THAT MY WALL IS LOAD-BEARING, I ADMIT MY SCHEMATIC IS WRONG. IF I ADMIT MY SCHEMATIC IS WRONG, I ADMIT MY COMPLIANCE RATING IS WRONG. IF I ADMIT MY COMPLIANCE RATING IS WRONG, I ADMIT THE CITATION IS VOID.

He wrote: THE CITATION IS VOID.

He stared at the page. The citation was void. He had cited the Inspector General for a violation that could not exist. The Hearing would proceed, and the Inspector General would argue that the citation was invalid, and the System would agree, and Gerald would lose.

Unless.

Unless the wall was real.

Unless the load path was real. Unless the System's physics engine, which the Inspector General had designed, was telling Gerald something the Inspector General had not intended. The physics said the wall was load-bearing. The physics said every partition in Gerald's dungeon was load-bearing. The physics were the Inspector General's own rules, and those rules were saying that Gerald's dungeon had structural elements that the schematic did not show.

The Inspector General had written the physics. The physics said the wall was load-bearing. The Inspector General could not argue against his own physics without contradicting the System itself.

Gerald uncapped his pen. He wrote: THE PHYSICS ENGINE IS THE INSPECTOR GENERAL'S OWN RULES. THE PHYSICS ENGINE SAYS MY WALL IS LOAD-BEARING. THEREFORE MY CITATION IS VALID. THEREFORE THE WALL IS LOAD-BEARING. THEREFORE MY SCHEMATIC IS WRONG.

He stopped. He read what he had written.

He had just proven that his own schematic was wrong. He had just admitted that his dungeon was built incorrectly. That every partition he had designated as non-structural was actually carrying load. That his entire structural filing was a fiction.

And the Inspector General would know this. The Inspector General would see Gerald's argument coming. The Inspector General had designed the physics to make this argument possible, which meant the Inspector General had designed the physics to make Gerald's argument self-defeating.

Win the argument, admit your dungeon is structurally unsound. Lose the argument, lose the Citation.

Gerald looked at the wall. The wall that was not on his schematic. The wall that was load-bearing. The wall that the System's physics said was real.

He pressed his palm against it. It was warm again. Not from the load test. He had not turned the load test back on. It was warm because something inside it was generating heat.

He pulled the core drill from his belt. He walked to the wall. He drilled a hole. Quarter-inch. Eight inches deep. He pulled the bit out.

The core sample was not concrete.

It was a material he did not recognize. Dense, dark, with a faint iridescence at the edges. It was warm in his hand. It pulsed once, like a heartbeat, and then went still.

Gerald looked at the core sample. He looked at the hole in the wall. He looked at the schematic, which said 8-inch concrete block.

He pulled up the System's material database. He searched for the material. No results.

He searched by density. No results.

He searched by thermal conductivity. One result. The entry was blank. The material code was there. The name was not. The description was not. Just a code and a blank field where the name should be.

Gerald held the core sample up to the light. The iridescence shifted as he turned it. The pulse had not returned. The sample was inert. Cold now.

He looked at the wall. The hole was a clean circle. Inside it, past the eight inches of concrete block, was a layer of the unknown material. It was not part of the wall. It was behind the wall. It was inside the wall's structure, hidden, and it was the reason the wall was load-bearing.

It was the reason the physics engine said THROUGH.

Gerald pulled up the material database again. He searched for the code. He searched for the blank field. He searched for any entry with no name.

One more result. A different code. Same blank field. Same iridescence.

He searched for all entries with blank names.

Forty-seven results. Forty-seven materials in the System's database that had no name, no description, and no application. Forty-seven materials that the Inspector General had designed into the System's physics and never told anyone about.

Gerald looked at his dungeon. Four thousand square feet. Every wall. Every floor. Every ceiling. He had built it all himself, with his own hands, with his own measurements, with his own filings.

But the walls were not what he had built.

He pulled up the Hearing screen. The Inspector General's thesis submission window closed. The Hearing was in thirty hours.

Gerald looked at the core sample in his hand. He looked at the forty-seven blank entries. He looked at his schematic, which said concrete block.

He uncapped his pen. He wrote on the clipboard: I DID NOT BUILD THIS WALL.

He underlined it once. He did not cross it out.
