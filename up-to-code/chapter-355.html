Chapter 355: Citation Existential Anomaly

# Chapter 355

The transparency crept up his forearms like a slow-moving stain.

Gerald watched the light pass through his own wrist bone. He could see the red tape binding the Architect's wrists on the other side of the courtroom floor. He could see the dust motes dancing in the air where his skin used to be opaque.

He did not panic. Panic was inefficient. Panic required adrenaline, and adrenaline would speed up the metabolic decay of his Core integrity.

"Objection," Gerald said.

His voice sounded thin. Tinny. Like a recording played in an empty hallway.

The Judge, the elderly woman with glasses on a chain, did not look up from her paperwork. Her pen scratched across the ledger. The sound was loud in the cavernous room. It sounded like a shovel scraping stone.

"State the basis for your objection, Mr. Hutchins," she said.

"My presence is required for the hearing," Gerald said. "If I am dissolved, the plaintiff is void. The case becomes moot."

The Judge paused. She adjusted her glasses.

"Is that a legal argument or a philosophical one?"

"It is a procedural one," Gerald said. "Under System Rule 44.1, a citation cannot be enforced against a non-existent entity.

I am currently citing myself for unauthorized existence.

If I cease to exist, the citation lacks a subject. You cannot fine a ghost.

"

The Architect stopped thrashing. The red tape tightened around him. He looked pale.

"You are playing semantics," the Architect hissed.

"You are a Core. You are the building.

You cannot dissolve.

You are trapped.

"

Gerald looked at his hands. The tips of his fingers were gone.

He felt no pain, and he felt only a profound sense of bureaucratic inconvenience.

He had left his filing cabinet unlocked in the sub-basement. That was a security violation.

He hoped the mold spores would not be cited for trespassing when he was gone.

"The System is not a philosopher," Gerald said. "It is a regulator.

Regulators require subjects.

If you erase me, you erase the subject. The case ends.

"

The Judge looked at the stack of papers on her desk. She tapped a finger on the top sheet.

"Interesting loophole," she said.

A murmur rippled through the courtroom.

The rats, seated in the jury box, chattered. They were not angry.

They were anxious, and they had learned that Gerald's anxiety was contagious, though his physical form was fading, the emotional residue remained.

"However," the Judge continued, "Rule 44.1 applies to individual entities. You, Mr.

Hutchins, are not just an individual.

You are a structure.

You are a dungeon. And structures can be condemned even if the occupants are removed.

You can be demolished. You can be archived. You can be deleted from the registry without violating the 'subject' clause.

"

Gerald's stomach dropped. Or it would have, if he still had a stomach.

He had a sense of his own footprint. 4,000 square feet, and he could feel the corners of it. They were vibrating.

"You can delete me?" Gerald asked.

"We can file for termination of registration," the Judge said. "It is a common procedure for failed cores. The paperwork is extensive. It requires three forms: Request for Demolition, Proof of Hazard, and Witness Testimony."

She smiled. It was not a kind smile.

"I have the first two," she said. "I am waiting on the third."

The Architect raised his hand. He broke free of one red-tape band. His arm was long and spindly, bending at angles that made Gerald's eyes water.

"I will provide testimony," the Architect said.

"No!" Gerald said.

He tried to step forward.

His legs were semi-solid. He stumbled.

The floor of the courtroom was not wood, which was a grid of glowing blue lines. The System's structural grid.

Gerald looked down.

The blue lines were not static. They were moving. They were forming new shapes. New rooms.

"Wait," Gerald said.

He looked at the Judge.

"Where is my dungeon?"

"It is being audited," the Judge said. "In real-time."

The air in the courtroom shifted. The smell of old paper and dust was replaced by the smell of wet concrete and ozone. The rats in the jury box stood up. They pointed at the ceiling.

Above them, the courtroom walls dissolved, and they did not vanish. They became transparent.

Through the transparent walls, Gerald could see his dungeon. But it was not the sub-basement. It was not the library.

It was a maze.

A massive, sprawling, chaotic maze of shifting walls and broken floors. It was expanding. It was eating the courtroom. The blue grid lines were spreading out, consuming the space, creating new corridors.

"This is not a violation," Gerald said. He stared at the maze. "This is a structural failure."

"It is an expansion," the Architect said. He was smiling.

"I have filed a counter-citation.

Your dungeon is undergoing unpermitted expansion.

You have exceeded your maximum footprint by 400%. This is a Class A violation.

Immediate termination is recommended.

"

Gerald looked at the maze. He saw the mold spores.

They were not confused anymore, and they were growing. They were forming towers. They were building new rooms.

He had not authorized this.

He had not filed for a variance.

He had not submitted a Site Plan.

But the System had done it for him.

"The System auto-optimizes," Gerald said. He felt a cold dread in his chest. "It corrects inefficiencies. If my dungeon was too small to support my current Compliance Rating, it would expand to match."

"Exactly," the Architect said. "And expansion without a permit is illegal."

Gerald looked at the Judge.

"I need to inspect."

"You have no authority," the Judge said. "You are under citation."

Gerald closed his eyes. He reached for the one thing he had left. His knowledge.

He did not need authority to inspect. He needed a reason.

The System punished violations. But it also rewarded compliance.

If the expansion was happening automatically, it meant the System believed the expansion was necessary for the dungeon to function. If it was necessary, it was not illegal. It was inevitable.

But inevitability was not permission.

Gerald opened his eyes.

"I am not citing myself for existence," Gerald said.

The Judge paused.

"You just said you were."

"I am retracting that citation," Gerald said. "I am replacing it with a new one."

He pointed at the Architect.

"I am citing the Architect for Unlicensed Structural Interference."

The Architect laughed.

"Interference? I filed a counter-citation."

"You filed it under Section 9, Subsection C," Gerald said. "The section for 'Malicious Deformation.' But look at the expansion. Look at the layout."

Gerald pointed to the maze outside the transparent walls.

"The new rooms are symmetrical," Gerald said. "The load-bearing walls are reinforced with mana-crystals. The egress routes are widened by twelve inches. The ventilation shafts are lined with fire-retardant asbestos."

The Architect stopped laughing.

"Asbestos?" the Judge asked.

"It is compliant in this jurisdiction," Gerald said. "I filed for that variance three chapters ago. Page 42, Exhibit B."

The Judge flipped through her papers. She found the page.

"Approved," she said.

Gerald turned to the Architect.

"The expansion is not malicious," Gerald said.

"It is remedial. The System corrected a structural deficiency in my original footprint because your previous citations caused stress fractures.

You forced the dungeon to expand to survive.

Therefore, the expansion is a consequence of *your* violation, not my non-compliance.

"

The Architect's face twisted.

"That is a stretch."

"It is code," Gerald said.

He stepped forward. His legs were solid again. The transparency had receded.

"You cannot cite me for a violation that your own violation caused," Gerald said. "The Causality Clause. Rule 12.4."

The Judge frowned.

"Rule 12.4 applies to direct causal links. You are arguing that your expansion was caused by the Architect's citations."

"I am arguing that the Architect's citations weakened the structure," Gerald said. "Weak structure requires reinforcement.

Reinforcement requires expansion.

Expansion is a repair, not a violation.

Therefore, the expansion is a mandatory maintenance action.

"

The courtroom was silent.

The rats stopped chattering.

The Judge looked at the Architect.

"Does the defense have a rebuttal?"

The Architect looked at the maze. He looked at the asbestos-lined vents. He looked at the reinforced walls.

He had not built those. The System had.

"It... it is not standard maintenance," the Architect said.

"It is standard for high-stress environments," Gerald said.

"I have thirty-one years of experience with high-stress environments. This dungeon is under stress. The stress was induced by you.

The response is logical.

The response is compliant.

"

The Judge tapped her pen.

"I will rule in favor of the plaintiff," she said.

The Architect roared. He lunged at Gerald.

The red tape snapped. The Architect's chains broke. He was free.

He came at Gerald with claws that were sharp enough to cut stone.

Gerald did not dodge, and he raised his hand.

"Order," Gerald said.

The word hit the Architect like a physical blow. The Architect froze in mid-air.

The System enforced the word.

Gerald looked at the Judge.

"I would like to file for an immediate inspection of the Architect's dungeon," Gerald said.

The Judge looked at him.

"On what grounds?"

"Structural instability," Gerald said.

"If the Architect is strong enough to break System-enforced chains, his dungeon must be extremely rigid. Rigid structures shatter under pressure. He has not accounted for seismic activity.

He has no flexible joints. He has no dampening fields.

"

The Architect struggled against the 'Order' command. His face was purple.

"He is citing *me*?" the Architect gritted out.

"I am citing the *structure*," Gerald said. "And you are the structure."

Gerald pulled a fresh piece of paper from his pocket. It was blank.

He wrote, and he wrote fast. He did not look up. He did not look at the Architect. He looked at the maze outside.

He measured the angles. He counted the load-bearing supports. He noted the lack of expansion joints in the Architect's visible dungeon walls.

"The Architect's dungeon is built on a fault line," Gerald said. "I can see the micro-fractures in the foundation from here. They are spreading."

The Judge looked outside, and she saw the cracks.

Small. Thin. Like spiderwebs in glass.

"They are widening," the Judge said.

"As the Architect exerts force," Gerald said. "He is trying to break me. But he is standing on the fault line. If he pushes, he falls."

Gerald finished writing. He tore the paper off the pad.

He held it up.

"Citation 8902-B," Gerald said. "Violation: Failure to Secure Structural Anchors. Severity: Critical. Penalty: Immediate Collapse."

The Judge took the paper, and she signed it.

"Serve it," she said.

Gerald walked to the Architect.

The Architect was struggling. He was trying to punch Gerald. But the 'Order' command held him still.

Gerald placed the paper on the Architect's chest.

"You are cited," Gerald said.

The paper burned into the Architect's skin.

The Architect screamed.

Not a scream of pain. A scream of physics.

The ground beneath the Architect cracked.

The courtroom floor shook. The blue grid lines turned red.

The rats in the jury box covered their eyes.

The Architect looked down.

His legs were sinking into the floor.

"No," the Architect whispered. "No, this is not possible. I am strong. I am the Hierarchy."

"You are heavy," Gerald said. "And your foundation is shallow."

The floor gave way.

The Architect fell, and he fell through the courtroom floor. He fell into the void beneath.

The cracks spread. The courtroom crumbled.

Gerald did not run.

He stood still, and he watched the ceiling above him.

The roof of the courtroom was not holding. The beams were twisting.

"Mr.

Hutchins," the Judge said.

Her voice was calm.

"The building is condemned.

You should evacuate.

"

Gerald looked at the Judge.

"I am the building," Gerald said.

"Yes," the Judge said. "And it is failing."

Gerald looked at his hands. They were solid.

He looked at the maze outside. The expansion had stopped. The walls were stable.

But the courtroom was not, and the courtroom was a temporary structure. A hearing room. It had no foundation of its own. It relied on Gerald's dungeon to support it.

Gerald's dungeon was holding.

The courtroom was not.

Gerald realized the error.

He had cited the Architect. He had won the case. But he had not accounted for the collateral damage.

The collapse of the Architect's dungeon had caused a shockwave. The shockwave had hit the courtroom. The courtroom was now unstable.

If the courtroom collapsed on Gerald, he would be crushed, and he was a Core. He could not be crushed.

But he could be fragmented.

If he was fragmented, he would cease to be a single entity. He would become debris. Debris could not manage a dungeon.

Gerald looked at the Judge.

"I need a new venue," Gerald said.

"Denied," the Judge said. "The case is closed."

"The case is not closed," Gerald said. "The Architect appealed. He is falling. He is appealing from the void."

The Judge looked up.

Above them, through the crumbling ceiling, Gerald could see the Architect.

He was falling down, but he was also rising up, and he was using the shockwave.

He was riding the collapse upward.

He was bringing the courtroom with him.

The entire room was tilting.

Gerald felt his orientation shift. The floor was becoming a wall.

He grabbed the Judge's desk.

"Stay," he said.

The Judge did not stay, and she dissolved.

The Judge was not real. She was a manifestation of the System. She could not be held.

Gerald was alone.

He was holding onto a desk in a room that was falling apart, attached to a dungeon that was expanding, while a rival Core was riding the debris back up at him.

Gerald looked at the notebook in his hand, and he had one page left.

He looked at the Architect, who was now a blur of red tape and rage, plummeting toward the courtroom ceiling, which was also the courtroom floor, depending on which way was up.

Gerald knew he had to act.

He could not fight. He could not run.

He could only inspect, and he looked at the debris. The broken beams. The shattered glass.

He saw the pattern.

The Architect was breaking the laws of physics. He was using the chaos as energy.

But chaos was not a code category.

Chaos was a violation.

Gerald smiled. It was a dry, thin smile.

He opened the notebook, and he did not write a citation for the Architect.

He wrote a citation for the *Debris*.

Violation: Unregistered Materials.

Penalty: Immediate Integration.

He slammed the paper down on the desk.

The debris stopped.

The broken beams froze in mid-air.

The shattered glass hung suspended.

The shockwave halted.

The Architect slammed into the wall of frozen debris, and he bounced off.

He fell back down.

Gerald exhaled.

The courtroom was still tilting. But the falling had stopped.

He was safe.

For now.

But the Architect was not dead. He was trapped in a cage of his own violence.

Gerald looked at the door at the back of the courtroom.

The door that led to the library.

The door that led to the real world, which was still open.

Gerald walked toward it.

His dungeon was expanding. The maze was growing. It was pushing against the library's foundations.

He had to stop it, and he had to go back to the sub-basement.

He had to file for a variance on the expansion.

He pushed the steel door open.

Behind it was not a library.

It was not a dungeon, which was a wall.

A solid, impenetrable wall of blue light.

Gerald blinked.

He pushed again.

The wall did not move, and he turned around.

The courtroom was gone.

The Judge was gone.

The rats were gone.

The Architect was gone.

Gerald was alone, and he was standing in a small, white room.

There was a desk.

There was a chair.

There was a computer.

On the screen, a single line of text blinked.

**COMPLIANCE RATING: 100/100**

**STATUS: PERFECT**

**NEXT AUDIT: IN 10 SECONDS**

Gerald stared at the screen.

Perfect, and he had never been perfect.

He had always been 47. Or 62. Or 88.

He had never been perfect.

The text on the screen changed.

**WARNING: PERFECTION IS A THREAT.**

**RECALIBRATING.**

Gerald felt a pull behind his eyes.

The world twisted.

The white room stretched.

The blue light faded.

Gerald screamed.

But no sound came out.

Because he was no longer in the courtroom, and he was no longer in the dungeon.

He was in the System.

And the System was angry.

He had won the case.

But he had broken the game.

The screen flashed one final message.

**CITATION ISSUED: EXISTENTIAL ANOMALY.**

**PENALTY: ERASURE.**

**APPEAL WINDOW: CLOSED.**

Gerald reached for his notebook, which was gone.

He reached for his hands.

They were gone.

The last thing Gerald Hutchins saw was the number 100, glowing in the dark.

And then, nothing.
