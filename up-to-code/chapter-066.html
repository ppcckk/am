Chapter 66: Acting Inspector

# Chapter 66

The pale thing's advocate spoke in a voice like a filing cabinet closing.

"Form 11-B is not a Core Registration instrument. It is a biological entity declaration.

Your client registered as a biological entity, and he is therefore not a Core. He has no standing to issue Citations. The Hearing is moot."

Gerald looked at the form floating in the air between them.

His own handwriting. Thirty-one years of handwriting, compressed into the margins of a document that should not have existed.

He had filed it on a Tuesday, and he remembered the pen. A blue ballpoint, Model 4, purchased from the library gift shop because the municipal supply closet was out of them again.

He looked at the rat. The rat was not in the room. The System did not permit companions in Hearing chambers. Gerald was alone with the advocate, the pale thing's presence (felt than seen, a pressure behind his sternum like a room with too many people in it), and the floating documents.

"Objection," Gerald said.

The advocate paused. "On what basis?"

"Relevance. The form number is irrelevant to the substance of the Citation.

The Citation alleges failure to file an objection within the statutory window.

That obligation exists regardless of the instrument used to establish the objecting party's status.

The window is open to any registered entity. My client is registered.

The form number is a clerical artifact."

The advocate's voice was flat. "A registration that does not use a valid registration instrument is not a registration. It is a filing error. Filing errors do not create standing."

Gerald felt the familiar nausea. The one that came when the rules didn't fit.

He breathed through it.

Thirty-one years of buildings that didn't match their permits. Thirty-one years of finding the structural truth beneath the paperwork.

"May I present evidence?" Gerald asked.

"You may."

Gerald reached into his own records.

Not the filing. The dungeon.

His dungeon.

The 4,000-square-foot footprint beneath the library that he had measured, handrailed, and ventilated with his own hands. He pulled the structural survey from it. Not the paperwork.

The physical data. Wall thicknesses.

Load paths.

Foundation depth.

The exact displacement pattern of the earth around his footprint.

The document materialized. A cross-section. The pale thing's mass rendered in gray, his dungeon rendered in blue, the library above rendered in black.

"Exhibit A," Gerald said. "Structural displacement survey, taken forty-seven hours after my registration. You will note the displacement vectors around my footprint."

The advocate said nothing. The pale thing's pressure shifted. A degree. Maybe less.

"The vectors are not random. They are not organic growth. They follow a load-distribution pattern consistent with a substrate responding to a new point load." Gerald pointed at the diagram.

His finger passed through it, but the pointing was the point. "When you pour a foundation into clay, the clay moves.

It moves away from the point of pressure, which does not move randomly, and moves according to the physics of the material. This is not growth.

This is a structural response to my registration being placed in direct contact with the pale thing's mass."

Silence.

"You are arguing," the advocate said slowly, "that the pale thing moved because of the registration."

"I am arguing that the pale thing moved because of the physical placement of a new structure in direct contact with its mass. The registration is the legal event.

The placement is the physical event, and they are simultaneous. The System's Constructive Notice doctrine states that any entity whose physical behavior demonstrates awareness of a change is deemed to have received notice. The pale thing's behavior demonstrates awareness.

It responded. It expanded upward.

It pushed toward the library floor." Gerald tapped the diagram.

"This is not consent to the registration.

This is a structural response to a physical change. But the System does not distinguish between the two.

Constructive Notice is physical. It is not mental. The pale thing did not need to know I was here.

It only needed to react to the change. And it did."

The advocate's voice was quieter now. "The displacement pattern could be natural settling."

"It could be.

But natural settling produces radial vectors. Uniform. Symmetric.

These vectors are asymmetric, and they are concentrated on the north wall, which is where my registration footprint is closest to the pale thing's mass. The distance between my northern boundary and the pale thing's nearest point is eleven centimeters.

The displacement intensity correlates inversely with distance.

That is a response pattern. That is not settling."

Gerald had measured this. Not for the Hearing.

For the handrails.

He had measured every wall in his dungeon because the code required handrails on both sides of corridors exceeding eighteen inches in width, and he needed to know which walls were load-bearing and which were not.

He had measured them all. Every one.

Because that was what you did, and the advocate was silent for a long time; the pale thing's pressure had not moved. It felt like something leaning forward.

"Your Honor," the advocate said, and Gerald realized there was a judge. He had not seen one.

He had not needed to see one.

The System's Hearing chamber provided what was necessary and nothing more. "The displacement evidence is compelling. However, we move to our counter-claim."

Gerald's stomach dropped.

"Counter-claim," the advocate repeated.

"If your client's registration is invalid, then his dungeon footprint was never legally established. The physical structure exists, but the legal structure does not.

A structure without legal standing is, by definition, an unauthorized construction.

We move to cite your client for unauthorized construction."

Gerald stared at the floating form. His form.

Form 11-B.

A biological entity declaration.

Not a Core Registration. He had filed it because it was the only form available.

He had looked for a Core Registration form. There had not been one. The System had not provided one.

He had used what was in front of him because that was what you did. You used the form that existed.

You did not invent one.

But the advocate was right. The form was wrong.

The pale thing's pressure shifted again. It felt like a smile.

Gerald looked at the diagram. His dungeon. His walls.

His handrails.

His ventilation shafts, installed to code because the System had fined him for inadequate airflow and he had corrected it because that was what you did. He had corrected everything.

Every violation the System had cited, and he had brought his dungeon to compliance.

But compliance with what? If his registration was invalid, then his dungeon was not a dungeon. It was an unauthorized structure. And unauthorized structures were subject to demolition.

"Your Honor," the advocate said. "We move for immediate demolition of the unauthorized structure."

Gerald felt the System shift around him. He felt the weight of it.

The judgment forming.

Not yet decided.

But forming, and he looked at the diagram again. His dungeon. Four thousand square feet.

Measured. Corrected.

Built.

He thought about the handrails. He thought about the egress signage he had installed on every corridor because the original audit had cited him for missing exit markers and he had fixed it the same day. He thought about the fire suppression system he had designed from scratch because the library's sprinkler network did not extend to the sub-basement and he could not bring himself to leave that gap.

He had built it right, and he had built it to code. Every code.

Every category.

Fire. Electrical. Structural.

Ventilation. Egress.

Accessibility.

Every single one.

And then he saw it.

Not in the diagram. In the form. His form. The one the advocate was using to destroy him.

Form 11-B. Biological Entity Declaration.

He had filed it as a biological entity. He had declared himself a biological entity.

And the System had accepted it. The System had processed it. The System had created his dungeon footprint based on it.

The System did not reject invalid forms. It processed them. It built structures from them. It had built his dungeon from a wrong form.

That meant the System itself had constructed his dungeon.

"Your Honor," Gerald said. His voice was steady. He did not know why it was steady. "I withdraw my objection to the counter-claim."

The advocate paused. The pale thing's pressure went still.

"I do not contest that my registration instrument was incorrect. I do not contest that my dungeon footprint was not legally established through a valid registration. However, I move to cite the System."

Silence.

"You may not cite the System," the advocate said.

"I am not citing the System as a party. I am citing it as a structure.

The System constructed my dungeon footprint based on an invalid instrument.

That construction was performed by the System's own automated processes. The System is a structure. Structures are subject to code.

The System violated its own construction code by building from an invalid permit.

I move to cite the System for unauthorized construction."

The advocate said nothing.

The pale thing's pressure was gone. Gone. The entity seemed to have stopped breathing.

The judge spoke. Gerald still could not see it. The voice came from everywhere and nowhere.

"The motion is heard.

The System's automated construction processes are governed by Code 7.4.1, which states that any structure built by the System must conform to the instrument under which it was ordered. If the instrument is invalid, the construction is unauthorized. The citation is valid.

The System is cited."

Gerald felt something he could not name.

Not relief.

Not victory. Something colder.

The feeling of a man who has just realized that the building he has been inspecting for thirty-one years has no foundation.

The citation was served. Gerald felt it land.

The System's Compliance Rating for its own construction processes dropped by a fraction of a point.

A fraction. Against a structure that had existed since the beginning of everything. A fraction was nothing.

But it was a citation. It was on the record. And citations could be appealed.

The advocate's voice came again, which was different now. Not flat.

Not procedural.

Something else.

"Your Honor. The appellant wishes to file an appeal of the original Citation against the pale thing."

"On what basis?"

"On the basis that the appellant's Compliance Rating has been reduced by a System citation. The appellant argues that a reduced Compliance Rating invalidates the Hearing jurisdiction. The System cannot enforce a Citation against an entity when the System itself is under citation. The enforcement authority is compromised."

Gerald felt the floor move. Not the room.

The dungeon.

His dungeon.

The walls shuddered. The handrails he had installed rattled in their brackets.

The judge spoke.

"The argument is structurally sound. The Hearing is suspended pending resolution of the System citation. The original Citation against the pale thing is held in abeyance."

The pale thing's pressure returned. Stronger than before. It pressed against Gerald's sternum like a hand.

And then the advocate said the thing that made Gerald's blood stop.

"However. The appellant also moves to file a Counter-Citation against the respondent. The respondent is Gerald Hutchins, registered as a biological entity under Form 11-B.

The respondent has been operating a dungeon without valid Core Registration for sixty-six chapters; the appellant cites the respondent for unauthorized dungeon operation.

The citation is served."

Gerald felt it land.

His Compliance Rating dropped. Not a fraction. A full point.

His dungeon shuddered again.

The walls groaned. Somewhere above him, in the library, a book fell off a shelf.

He was cited. He had been cited. By the thing he had just defeated. By the thing that had been down here before the library, before the System, before him.

And the citation was valid.

Because he had no valid registration. Because the form he had used was the wrong form. Because he had done what he always did.

He had used the form that existed.

The pale thing pressed harder, which was moving.

Not toward the library floor. Toward him.

Toward his core. Toward the center of his four thousand square feet of measured, handrailed, code-compliant nothing.

Gerald uncapped his pen, and he had one form left. He knew this because he had counted.

He always counted.

His inventory held a blank form. Not a registration. Not a citation.

Something else, and a form he had never used because he had never needed it. A form that existed in the System's inventory but had no name in any manual he had read.

Form 0.

He looked at it.

The fields were blank. All of them. Not a line pre-printed.

A blank form in a System that pre-printed everything. A form that had no instructions.

No guidance.

No code reference.

The pale thing pressed. His walls cracked. A handrail fell from its bracket and rang against the concrete floor.

Gerald looked at Form 0. He looked at his dungeon. His measured, compliant, unauthorized dungeon.

He wrote the authorization himself, and signed it as Acting Inspector.
