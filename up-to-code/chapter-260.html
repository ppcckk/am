Chapter 260: The Keystone Rat

# Chapter 260

The bullet struck the concrete six inches from Gerald's left hip.

Dust bloomed in the grey air. The sound was deafening, a sharp crack that shattered the fragile illusion of order.

Gerald did not flinch.

His body had locked into the posture of a man who had spent three decades standing in front of angry contractors. Feet shoulder-width apart. Arms hanging limp.

Eyes fixed on the muzzle of the weapon.

The officer's face was pale beneath the grime. Sweat beaded on his forehead.

The gun shook in his grip, which was not professional trembling. It was the tremor of a man who had just fired at a ghost and missed.

Gerald looked down at the crater in the pavement. Then he looked up.

"Sir, discharging a firearm in a residential zone constitutes a Class A misdemeanor," Gerald said. His voice was dry.

Flat.

The voice of a memo read aloud in a sterile conference room.

"I have not yet filed my incident report, but I am prepared to do so now."

The officer blinked. The gun wavered.

"What?"

Sarah grabbed Gerald's arm. Her fingers dug into his sleeve, hard enough to bruise.

"Move," she hissed.

"He's not hearing you. He's a citizen.

You're a suspect.

"

"I am not a suspect," Gerald corrected, though the word tasted like ash. "I am a public servant."

On the ground, the man with black eyes sitted up. The movement was wrong. Joints snapped back into place with wet, cracking sounds.

He was not a man anymore, and he was a vessel. A conduit for something that did not obey the laws of physics, let alone the municipal code.

Gerald needed data. He needed a metric. He needed to measure the threat so he could regulate it.

But the blue glow of his Core was gone, and the interface was absent. There were no stats. No health bar.

No mana pool.

There was only the street. The broken asphalt. The shattered windows. The unconscious bystanders.

Gerald closed his eyes, and he breathed in.

The air tasted of ozone and burnt copper.

He opened his eyes.

The grey haze flickered. For a second, just a second, the world snapped into high-definition focus. The cracks in the sidewalk glowed with a faint, amber light. The structural integrity of the nearby lamppost flashed in his mind's eye: *Load bearing compromised by 14 percent due to thermal expansion.*

It was not magic. It was observation.

Gerald Hutchins did not need a System interface to see code violations. He had eyes. He had thirty-one years of experience. And he had a pen.

He picked it up from the asphalt.

The officer saw the movement. He raised the weapon again.

"Drop it!"

Gerald did not drop it. He uncapped it.

"Sir, you are citing the wrong statute," Gerald said.

He pointed the pen at the officer.

"You are subject to local law. I am subject to local law.

But this," he gestured to the man on the ground, "is not a citizen. This is an unlicensed entity operating within city limits without a Certificate of Occupancy."

The man on the ground stood up. He was seven feet tall.

His skin was grey, peeling away to reveal pulsing black veins underneath.

He opened his mouth, and a sound emerged that was not a voice. It was the sound of tearing metal.

"Fire," the officer screamed.

He pulled the trigger.

The bullet hit the entity in the chest. It stopped. The impact didn't even make the creature stagger. It looked down at the hole in its torso, confused by the lack of pain.

Gerald looked at the bullet impact site. He looked at the surrounding pavement. He looked at the fire hydrant twenty feet away.

He didn't have a fire suppression system. He didn't have a sprinkler grid. He didn't have fire code compliance.

But he had a fire hydrant. And he had a man in a uniform who was terrified and trained to shoot.

"The fire hydrant is out of order," Gerald shouted. "Violation 4-B. Immediate remediation required."

The officer stared at him.

"What?"

"Shoot the hydrant," Gerald said. "Now."

The officer hesitated.

The entity lunged.

It moved faster than a human. It crossed the twenty feet in a blur of grey motion. One hand, large and clawed, swiped through the air where Gerald's head had been.

Sarah shoved Gerald backward, and they both fell onto the broken concrete. The entity's hand passed through the space they had occupied, shattering the lamppost behind them.

Metal shrieked.

Glass rained down.

"Shoot the hydrant!" Gerald yelled, scrambling to his knees. He didn't look at the monster. He looked at the officer.

The officer fired.

The bullet struck the red paint of the fire hydrant. It ricocheted off the valve.

It wasn't enough. The entity turned. It loomed over Gerald. Black eyes focused on the pen in Gerald's hand.

*"Paper,"* the entity rasped. *"You write... on flesh."*

Gerald looked up at the towering horror. He felt the cold clarity settle in again.

He wasn't afraid, and he was annoyed.

This entity was violating Section 12, Paragraph 4: *Prohibition of Unauthorized Biological Hazards in Public Spaces.*

But he couldn't cite it. He had no authority.

He had no Core, and he had only the pen. And the pen was a tool. A tool for documentation.

Documentation was not power. Not directly. But documentation created liability. Liability created leverage.

Gerald looked at the officer. The officer was reloading. His hands were shaking so badly he dropped the magazine.

Gerald looked at Sarah. She was bleeding from a cut on her forehead. She was watching the entity, her eyes wide, her breath coming in short, sharp gasps.

She was a Core. She had a dungeon.

She had walls.

"Gerald," she whispered. "The library. We need to get to the library."

"No," Gerald said. "We need to get to the hydrant."

He stood up.

The entity paused. It tilted its head. It was waiting for him to run. It was waiting for fear.

Gerald walked toward the fire hydrant.

One step. Two steps. Three.

The officer shouted something. Gerald didn't hear it.

The entity took a step forward. The ground cracked under its weight.

Gerald reached the hydrant. He looked at the damage. The bullet had dented the valve housing. It hadn't opened it.

He looked at his pen. It was a standard-issue Bic. Black ink. Plastic barrel.

He jammed the tip of the pen into the gap between the valve handle and the housing. He twisted.

It was not a tool for opening valves. It was a tool for signing forms.

But force is force.

The valve groaned. The metal twisted.

Behind him, the entity roared. A sound like a subway train derailing.

Gerald twisted harder. His knuckles whitened. The plastic barrel of the pen cracked. Ink leaked onto his fingers, staining his skin blue.

The valve broke free.

Water exploded outward.

A jet of high-pressure water hit Gerald in the chest, knocking him backward. He hit the asphalt hard. The air left his lungs.

The water stream swept across the street. It hit the entity.

Steam hissed.

The entity shrieked. It was not water damage. It was thermal shock.

The creature's body was hot, and the water was cold. The rapid expansion of steam ruptured the skin.

The officer fired again. And again.

The bullets hit the entity, but the water was the real weapon. The sheer volume of flow, the chaotic spray, disrupted the creature's balance. It stumbled. It slipped on the wet, icy pavement.

Gerald lay on his back, gasping. Water soaked his suit. It ruined his tie. It soaked into his shoes.

He looked at the pen in his hand. It was broken. The tip was snapped off.

Useless.

But the water was flowing. The violation was being remediated. The hazard was being neutralized.

The entity slipped. It fell. It crashed into a parked car. The alarm blared.

Gerald tried to sit up. His ribs screamed in protest. He looked at the officer. The officer was staring at the water, then at Gerald, then at the monster.

"Stay down," the officer said. His voice was different now. Less aggressive. More confused.

Sarah ran to Gerald. She grabbed his arm.

"Get up. We're leaving."

Gerald looked at the water pooling around him. He looked at the officer.

"Sir," Gerald said. "You should file a report. Unauthorized use of municipal infrastructure requires a form 12-C."

"Go," the officer said. He didn't look at Gerald. He was looking at the backup sirens approaching down the block.

Sarah dragged Gerald up, and he stumbled. His left knee buckled. He had torn something.

He couldn't feel the pain yet. Adrenaline was a liar.

They ran.

They ran down the side street, away from the fire, away from the water, away from the man who was no longer a Core.

The library was three blocks away.

Gerald counted the blocks. One. Two. Three.

He couldn't feel the hum of the earth. He couldn't sense the dungeon. He couldn't sense the rats.

He was alone, and they reached the back entrance of the Ashford Municipal Library.

The heavy steel door was locked.

Sarah pressed her hand against the wood. She pushed.

Her eyes glowed with a faint, desperate amber light.

The lock clicked.

The door swung open.

Darkness rushed out to meet them.

Gerald stepped inside. The smell of old paper and dust was comforting. It was familiar. It was safe.

But it was silent.

Usually, a dungeon hummed. The walls vibrated with the presence of the Core. The air moved with the breath of the spawns.

Now, there was only the smell of mold.

Gerald walked to the center of the sub-basement. His footsteps echoed. Too loud. Too hollow.

He looked at the spot where his Core used to be, and the blue glow was gone.

The pedestal was bare.

In its place lay a single object.

It was not a Core. It was not a crystal.

It was a piece of paper.

Gerald walked toward it. Sarah hovered behind him, her hand on his shoulder.

Gerald picked up the paper, which was a receipt.

From the office supply store.

Item: Ballpoint Pen, Black, 12-pack. Total: $1.99.

Underneath the receipt, written in handwriting that was not Gerald's, was a single line.

**APPEAL DENIED.** **REASON: LACK OF SUBJECT.**

Gerald stared at the words, and he looked at his hands. They were stained with ink. They were trembling.

"Lack of subject," Gerald muttered. "I am the subject."

"Core," Sarah said. Her voice was tight. "What does it mean?"

Gerald looked at the empty pedestal. Then he looked at the door.

The door that led back to the street.

To the police. To the man with the black eyes.

It meant he had no authority, which meant he had no defense.

It meant that without the System's protection, he was just a man in a wet suit, holding a broken pen, in a building that was no longer his.

A shadow moved in the corner of the room.

Gerald turned.

It wasn't a shadow, which was a rat.

The rat was large. Too large. Its eyes were black.

It stood on its hind legs. It was four feet tall.

It looked at Gerald.

It smiled.

And then it spoke.

"Mr. Hutchins," the rat said. "Your lease has been terminated."

Gerald took a step back.

The walls of the library shuddered. The floor beneath them tilted.

Gerald looked at Sarah. Sarah looked at the floor.

"It's collapsing," she said.

Gerald looked at the ceiling. The beams were cracking. The load-bearing supports were failing.

He had spent thirty-one years inspecting buildings. He knew what happened when you removed the keystone.

The entire structure was coming down.

And the rat was holding the pen.
