Chapter 149: Press Y

# Chapter 149

The red form dangled in the Auditor's hand like a dead bird.

Gerald did not reach for it. He reached for his clipboard. The paper was damp, warped by the humidity of the collapsing high school, but the ink held. He had written the citation in real-time, under pressure, with a ballpoint pen that had begun to leak ink onto his thumb.

"You have no jurisdiction to serve me," Gerald said. His voice was flat. It lacked the tremor it had possessed five minutes ago.

The Auditor tilted his head. His face was a smooth, featureless mask of gray static, save for the mouth, which was a thin, precise line.

"Jurisdiction is determined by proximity and severity, Inspector.

You are in a restricted zone. You are unauthorized. The penalty is immediate termination of Core status."

"Termination requires a final order," Gerald said. "This is a provisional notice. I am filing a motion to stay execution of penalty pending review of the evidentiary chain."

The Auditor's hand twitched. The red form fluttered.

Gerald stepped forward.

He did not walk like a hero. He walked like a man who had been walking to the same desk in the same municipal building for thirty-one years.

His stride was measured.

His posture was rigid. He held the clipboard at chest height, a shield made of bureaucracy.

"Motion to stay," the Auditor repeated. The word sounded foreign in the System's language. It tasted like rust.

"Under System Code 44-B, Subsection 9," Gerald recited. He didn't need to look at the manual.

He had memorized the index.

"An interrupted hearing defaults to the last submitted evidence.

The last submitted evidence is the measurement of the load-bearing failure in the eastern wall. That failure constitutes a Class 3 Structural Hazard.

The hazard is ongoing. The hazard is public. The hazard is mine to regulate."

The Auditor's mouth opened. No sound came out.

Gerald clicked his pen. The click echoed in the silence of the ruined gymnasium.

"You are citing *me* for the hazard you created," Gerald said. "You collapsed the ceiling.

You exposed the rebar.

You violated the public safety code. I am issuing a counter-citation."

The Auditor looked at the clipboard. He looked at Gerald. The gray static of his face rippled.

For a long moment, nothing happened. The dust motes danced in the shaft of light that pierced the hole in the roof.

Then, the System pinged.

It was not the chime of a victory. It was the harsh, grinding noise of a gear slipping.

**[Hearing Status: Suspended.]** **[Counter-Citation Received: Class 3 Structural Hazard.]** **[Processing...]**

The Auditor stepped back. He lowered the red form. It dissolved into pixels.

Gerald exhaled.

His knees locked. He forced them to stay straight.

If he sat down now, the System might register the collapse as an admission of guilt.

He had to stand. He had to remain an object of the code.

"Go," the Auditor said. The voice was not static anymore.

It was human, and it was tired.

"Leave the zone.

You are no longer protected. You are merely... tolerated."

Gerald turned. He walked toward the exit. The hallway was filled with debris.

Rebar stuck out of the concrete like broken teeth. He stepped over them.

He did not look back at the Core that had died behind him.

He looked at the walls.

He looked for cracks.

There were many.

***

The return to the sub-basement was a transition from chaos to silence.

The library's basement smelled of old paper and mildew.

It smelled of home. Gerald placed his clipboard on his desk.

The desk was a makeshift structure, cobbled together from pallets and a door he had salvaged from the ruins of the first raid.

It was not up to code. He made a note to fix it.

He sat in his chair. It was a swivel chair, missing one wheel. It wobbled when he leaned forward.

Gerald leaned forward.

He pulled a fresh sheet of paper from the stack. The System provided the paper.

It was always white, always 8.5 by 11 inches, always heavy stock.

Gerald hated it.

It was too smooth. It slid off the desk if he wasn't careful.

He uncapped his pen.

**FORM 12-C: INCIDENT REPORT** **Date:** October 14, Year 3 **Subject:** Unauthorized Structural Collapse / Core Elimination **Reporting Officer:** Gerald Hutchins, Inspector Level 4

Gerald wrote. His handwriting was small, cramped, and perfectly legible. He did not write about the dragon that had tried to eat him.

He did not write about the Auditor who had tried to erase him. He wrote about the rebar.

*Exhibit A: Failure of lateral support in Sector 4, Zone B. The load-bearing column was compromised by external thermal stress. The resulting collapse created a tripping hazard for both spawned entities and external intruders. This violates Ashford Municipal Ordinance 7.2, which mandates a minimum ceiling height of eight feet and clear egress paths.*

He paused. He looked at the word "tripping." It was too weak.

He crossed it out.

*...created a catastrophic failure mode that rendered all subsequent spawn points inaccessible.*

Better. Specific.

He continued writing.

He described the water leak he had ignored for three chapters because the mold spores were useful for stealth. He described the rat. The rat was currently sitting on the desk, cleaning its whiskers.

It had not filed a Form 11-B.

It was still unlicensed.

Gerald did not cite the rat. He had bigger problems.

The System notification arrived on the desk. It appeared as a slip of paper, identical to the ones he wrote.

**[NOTICE: Appeal Deadline Extended.]** **[Reason: Complexity of Evidence.]** **[Time Remaining: 4 Hours.]**

Gerald frowned.

The Auditor had not withdrawn the citation against him. He had suspended it. That meant the debt was still owed.

It meant the System was still watching.

A knock came at the door.

Not a knock on the dungeon entrance. A knock on the physical door of the library sub-basement, the heavy steel door that separated the dungeon from the rest of the building.

Gerald stood up. He adjusted his tie. He checked his watch. It was 3:14 AM.

He walked to the door. He opened it.

Standing in the hallway was a man in a suit.

He was young.

Too young to be a building inspector. He held a tablet in his hand.

His face was pale, and he was sweating.

"Inspector Hutchins?" the man asked.

"Gerald," Gerald corrected. "Who are you?"

"I'm from the Office of Inspector General."

Gerald's hand tightened on the doorframe. The wood splintered. He didn't notice.

"You're early," Gerald said.

"The OIG doesn't keep time," the man said. "We keep schedules. You have a hearing. In forty minutes."

"I have four hours."

"The clock started when you left the zone. You spent twenty minutes walking. You spent ten minutes writing. You have ten minutes left."

Gerald looked at the man. He looked at the tablet.

The screen displayed a graph.

The graph was red. It showed Gerald's Compliance Rating. It had dropped to 30.

"You filed a counter-citation," the man said. "That was... unexpected."

"It was necessary."

"It was arrogant."

"It was accurate."

The man sighed. He looked at the rat, which had scampered out from under the desk and was now sniffing the man's shoe.

"The Core you destroyed was not just any Core," the man said. "It was a franchise. Part of the 'Ironclad' network. They have lawyers."

"I don't have lawyers," Gerald said.

"I know. That's why you're going to lose."

Gerald closed his eyes for a second. He opened them.

"Show me the evidence," Gerald said.

The man held up the tablet. He swiped.

A holographic projection appeared in the hallway air. It was a blueprint. It was the blueprint of the high school gymnasium.

But it was wrong.

Gerald leaned forward. He squinted.

"Who drew this?" Gerald asked.

"The Ironclad Core submitted it during their defense," the man said. "They claim the structural failure was pre-existing. They claim you didn't measure it properly."

Gerald looked at the blueprint.

The lines were crisp. The dimensions were perfect.

The load calculations were complex, filled with equations that Gerald didn't fully understand.

But he understood the geometry.

He pointed to the north wall.

"Here," Gerald said. "This beam is marked as steel."

"Yes," the man said.

"It's wood," Gerald said. "I touched it.

It splintered.

Steel doesn't splinter.

It bends."

The man looked at the hologram. He looked at Gerald.

"The System accepts the Core's submitted blueprints as primary evidence," the man said. "Unless you can prove the blueprint is a forgery."

"I can't prove a forgery," Gerald said. "I can prove a discrepancy."

"Discrepancies are minor," the man said. "They don't overturn citations."

Gerald stepped into the hallway. He walked around the hologram. He walked around the blueprint.

He walked to the elevator at the end of the hall. He pressed the button for the ground floor.

"What are you doing?" the man asked.

"I need a ruler," Gerald said.

***

The city of Ashford was asleep. The streets were empty. The streetlights buzzed.

Gerald stood in front of the Ashford Municipal Library. He was not inside his dungeon. He was outside.

He was in the physical world. This was dangerous.

His Core was vulnerable when separated from the dungeon footprint, though the System allowed inspectors to roam within a small radius.

He held a tape measure. It was two meters long. He held a protractor. It was plastic.

He walked to the side of the library. He walked to the alleyway where the new construction had begun three years ago. A contractor had built an addition to the library. A small, glass-walled room for computer terminals.

Gerald measured the foundation.

He measured the spacing between the support columns.

He wrote it down.

Then he walked to the high school. It was a block away. The building was cordoned off with police tape.

The roof was gone.

The walls were jagged teeth against the night sky.

Gerald walked under the tape.

The police officer at the entrance looked at him. The officer saw the tie. He saw the clipboard.

He let him pass.

Gerald walked into the gymnasium. He walked to the center of the room. He walked to the spot where the ceiling had fallen.

He looked at the rebar.

It was twisted. Not from heat. From torque.

Gerald pulled out his notebook. He drawed. He did not draw the room as it was. He drew the room as it should have been.

He drew the load paths. He drew the force vectors. He calculated the weight of the roof. He calculated the tensile strength of the steel.

Then he did the math.

He did it in his head. He had done it a thousand times. He knew how much weight a beam of that size could hold. He knew how much weight the Ironclad Core's blueprint claimed it could hold.

The numbers didn't match.

The blueprint lied.

Not because of a mistake. Because of a lie.

Gerald's pen stopped. He stared at the number.

If the beam was overrated, then the Core had known. They had known the structure was weak. They had reinforced it with magic, not steel. And when the magic failed, the structure failed.

This was not a hazard. This was a fraud.

And fraud was not a building code violation.

Fraud was a criminal offense.

Gerald tore out the page. He folded it. He put it in his pocket.

He walked back to the library. He walked back to the basement.

The man in the suit was waiting. He was pacing.

"Time's up," the man said. "The Hearing begins in three minutes."

Gerald sat at his desk. He placed the folded paper on the desk.

"I am invoking the Right to Physical Inspection," Gerald said.

The man stopped pacing. "That's not a standard clause."

"It is," Gerald said. "Section 12, Paragraph 4. An inspector may demand physical verification of structural claims if the digital evidence is contradicted by direct observation."

"The Ironclad Core will object."

"They can object," Gerald said. "But the objectors must be present. And the Inspector General is present. And I am present."

Gerald looked at the man.

"Call the Hearing."

The man hesitated. He looked at his tablet. He tapped a button.

The room changed.

The walls dissolved. The floor became a grid of light. A table appeared. Two chairs.

One chair was empty.

The other was occupied by a figure made of black smoke and gold coins. It had no face. It had many eyes.

"Inspector Hutchins," the figure said. Its voice sounded like coins falling down a well. "You have challenged our blueprint."

"I have challenged your integrity," Gerald said.

The figure laughed. It was a dry, rattling sound.

"Prove it."

Gerald stood up. He walked to the center of the grid. He pulled the folded paper from his pocket. He unfolded it.

He did not hand it to the Core. He handed it to the System.

**[Evidence Submitted: Mathematical Proof of Structural Fraud.]** **[Cross-Referencing...]** **[Verifying...]**

The grid flickered.

The black smoke of the Core turned a deep, bruised purple.

**[Verification Failed. Blueprint does not match physical reality of Sector 4.]** **[Error: Material Strength Overstated by 400%.]**

The Core screamed. It was not a scream of pain. It was a scream of rage.

"You cheated!" the Core roared. "You used magic! You used illusions!"

"I used math," Gerald said. "And I used a ruler."

The System paused.

The grid turned red.

**[Violation Confirmed: Fraudulent Submission.]** **[Penalty: Asset Forfeiture.]**

The black smoke vanished. The gold coins clattered to the digital floor.

Gerald sat down.

He rubbed his temples.

He was exhausted. His body felt hollow.

The effort of holding the hearing, of standing, of speaking, had drained him.

The man in the suit reappeared. He was smiling.

"Well done, Inspector," the man said. "That was a first. No one has ever cited a Core for fraud. You've changed the meta."

Gerald looked at him. "What does this mean?"

"It means you're famous," the man said. "And it means you have enemies."

"Which enemies?"

The man checked his tablet. He scrolled. He stopped.

"The Ironclad network," the man said. "They are filing a class-action lawsuit against all inspector-type Cores. They are claiming bias. They are claiming you are 'over-enforcing.'"

Gerald felt a chill. It was not cold. It was the cold of a bureaucracy that had turned against you.

"What happens if they win?" Gerald asked.

"They don't just sue," the man said. "They lobby.

They have money.

They have influence. They are going to try to change the Code."

Gerald looked at his desk. He looked at the Form 12-C.

"If they change the Code," Gerald said, "I can't cite them."

"Correct."

Gerald picked up his pen. He capped it. He uncapped it. He capped it again.

He looked at the man.

"How many votes do they need?"

"Simple majority in the System Council," the man said. "There are twelve seats. Three are open. The Ironclad network has filled two of them with proxies this morning."

Gerald's heart stopped.

He looked at the empty third seat.

It was glowing.

A notification appeared in the air. It was gold. It was shiny. It was terrifying.

**[VACANCY DETECTED: Inspector General Seat 3.]** **[Nomination Open.]** **[Candidate: Gerald Hutchins.]** **[Accept? Y/N]**

Gerald stared at the option.

He was an inspector. He enforced the rules. He did not make them.

But if he did not take the seat, the rules would change. And if the rules changed, his dungeon would fail. His rat would die. The mold would die.

He looked at the rat. It was sleeping on the pile of citations.

He looked at the button.

Gerald raised his hand.

His finger hovered over the 'Y'.

The man in the suit stepped forward. He reached out.

"Don't," the man whispered. "You don't know what you're getting into."

Gerald looked at the man. He looked at the System.

He pressed 'Y'.
