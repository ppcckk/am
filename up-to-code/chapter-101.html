Chapter 101: Box Four

# Interlude: The Delve, Part One

The waiver was four pages long.

Halla had a reason, and the reason was a form. Two years ago her brother died in a collapse four days' walk from here, and the Guild closed the file in eleven days with the finding no actionable negligence. The dungeon had filed an entry permit. The entry permit had a box for structural condition. The box had been ticked. She had been trying to see the paperwork behind a ticked box ever since.

Halla Brodd read all four, because she had been delving for nine years and had learned that the four-page waivers were the ones that killed you. Tem read the first page and signed the rest. Pip, who had turned nineteen eleven days ago and had a new sword with the maker's oil still on it, read nothing and signed everything, twice, including a box that said SIGN ONLY ONCE.

"Box four," Halla said. "It asks if you have any condition that prevents you from descending stairs. Do you?"

"I don't know."

"Then write that."

The clerk behind the counter was not a clerk. It was a stack of paper that had achieved, through means nobody had explained to the Delvers' Guild, a working relationship with gravity. It produced a stamp from somewhere inside itself and pressed it against Pip's form with a sound like a door closing in an empty building.

"Entrant numbers assigned," the stack said. "One hundred and six, one hundred and seven, one hundred and eight. You will be received in order. Do not exceed the posted occupancy. Do not remove fixtures. Do not address the Core directly unless addressed first. Sub-basement access is by appointment."

"We have an appointment," Halla said.

"You have an entry permit. An appointment is different."

The stairs went down eleven steps and then turned, which was a code violation in every jurisdiction Halla had ever worked in, and she said so.

"Landing width is four feet six," Tem said, measuring with a string. "Code wants five."

"Write it down."

"Write what down? There's no complaint form."

There was a complaint form. It was mounted on the wall at the turn, in a brass holder, with a pen attached by a chain that had been welded to the holder rather than looped through it, so the pen could not be removed and could not reach the form. Halla looked at this for a while.

"That's deliberate," Pip said.

"That's municipal," Halla said.

The first chamber was empty except for a clipboard, and the clipboard hung at chest height with nothing visibly holding it. On it was a single page with three lines of text and a box for a signature, and the three lines said that the party acknowledged the presence of an uneven substrate at the marked location and accepted the risk of tripping.

Tem stepped around the marked location without reading it, which was the correct instinct and also the wrong one, because the marked location was not where the paper said it was.

He went down hard. His knee made a sound that Halla had heard twice before, both times in people who did not walk properly afterward.

"Trip hazard," said a voice from the floor. It was not the stack of paper. It was smaller and flatter and came out of a grate. "Reported. Cited. You are the injured party. You may countersign."

"Countersign what?"

"That you were injured on a documented hazard."

"I was injured because I didn't look."

"That is a separate finding," the voice said, "and it is not in your favor."

Halla got Tem upright and checked the knee. It would hold weight. It would not hold a fighting stance. Nine years, and this was the fourth time a dungeon had hurt one of hers before the first monster.

"Rennick's crew said this place had a boss on the second landing," she said. "Armored. Axe. Big."

"Rennick's crew," the voice from the grate said, "did not file an after-action report. Their observations are anecdotal. Would you like to file one?"

"Yes."

The clipboard drifted down the corridor toward her, unhurried, and stopped at a distance that was exactly too far to reach without stepping forward.

Halla did not step forward.

"Reading room is through there," the voice said, when she had stood still for long enough that Pip started shifting his feet. "Occupancy three. No food. No open flame. No resurrection on the premises; if you require resurrection, exit first and re-enter under a medical permit."

"Does anyone ever come back?" Pip asked.

"Everyone comes back," the voice said. "The question is whether they come back as the same entrant number."

---

The reading room was a reading room. That was the problem. Halla had been in forty-one dungeons and none of them had been a reading room, and the part of her that had stayed alive for nine years kept telling her that a room with tables and lamps and a card catalogue was a room with a trap in it, and the trap was the shape of the room itself.

There were four delvers already at the tables. Three of them were dead and had been dead for some time; they sat with their books open and their heads down and did not smell, which meant the room was doing something about the smell, which meant the room had opinions.

The fourth was alive and writing.

"Don't sit at table six," the living delver said without looking up. "Table six has an outstanding violation. It'll be resolved in eleven days. Sit at four."

Halla sat at four. Tem sat at four with his leg out straight. Pip stood, because there were three chairs at four and he had not been told to bring one.

"Name's Ovick," the delver said. "Level four. Licensed for documentation retrieval, not combat. I've been down here six weeks."

"Six weeks?"

"There's a queue." Ovick turned a page. "You want the boss, you need a hearing slot. Hearings run Tuesdays and Fridays. I filed in week two and I'm up in week seven, which is fast, because I filed the right form. Half the people who come down here file under the wrong classification and get sent to the back."

"What classification did you file under?"

"Structural non-compliance, affecting egress." Ovick tapped the page. "It's the only one where the burden of proof is on the dungeon instead of you. They hate it. The Core hates it. He's very polite about hating it, but he hates it."

"He?"

"The Inspector. You'll meet him. He'll measure something you own and then tell you the measurement."

Pip laughed. Nobody else did.

"There's a thing below the sub-basement," Ovick said, and his pen stopped. "I've been at four for six weeks and I've mapped eleven rooms and there is a stair at the back of the map room that goes down further than the dungeon goes. It's not on any permit. It's not on the plan. When I asked about it, I got a form."

"What kind of form?"

"A form for asking about things that are not on the plan." Ovick closed his book. "I filled it out. It came back stamped INSUFFICIENT. That's not a refusal. A refusal would tell me what was insufficient. INSUFFICIENT means the clerk doesn't know either."

Halla looked at the three dead delvers with their books open.

"They didn't file," Ovick said. "They just kept going. The room keeps them as evidence."

---

They left the reading room at the bell, which rang at four, which nobody had told them about. In the corridor the clipboard was waiting.

"Exit is permitted," it said. "Before you go: the party named Brodd, Tem, and the entrant formerly known as Pip is cited under Structural Code, section nine, for occupancy variance at the turn in the entry stair, and under Access Code, section two, for unauthorized measurement of a fixture."

"We measured a landing."

"You measured a landing with a string that was not issued by this office."

"That's not a rule."

"It is now," the clipboard said. "It was entered at three forty-one this afternoon in response to your measurement. You may appeal. Appeals are heard Tuesdays and Fridays."

Pip said, "How much is the fine?"

The clipboard turned slightly, the way a head turns.

"The fine," it said, "is that you will come back."

---

They came up the eleven steps and out through the reading-room door that was not a reading-room door and into the Ashford Municipal Library's west entrance, where a woman with a cart was shelving returns and did not look at them.

Halla signed them out at the front desk. Entry numbers one hundred and six through one hundred and eight, exit numbers the same, which was the only part of the day that had gone correctly.

"Six weeks," Tem said, on the steps. "He's been down there six weeks and he's still in a queue."

"He's alive."

"He's alive and he's in a queue."

Halla looked back at the library doors, which were glass and had a notice taped to them about the lift being out of service, and which had, behind them, eleven steps and a turn and a room with a card catalogue that was keeping three people as evidence.

"Tuesday," she said. "We file under structural."

"We don't have standing."

"We will by Tuesday."
