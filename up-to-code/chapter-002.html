Chapter 2: Three Auditors

# Chapter 2

The third-floor slab was coming down. Gerald watched it through the System's structural overlay, a wireframe diagram floating in his peripheral vision, and he could see the load-bearing walls on the east side already bowing inward at three degrees. Three degrees was the point where he used to write "immediate evacuation" in red ink and call the crane company.

The adjacent Core's dungeon was at eight out of one hundred, and the System's debuff cascade was working through its spawned entities like a slow poison.

The goblin-class creatures on the second floor had lost their regeneration entirely.

They were bleeding out from wounds they would have shrugged off ten minutes ago. The larger constructs on the third floor, the ones made of packed earth and rebar, were losing cohesion as the structural debuff ate their internal support geometry.

Gerald stood at the edge of the 200-meter range and watched a stone golem disintegrate mid-stride. Its left arm separated at the shoulder joint, not from damage but from the failure of the bonding agent that held it together. The arm hit the ground and crumbled into gravel. The golem's remaining half took two more steps before its legs gave out and it collapsed into a pile of aggregate.

He made a note on his pad. *Structural failure cascade confirmed. Entity dissolution rate: approximately one per ninety seconds. Projected total loss: under four hours.*

The problem was not the monsters.

The problem was that the dungeon's walls were also failing, and the dungeon's walls were holding back a subterranean water table that had been compromised by the cross-connected plumbing he'd cited in the first pass. Water was pushing through cracks in the foundation.

The water was carrying dissolved calcium from the failing mortar.

The calcium was depositing in the water table and reducing its volume, which was creating voids under the dungeon's floor, which was making the floor settle unevenly, which was putting lateral load on walls that were already at three degrees of deflection.

Gerald had seen this exact failure mode four times in his career, and it was called progressive collapse initiated by foundation settlement due to hydrostatic pressure differential. The buildings he'd seen do this were demolished within seventy-two hours of detection.

This dungeon had maybe six hours, and he started writing. Not a citation this time.

A corrective action order.

He listed the interventions in order of priority: shore the east walls, install temporary dewatering pumps, re-seal the plumbing cross-connection, replace the compromised mortar joints.

He knew exactly what needed to happen because he had written this same list for the Calloway Street parking garage in 2019 and for the Ashford Municipal Library annex in 2022.

He held up the pad. The System recognized the corrective order and pushed it through to the adjacent Core.

Nothing happened.

Gerald waited. He waited another thirty seconds. The golem on the fourth floor lost its head and kept walking for two more steps before toppling.

He tried again. Same corrective order. Same result.

The adjacent Core was not responding. It was either dead or it was ignoring him.

A notification pinged in his peripheral vision. He almost missed it because he was watching a support column develop a shear crack along its entire height.

**NOTIFICATION: Adjacent Core (ID-7741) has filed a procedural objection to Citation No. 2024-1147. Objection basis: Citation served without prior notice of inspection. System appeal window: 72 hours. Status: PENDING.**

Gerald stared at the notification. Then he looked at the adjacent dungeon. Then he looked at the notification again.

Procedural objection, and the Core was appealing on the grounds that Gerald had not given notice before inspecting. That was a real provision in the System's code. He could see it in the rulebook that had been burned into his consciousness when he'd become a Core.

Section 4.1.2: *All inspections shall be conducted with reasonable prior notice unless exigent circumstances exist.*

Gerald had not given notice. He had walked up to the adjacent dungeon and started writing citations.

He had never even thought about giving notice.

In thirty-one years of municipal work, he had never given notice. You did not announce your inspection. You showed up.

You looked. You cited.

The element of surprise was the entire point.

But the System was not the City of Ashford. The System had a rulebook, and the rulebook had Section 4.1.2, and Gerald had violated it.

He could argue exigent circumstances.

A Compliance Rating of twelve was exigent. He had photographs.

Well, he had System records. He had the structural overlay showing three-degree deflection on load-bearing walls. He had forty-three citations.

He had a water table actively eroding the foundation.

But he had not written the words "exigent circumstances" on the citation pad. He had not checked the box. The System had a checkbox for exigent circumstances, and he had not checked it, and the adjacent Core's objection was technically valid.

If the objection was upheld, the citation would be reversed.

The adjacent Core would go back to twelve. The dungeon would stop collapsing. The water table would keep eroding.

The foundation would keep settling.

And in six hours the whole thing would come down anyway, and the collapse would be uncontrolled, and the voids under the floor would channel the collapse energy laterally into the surrounding earth.

Which meant the adjacent Core's appeal was not trying to save its dungeon; it was trying to save its own skin. If the citation was reversed, the dungeon would collapse naturally, and Gerald would be liable for the uncontrolled collapse because he had cited it and then the citation had been reversed, and the System would attribute the collapse to his intervention than to the Core's original non-compliance.

Gerald felt his stomach turn. Not with nausea this time. With the specific cold clarity of a man who has just realized he has been outmaneuvered by a pile of rocks that learned to file paperwork.

He sat down on a rock. He took out his pad. He started writing an appeal response.

The response needed to establish that exigent circumstances existed at the time of inspection.

He needed to reference the structural data. He needed to reference the water table. He needed to reference the entity dissolution rate.

He needed to make the argument in the specific format the System required: a logical chain of premises leading to a conclusion, each premise verified against System records.

He wrote for forty minutes, and he rewrote the argument three times.

The first version was too emotional. The second version had a logical gap in the third premise.

The third version was tight.

He submitted it.

**NOTIFICATION: Appeal response received. Hearing scheduled: 00:00:00:00 (immediate).

Both parties present.

Hearing format: Structural Logic. Rules: Each party presents one structural argument. System evaluates logical validity.

Higher validity wins.

Ties were broken by Compliance Rating differential.**

The hearing began. The adjacent Core's argument appeared in his vision as a block of text, rendered in a font that looked like chiseled stone.

*The citation was served without notice.

Section 4.1.2 requires notice. No notice was given. The citation is therefore invalid.

The collapse of this dungeon is not attributable to the citation but to pre-existing conditions that existed prior to the citation. The citation did not cause the collapse.

The citation merely documented what was already happening.*

Gerald read it twice, which was a good argument. Clean.

Simple. It relied on a strict reading of the procedural rule and a causal claim that was technically defensible.

He submitted his response.

*Premise one: The adjacent Core's Compliance Rating at time of citation was 12. Premise two: System Rule 7.3 states that any structure with a Compliance Rating below 15 constitutes an exigent circumstance requiring immediate intervention.

Premise three: The citation was served when the Compliance Rating was 12, which is below 15.

Premise four: Rule 7.3 overrides Rule 4.1.2 in cases where the rating threshold is met. Conclusion: Notice was not required. The citation was validly served.*

The System paused. The pause lasted four seconds, which was a long time for the System.

**HEARING RESULT: Appellant argument validity: 0.61.

Respondent argument validity: 0.89. Citation No. 2024-1147 upheld.

Appellant Compliance Rating: 8.

Bonus granted to Respondent: +2 Compliance Rating (procedural knowledge bonus).**

Gerald looked at his own Compliance Rating. It had gone from 12 to 14. The bonus was small. It was the first time anything had made his number go up.

Then a new notification appeared. This one was different. It was not a System notification. It was a message from another Core.

**CORE-9902: Well done, Inspector. Your structural logic is sound.

I have been operating in this region for six years.

My Compliance Rating is 87.

I would like to discuss a partnership.**

Gerald stared at the message. Core-9902. He pulled up the details.

The Core's dungeon was four hundred meters to the northeast. Its Compliance Rating was 87.

Its violation count was three.

Minor electrical code issues. Nothing structural. Nothing life-threatening.

This was a professional dungeon. It had been built correctly. It had been maintained. It had survived six years of System audits because it was actually up to code.

Gerald typed a response. *What kind of partnership?*

The reply came back fast. *There are eleven Cores within a two-kilometer radius. Nine of them are below forty. The two that are above forty have both filed complaints with the Inspector General claiming that your citation methodology constitutes harassment. The Inspector General has flagged your Core for a compliance audit.*

Gerald read this twice. Then he looked at his own Compliance Rating. Fourteen.

**NOTIFICATION: Inspector General Audit scheduled.

Time remaining: 47:59:12. Current Compliance Rating: 14.

Audit threshold for critical review: below 30.

Audit threshold for Core dissolution: below 10.**

Forty-eight hours. His rating was fourteen. If the audit found his dungeon at fourteen, he would be in critical review. If it dropped below ten at any point during the audit, his Core would be dissolved.

He looked at his dungeon. His own dungeon.

The one he had been building for less than two hours.

He had not built anything yet, and he had only issued citations. His dungeon was a hole in the ground with a System shell around it.

The room had no ventilation. No egress. No fire suppression.

There was no structural support, as there was nothing to support.

His Compliance Rating was fourteen because the System had given him a baseline for having a Core at all. Fourteen was the floor. It was the number you got for existing.

He needed to build.

In forty-seven hours and fifty-nine minutes, he needed to build a dungeon that could pass an Inspector General audit. He needed ventilation. He needed egress routes.

He needed fire suppression, and he needed structural support. He needed electrical code compliance.

He needed plumbing separation.

He had thirty-one years of experience doing exactly this thing. He had written thousands of corrective action orders. He knew every requirement, every threshold, every exception clause. He knew what a compliant building looked like because he had spent his entire career making other people's buildings look that way.

But he had never built one himself.

Core-9902 sent another message. *The audit will check your current state, not your projected state. You need to be compliant at the moment the audit begins. Not before.

Not after. At the moment.

I can give you the audit checklist if you agree to share your citation methodology.

The other Cores want it.

I want it. The methodology is what's making them weak.

I want to know how you're doing it.*

Gerald looked at the message. Core-9902 was offering him the audit checklist.

The exact list of items the Inspector General would check, which was the single most valuable piece of information he could receive. It was the difference between building blind and building to spec.

And it wanted his citation methodology in return.

It wanted the knowledge of how to find violations. How to cite them.

How to make the System recognize a paper pad as a legal instrument.

How to use Section 7.3 to override procedural objections. How to win a hearing on structural logic.

If he gave that knowledge to Core-9902, Core-9902 could cite him. Core-9902 had a rating of 87.

It had six years of dungeon infrastructure, which had monsters.

It had defenses. It had everything Gerald did not have.

If he did not give it, he had forty-seven hours to build a compliant dungeon from nothing, without knowing the exact checklist, relying only on thirty-one years of municipal experience and the general rulebook.

Gerald looked at his pad, and he looked at the adjacent dungeon, which was still losing a golem every ninety seconds. He looked at the timer.

Forty-seven hours and fifty-seven minutes, and he typed: *What are your terms?*

The reply came back in two seconds. *Come to my dungeon. Walk the perimeter. I'll show you the checklist.

You tell me how the citation works. We do this in person because I want to see your face when you realize what Section 7.3 actually says.*

Gerald stood up. He put his pad in his pocket. He started walking northeast.

He had not gone fifty meters when a second message arrived. This one was from Core-9902.

*One more thing, and the Inspector General is not one auditor. It is three. They will arrive from three different directions.

They will each conduct an independent audit.

If any one of them rates you below ten, the dissolution is automatic. The other two cannot override it.

You need to pass all three.*

Gerald stopped walking. He looked at the message. Three auditors.

Three independent ratings. One failure meant death.

He started walking again.
