Chapter 50: Code Compliance Bonus

# Chapter 50

The support wall went up in four minutes and thirty seconds.

Gerald's hands moved through the System's construction interface with the speed of a man who had spent three decades watching contractors cut corners. He didn't build the wall.

He placed it, and the System did the work. But the placement required knowledge. Where to anchor.

What load path.

How to tie into existing structure without compromising the floor slab.

He'd done this a thousand times in the field. He'd never done it on himself.

The wall was eight feet long, six inches thick, anchored to the dungeon floor and ceiling with System-generated anchor bolts. It wasn't elegant. It was code-compliant. That was the point.

NOTICE: STRUCTURAL SUPPORT ELEMENT INSTALLED. CATEGORY: LOAD-BEARING WALL. COMPLIANCE BONUS: +2 STRUCTURAL.

Two points. He needed thirty-three.

Six minutes.

Gerald stared at the number.

He'd installed handrails. He'd widened the corridor to minimum required width. He'd put up an egress sign that the System had printed in fluorescent orange with the correct pictogram height.

None of it mattered. The door was gone.

Without the door, the egress route was non-compliant, and the egress violation carried a severity multiplier that ate every point he gained from adjacent categories.

He couldn't build a door in six minutes, and he didn't have the materials; the System's construction queue was backed up behind the wall he'd just placed.

Three items deep. A handrail bracket. A ventilation duct fitting.

A fire-rated door assembly.

Five minutes.

Gerald stopped, and he looked at the violation notice.

He looked at the point breakdown. The System displayed it in the same format it used for all notifications: category, violation, severity, point impact. Clean columns.

Consistent font.

Gerald had seen worse formatting in the municipal database.

EGRESS. Severity: CRITICAL. Impact: -35.

Thirty-five points lost. He was at seven. The violation was worth thirty-five. That meant the egress category alone was dragging him down by thirty-five points.

He couldn't fix it. Not in five minutes.

But he could make it not matter.

Gerald pulled up the Occupancy Classification panel, which was buried three submenus deep, under Administrative, then Licensing, then Classification. He'd never looked at it before. Why would he?

His dungeon was classified as Assembly. It had always been Assembly.

The System had assigned it at registration, probably because a library sub-basement was technically a public assembly space.

Assembly. That was the classification that required egress doors. That was the classification that required egress signage, minimum corridor widths, fire-rated assemblies, occupancy load calculations, and door hardware that swung in the direction of egress travel.

Storage did not require any of those things.

Gerald pulled up the Change of Use Permit form, which was Form 14-C.

He knew Form 14-C. He'd rejected forty-three Form 14-C applications in his career.

He'd also approved eleven. The eleven had all been for legitimate conversions. A warehouse becoming a retail space.

A retail space becoming a restaurant. A parking garage becoming a parking garage with a different parking ratio.

This was the reverse. Assembly to Storage. A downgrade.

The form asked three questions:

1. New occupancy classification. 2. Justification for change. 3. Confirmation that the new classification does not create a life-safety hazard.

Gerald filled in: Storage.

Justification: "Structure contains no occupant assembly function. No fixed seating. No assembly load. Primary use is material storage and biological containment."

That was true.

His dungeon had mold. It had a water leak. It had a rat that had been there since day one and had never been evicted because Gerald couldn't figure out the Form 11-B process.

None of those were assembly functions.

Confirmation: "No life-safety hazard created by reclassification. Storage occupancy does not require egress doors per applicable code."

He filed it.

NOTICE: CHANGE OF USE PERMIT SUBMITTED. REVIEW TIME: 15 MINUTES.

Gerald felt his stomach drop. Fifteen minutes. The Inspector General was arriving in four.

He looked at the clock. Four minutes. The permit would not be approved in time.

He looked at the form again. He looked at the review time. Fifteen minutes. Standard processing.

Then he looked at the fine print, and the fine print was in section 4. Subsection 4.2. It said: "A Change of Use Permit may be granted provisional effect upon submission if the new classification is a downgrade from the current classification and the structure contains no active hazards under the new classification."

Provisional effect. Upon submission. Not upon approval.

Gerald had submitted. The classification was a downgrade.

And his dungeon, under Storage classification, had no active hazards.

Storage occupancies didn't require doors. They didn't require egress routes. They didn't require occupancy load calculations.

They required, at minimum, a minimum ceiling height and adequate ventilation.

He had adequate ventilation. He'd installed the duct fitting three chapters ago.

NOTICE: CHANGE OF USE PERMIT GRANTED. PROVISIONAL EFFECT. OCCUPANCY CLASSIFICATION: STORAGE.

NOTICE: EGRESS VIOLATION INVALIDATED. CATEGORY NOT APPLICABLE UNDER CURRENT CLASSIFICATION.

NOTICE: COMPLIANCE RATING: 42/100.

Forty-two.

Gerald exhaled.

His hands were shaking, and he pressed them flat against the support wall he'd just installed and felt the anchor bolts holding. Good.

They were holding.

The clock hit zero.

The air in the center of his dungeon split. Not like a portal.

Like a seam.

A thin line of white light that widened into a rectangle. The rectangle had edges. Those edges had a bevel.

A clean 45-degree bevel.

Gerald noticed the bevel. Of course it had a bevel. Everything in the System had a bevel. The System was, at its foundation, a code.

The Inspector General stepped through, which was not humanoid. It was not a creature.

It was a shape.

A geometric shape with too many faces, each face covered in text. The text moved. It scrolled.

It was code. Actual code.

Building code, fire code, electrical code, plumbing code, all running simultaneously across the surface of the thing.

The text was dense, exhibiting the specific density of code amended three hundred times.

Gerald looked at it and felt something he had not felt since he was twenty-three and a junior inspector and his boss had handed him a set of plans for a twelve-story residential tower and said "good luck, you're on your own."

He felt out of his depth.

The Inspector General spoke. Its voice was the sound of a stamp hitting paper. Not one stamp.

Many stamps. Layered.

"CORE HUTCHINS. LICENSE REVIEW INITIATED."

Gerald nodded.

He'd expected this. He'd prepared for this. He had a rating of forty-two.

The threshold for license renewal was forty, and he was two points over.

"I'm compliant," Gerald said. "Forty-two. Above threshold."

The Inspector General's faces rotated.

The text on them scrolled faster. Gerald caught fragments. R-3.

R-2. B-1.

A-3.

H-2.

All occupancy classifications. The thing was checking his work against every possible classification simultaneously.

"LICENSE RENEWAL: APPROVED. CONDITIONAL."

Conditional. Gerald had seen conditional approvals before. They always implied a catch. They always meant the building owner would be back in his office in six months with a new problem.

"CONDITION: COMPLIANCE MAINTENANCE WINDOW OF 30 DAYS. RATING MUST NOT DROP BELOW 40 DURING PERIOD. VIOLATION OF CONDITION TRIGGERS IMMEDIATE REVOCATION."

Thirty days. He could work with thirty days. He could fix the door.

He could reclassify back to Assembly once he had a proper egress route, and could get the rating up to a safe margin.

Forty-two was thin, but it was enough to survive the next audit cycle if nothing went wrong.

Nothing ever went wrong. That was the whole point of code.

"IS THERE ANOTHER CONDITION?" Gerald asked.

The Inspector General's faces stopped rotating.

"SECONDARY REVIEW: CITATION AUTHORITY."

Gerald went still.

"YOU HAVE ISSUED THIRTY-SEVEN CITATIONS IN THE CURRENT CYCLE. CITATION AUTHORITY IS NOT A LICENSE. CITATION AUTHORITY IS A SEPARATE REGISTRATION. CITATION AUTHORITY REQUIRES A COMPLIANCE RATING OF SEVENTY."

Seventy.

Gerald was at forty-two.

"YOUR CITATIONS ARE PENDING VALIDATION. IF YOUR COMPLIANCE RATING REMAINS BELOW SEVENTY AT THE END OF THE 30-DAY WINDOW, ALL THIRTY-SEVEN CITATIONS ARE VOIDED."

Gerald's mouth went dry. He thought about the thirty-seven dungeons. He thought about the debuffs he'd applied.

The elite spawns that had died in the Hearing, and the Cores that had been crippled. The ones that had begged him to withdraw citations.

The ones that had tried to attack him and been repelled by his compliance enforcement.

"VOIDED CITATIONS TRIGGER WRONGFUL CITATION PENALTY TO CITING CORE. PENALTY: PERMANENT STAT DEBUFF EQUAL TO COMBINED DEBUFFS ORIGINALLY IMPOSED."

He did the math. He couldn't help it. He'd always done the math.

Thirty-seven citations. Average debuff severity of moderate.

Some severe.

The fire exit citation on the Thornwall Core had been catastrophic.

That one alone had killed four elite spawns.

The combined debuff would be worse than anything any Core could hit him with. It would be worse than the Inspector General itself.

"YOU WILL ALSO BE SUBJECT TO COUNTER-CITATION BY ALL THIRTY-SEVEN CITED CORES. THEY MAY CITATE YOU FOR ANY VIOLATION. WITHOUT LIMIT. WITHOUT COOLDOWN."

Thirty-seven Cores. Citing him. Simultaneously.

Without limits. Without cooldown.

Every violation he'd ever accumulated.

Every corner he'd cut. Every handrail bracket he'd skipped because the System didn't require it under Storage classification.

They would find all of it. They would cite all of it.

Gerald looked at the Inspector General. The Inspector General looked back with all of its faces.

"RATING REQUIRED FOR CITATION AUTHORITY: SEVENTY. CURRENT RATING: FORTY-TWO. WINDOW: THIRTY DAYS."

The Inspector General folded itself back into the seam. The seam closed. The bevel edges disappeared. The air healed.

Gerald stood in his dungeon, and he had a rating of forty-two. He needed seventy.

He had thirty days.

He had no door. He had a provisional Storage classification that meant no adventurer parties could legally enter his dungeon. He had no income.

He had no way to attract the kind of compliance pressure that had driven his rating up in the first place. Adventurers were the mechanism.

They triggered violations.

Violations triggered his awareness.

Awareness triggered remediation. Remediation triggered points.

Without adventurers, he was just a basement with a rat, and he looked at the handrails. He looked at the mold. He looked at the water leak.

He looked at the Form 14-C in his inventory. Provisional. Thirty days.

He had thirty days to go from forty-two to seventy without any visitors, any income, or any way to attract the kind of external compliance pressure that had built his rating from twelve to forty-two in the first place.

Unless he changed the classification again.

Unless he went back to Assembly.

Unless he built a door, and he started measuring the wall opening. Four feet wide. Eight feet tall.

Minimum clear width for Assembly egress: thirty-two inches. He had four feet.

That was enough.

He needed a door assembly. Fire-rated. Self-closing.

Positive latch.

Panic hardware on the egress side.

The System's construction queue still had three items in it. The door assembly was item three. Estimated completion: eleven minutes.

He had thirty days, and he pulled up the Citation Authority requirements.

Seventy points. He had forty-two. He needed twenty-eight more.

In thirty days. Without adventurers.

Without external pressure.

Unless he cited himself.

Gerald stared at the thought, which was absurd; it was also, he realized, technically permitted under Section 7 of the Citation Authority rules.

A Core could issue a Citation to any dungeon within range. Including its own.

The question was whether self-citation counted toward Citation Authority validation.

He pulled up the fine print. He read it twice.

It did not say you could not, which did not say you could.

It was silent.

Gerald had spent thirty-one years in the silence between what a code said and what a code meant. He knew where the gaps were. He knew where the municipal attorneys made their money.

He opened the Citation form.
