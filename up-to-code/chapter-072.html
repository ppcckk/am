Chapter 72: System Egress Route

# Chapter 72

Gerald was on the third floor when he realized the System's definition of "egress route" was wrong.

It was not wrong like a building. It was wrong like a dictionary defining a known word.

The System had a definition.

Gerald had a definition. They did not match.

The municipal code said an egress route was a continuous, unobstructed path from any point in a structure to a public way. The System said an egress route was a continuous, unobstructed path from any point in a structure to a point of "System-recognized termination." Gerald did not know what that meant. He had asked the System three times. The System had answered: "A point beyond the boundary of the cited structure at which a spawned entity can transition to an adjacent dungeon zone or public domain."

Gerald had read that four times. It still meant nothing.

He was standing in the east corridor of the third floor, measuring the distance from the stairwell door to the nearest window. The window was not an exit. It was a window. It was a glass rectangle that did not open, set into a wall that was not load-bearing but was also not, under the System's definition, a "point of System-recognized termination."

The corridor was compliant under municipal code, which was non-compliant under the System. Gerald had cited the library.

The library was now his problem.

The library had a Compliance Rating of 4. Gerald had not known a building could have a rating that low. He had been a building inspector for thirty-one years and he had never seen a number that low.

Not because buildings were good. Because buildings did not have numbers.

He checked his clipboard. The citation had gone through. The System had accepted it. The library was now flagged as "Under Citation: Core Hutchins, Responsible Party." Below that, in smaller text the System rendered in a font Gerald found personally offensive, was a Remediation Schedule.

Phase 1: Structural Integrity Assessment.

Deadline: 14 hours.

Phase 2: Egress Compliance. Deadline: 36 hours.

Phase 3: Fire Supplementation. Deadline: 60 hours. Phase 4: Ventilation and Air Quality.

Deadline: 72 hours.

Fourteen hours.

Gerald had seventy-one hours total. Phase 1 alone consumed fourteen. That left fifty-seven for the rest.

He looked at the library.

Four stories. Three hundred thousand square feet.

A building that had been standing since 1987, and he could not assess the structural integrity of a four-story building in fourteen hours. He had never assessed a four-story building in fourteen hours. He had assessed buildings in days.

Weeks. He had assessed a parking garage in nine months because the owner kept appealing.

But the System did not care about nine months. The System had fourteen hours.

Gerald went to the basement.

The library's basement was concrete.

Gerald ran his hand along the foundation wall and felt nothing.

No vibration. No settling.

The building was not moving. He knew this because he had inspected this building in March, two years before the System arrived, and he had written in his report: "Foundation appears adequate. No visible signs of differential settlement."

Adequate. Not compliant.

The System did not accept "adequate." Gerald needed to prove the foundation was compliant. The System required a Structural Integrity Assessment, which meant he needed to identify every load-bearing element, verify its capacity, and confirm it met the System's minimum standards.

Gerald did not know the System's minimum standards, and he knew the municipal standards. They were different. The municipal standards had numbers in them.

The System's standards had numbers in them too, but the numbers were different numbers.

He opened his clipboard and started writing.

Load-bearing walls, basement level: perimeter concrete block, 8-inch nominal. The System's structural code required a minimum compressive strength of 1,900 PSI for load-bearing masonry in a structure of this height.

Gerald knew the municipal code required 1,500, and the difference was 400 PSI; he did not know if the library's block met 1,900. He did not know if it met 1,500.

He had never tested a block. He had inspected blocks.

He had looked at them.

He had tapped them with a hammer and listened for the sound of solidity.

The System required a hammer test. Gerald had a hammer.

He pulled one from his tool belt and struck the block. It rang. The sound was high and clear.

Gerald had heard that sound in thirty-one years of inspections. It meant the block was solid.

It did not mean the block was 1,900 PSI.

The System logged the strike. A line appeared on his clipboard: "Masonry test recorded. Insufficient. Additional verification required."

Gerald struck the block again. The System logged it. "Insufficient."

He struck it a third time. "Insufficient."

He stopped. The System did not want a hammer test. It wanted a load calculation. It wanted him to calculate the weight of everything above this wall and divide it by the wall's cross-sectional area and compare the result to a material strength number he did not have.

Gerald sat down on the basement floor. He was fifty-four years old. His knees were not good. He sat on the concrete and he thought.

The library was four stories. The first floor held the circulation desk, the children's section, and a coffee shop that had been closed since the System arrived. The second floor held the reference section and the periodicals.

The third floor held the administrative offices and the community meeting rooms.

The fourth floor held the HVAC equipment and a storage room that smelled like old paper.

He could estimate the loads, and he had estimated loads before. Not for compliance. For citations.

When a building was too full, when a room was over-occupied, Gerald had estimated the live load and compared it to the design load. He had done it with a pencil and a calculator and a floor plan he had pulled from the building department's files.

He did not have the floor plans. The library's plans were in the building department's files, which were in the municipal building, which was three blocks away, which was now a dungeon core's territory.

Gerald stood up, and he walked to the stairwell.

He counted the steps. He measured the stair width with his tape.

He counted the doors. He counted the windows. He counted the rooms.

He used his body, as he had done a thousand times without plans. You walked the building.

You measured what you could see.

You estimated what you could not.

By hour three, he had a floor plan, which was rough. It was on a clipboard.

It was not a certified document.

The System did not care about certification. The System cared about measurements.

By hour six, he had load estimates for each floor. He used the municipal live load tables, because the System's tables were not available to him.

He used them anyway.

The System logged each calculation and marked them "Provisional." Provisional meant the System would accept them for now but could reject them later.

Gerald did not know what "later" meant.

By hour nine, he had identified every load-bearing wall in the basement and the first floor. The second and third floors were wood-frame. The fourth floor was steel.

Gerald knew this because he had inspected the library in March and written it down. He had written: "Upper floors appear to be conventional wood-frame construction.

Fourth floor mechanical penthouse appears to be steel-frame."

Adequate. Not compliant.

"appears to be" into measurements. He converted "conventional" into dimensions. He converted "steel-frame" into a specific grade of steel he estimated based on the visual thickness of the columns.

It was not a real estimate.

It was a building inspector's estimate, which was a thing Gerald had done his entire career without ever being asked to defend it under penalty of structural condemnation.

The System accepted it. "Provisional."

By hour eleven, Gerald had completed the Structural Integrity Assessment. The System logged it. The result appeared on his clipboard in the same offensive font.

Structural Integrity: COMPLIANT (Provisional).

Gerald exhaled. Fourteen hours. He had done it in eleven.

Phase 2 was Egress Compliance, and he had twenty-five hours remaining for that phase. He had already identified the problem. The System's definition of "egress route" did not match the municipal definition.

The library had egress routes. They went to doors.

The doors opened onto sidewalks.

The sidewalks were public.

Under municipal code, this was compliant. Under the System, the doors were not "points of System-recognized termination" because they opened onto a zone that was not a "dungeon zone or public domain."

The public sidewalk was not a public domain. It was a contested zone. Gerald knew this because the sidewalk outside the library had been cited by a neighboring dungeon core three chapters ago.

The citation was still active. The sidewalk was under citation.

A cited sidewalk was not a public domain, which was a contested space. Which meant the library's egress routes terminated in a contested space, which meant they were not compliant.

Gerald stared at this. He did not look away from it.

Then he found the loophole.

The System's egress definition required termination at a "dungeon zone or public domain." A dungeon zone was any area claimed by a registered Core.

Gerald was a registered Core. Gerald's dungeon was beneath the library.

The library's basement was, by adjacency, within Gerald's dungeon footprint, and the basement had a door. The door opened onto the sidewalk, and the basement also had an interior stairwell that connected to the first floor.

If Gerald designated the basement stairwell landing as a "point of System-recognized termination," then every egress route in the library terminated at that point. The stairwell landing was inside Gerald's dungeon. It was a dungeon zone. It met the definition.

The egress routes did not need to reach the sidewalk. They needed to reach Gerald.

Gerald wrote the designation on his clipboard. He wrote: "System-recognized termination point designated at basement stairwell landing, coordinates [0,0,0] relative to Core Hutchins footprint."

The System logged it. The library's egress rating updated.

It went from 0 to 34.

Still terrible.

But not zero.

Gerald moved to Phase 3. Fire Supplementation. He had twelve hours.

He went to the first floor. He looked at the fire extinguishers. There were six.

The municipal code required one per 75 square feet of floor area. The first floor was 75,000 square feet.

It needed a thousand, which had six.

The System required something different.

The System required "suppression capacity proportional to fire load classification." Gerald did not know the library's fire load classification. He estimated it based on contents.

Paper.

Books. A coffee shop with a deep fryer. He classified it as High.

High fire load required a suppression system. The library did not have one. It had six fire extinguishers and a sprinkler system that had not been inspected in eleven years.

Gerald could not install a sprinkler system in twelve hours. He could not install anything in twelve hours.

He did not have materials, and he did not have contractors.

He had a clipboard and a tape measure and a hammer.

He looked at the sprinkler system. It was there. It was old.

It was probably functional. He had inspected it in March.

He had written: "Sprinkler system appears intact.

No visible corrosion. Recommend inspection within 12 months."

Adequate. Not compliant.

The System required a "verified suppression capacity." Gerald needed to verify it. He needed to open a sprinkler head and see if water came out.

He went to the second floor, and he found a sprinkler head. He unscrewed it.

Water came out.

It was rusty and brown and it smelled like a lake that had been dead for a long time. But it was water. It was under pressure.

It was flowing.

The System logged it: "Suppression capacity verified. Partial. Coverage incomplete."

Partial.

Not zero.

Gerald moved to the third floor. He unscrewed another head.

Water. The fourth floor. Water.

The basement. Water.

He unscrewed forty-three heads across four floors. Every one of them had water. The system was functional. It was old and it was rusty and it was not up to code by any standard Gerald had ever applied, but it was functional.

The System logged it: "Suppression capacity verified. Coverage: 71%. COMPLIANT (Provisional)."

Gerald sat down in the fourth-floor storage room, and he was in hour thirty-one. Phase 4 was ventilation. He had forty-one hours for that phase, which was generous.

The library had windows.

The library had an HVAC system on the fourth floor. Gerald had inspected the HVAC system in March.

He had written: "HVAC operational.

Filter replacement recommended."

He went to the fourth floor. He looked at the HVAC unit.

It was running, which had been running for thirty-seven years.

It was loud and it smelled like dust and it moved air.

The System required "air exchange rate of 0.35 ACH minimum." Gerald did not know the library's ACH. He could estimate it. The HVAC unit's rated capacity was on a plate on the side of the unit.

Gerald read it. He divided.

He multiplied, and he got a number.

0.22 ACH. Below the minimum.

Gerald opened a window.

He opened another. He opened a third.

He opened every window he could find on the fourth floor.

The air changed. He recalculated. 0.31. Still below.

He went to the third floor. He opened windows.

He went to the second floor, and he opened windows.

He went to the first floor. The first floor had the main entrance doors, which were propped open.

He went to the basement. The basement had two exterior doors. He opened them.

He recalculated. 0.36 ACH. Compliant. Provisional.

The System logged it. The library's Compliance Rating updated.

It went from 4 to 23.

Still terrible. But it was moving.

Gerald looked at the clock.

Thirty-eight hours remaining. The library was not compliant.

It was provisionally compliant, and the System had accepted his work. The citation was satisfied. The remediation was complete.

He sat down in the first-floor lobby. He put his clipboard on the circulation desk.

He looked at the building.

It was the same building it had always been, which was not compliant. It would never be compliant.

But it was provisionally compliant, and the System had accepted that, and the citation was closed.

Then his clipboard updated.

CITATION STATUS: CLOSED. REMEDIATION: ACCEPTED (PROVISIONAL). STRUCTURAL ASSESSMENT: TRIGGERED.

Gerald read it twice.

STRUCTURAL ASSESSMENT: TRIGGERED. REASON: CITATION ACCEPTED WITH PROVISIONAL COMPLIANCE.

PROVISIONAL COMPLIANCE REQUIRES FULL STRUCTURAL ASSESSMENT BY SYSTEM-APPOINTED INSPECTOR.

ASSESSOR: INSPECTOR GENERAL, SUB-UNIT 7. ETA: 00:47:12.

Forty-seven minutes.

Gerald stood up. His clipboard updated again.

ASSESSOR ARRIVAL WILL TRIGGER CONDEMNATION PROTOCOL IF STRUCTURAL INTEGRITY CANNOT BE CONFIRMED TO SYSTEM STANDARD (NOT MUNICIPAL STANDARD). CONDEMNATION PROTOCOL: STRUCTURAL ERASURE.

Structural erasure. The library would cease to exist.

Gerald looked at the building. He looked at the shelves of books. He looked at the circulation desk. He looked at the coffee shop that had been closed since the System arrived.

He looked at his clipboard. He looked at the clock.

Forty-seven minutes.

The System's standard was not the municipal standard. Gerald had used the municipal standard. He had used it because it was the only standard he knew. He had used it because he had thirty-one years of experience applying it and zero years of experience applying anything else.

He had cited the library using a standard he could not meet. He had provisionally satisfied the citation using calculations based on a standard that the System's assessor would reject.

Gerald picked up his clipboard. He started running.
