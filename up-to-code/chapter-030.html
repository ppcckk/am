Chapter 30: Throughput Penalty

# Chapter 30

Gerald's hands shook.

Not from fear. From the act of filling out a form he had never filled out before.

Form 4-B, Standard Citation, Cross-Connection Violation, Category 7, and he had cited for ventilation, for egress, for fire separation, for structural load. He had never cited for cross-connection because he had never encountered one. UNDERFLOW had given him the violation.

He was taking it.

The penalty matrix auto-populated. Cross-connection between separate dungeon footprints without inter-Core consent agreement: Severity 3.

Debuff: all water-attribute spawns lose 40% throughput for 72 hours.

Gerald stared at the number. Forty percent. UNDERFLOW's entire economy was water.

The debuff would starve his spawns. It would collapse his spawn list within a week if not remediated.

That was the point. The System's remediation provision required the citing Core to enter the cited Core's space and bring the violation up to code within the debuff window. If Gerald did not enter, UNDERFLOW's spawns would die. If he did enter, he would be inside another Core's infrastructure with full building authority.

He pressed submit.

The form dissolved into the System interface, and a chime sounded, low and flat, like a parking meter accepting a coin.

CITATION ISSUED.

TARGET: UNDERFLOW.

SEVERITY: 3. REMEDIATION WINDOW: 72:00:00.

CITING CORE MUST ENTER TARGET FOOTPRINT TO INITIATE REMEDIATION.

Gerald felt the familiar pull. The System's remediation protocol opened a pathway through the sub-basement floor, a temporary easement that existed only while the citation was active. He could see it as a faint blue line running through the concrete, angling northeast toward the storm drain network beneath Ashford's east side.

He did not walk toward it. He went to his filing cabinet first.

The cabinet was real. It was a four-drawer metal unit he had requisitioned from the library's supply closet during his first week as a Core, back when he still had hands.

It was now a System construct, a conceptual filing cabinet with a 2x3-foot footprint in his dungeon, holding documents in a manner that kept him sane.

He pulled Form 11-C: Remediation Authorization. Blank. He had never used it.

He read the instructions for the third time.

The instructions said: The citing Core may enter the cited Core's footprint for the duration of the remediation window. During this period, the citing Core has full building authority over the cited Core's infrastructure, and the citing Core may inspect, modify, and repair. Any code category brought into compliance during remediation grants the citing Core a permanent passive resistance tied to that category.

Permanent. He read it again.

He had cited UNDERFLOW twice before.

Each time, the debuff had done its work and expired. He had never entered. He had never taken the resistance.

Now he would, and he filled out Form 11-C. Authorized Remediation.

Citation Reference: 4-B-003.

Category: Cross-Connection / Drainage Infrastructure.

He signed it with his Core designation. The form dissolved into the pathway.

Gerald walked into the blue line.

---

UNDERFLOW's footprint was not what Gerald expected, and he had imagined pipes. Dark, dripping, utilitarian. A drainage Core would be all infrastructure, he thought.

No rooms. No spawns in the traditional sense.

Just water moving through channels.

He was wrong.

The space opened around him like a throat. Twelve thousand square feet of curved walls, smooth and wet, lined with a material that looked like compressed silt but felt like tile. The channels were everywhere.

Not pipes.

Channels. Open troughs cut into the floor at varying depths, each carrying a different volume of water at a different speed.

Some moved fast, thin threads of current.

Others were wide and slow, almost still, reflecting the dim bioluminescence that UNDERFLOW had installed along the upper walls.

Gerald counted the channels as he walked. He counted them because counting was what he did. He counted them and he measured them and he noted their dimensions in the air with a flick of his Core interface, generating a running survey that the System logged automatically.

Channel 1: 14 inches wide, 6 inches deep, flow rate 2.3 liters per second. Compliant with Category 7 minimums for residential-grade drainage.

Channel 2: 22 inches wide, 11 inches deep. Commercial grade. Compliant.

Channel 3: 8 inches wide, 3 inches deep, and he stopped.

Channel 3 was undersized. By a factor of three.

A channel this narrow carrying this volume of water would back up under load.

It was not a cross-connection, which was a maintenance failure. UNDERFLOW had a third violation that he had not mentioned.

Gerald noted it. He did not cite it. He was here to remediate the cross-connection, not to ambush a partner.

But he noted it. He would mention it later, after the citation was resolved, in a professional manner.

He followed the blue line deeper.

The cross-connection was where he expected it.

A junction point where three of UNDERFLOW's channels fed into a shared manifold that led, via a sealed conduit, toward Gerald's own footprint. The conduit was 6 inches in diameter, properly sealed, properly rated. UNDERFLOW had built it to code.

That was the trick.

The cross-connection was not a violation of construction standards. It was a violation of consent.

No inter-Core agreement existed, and the System did not care that the pipe was well-made; it cared that the pipe was unauthorized.

Gerald found the consent form UNDERFLOW had prepared, and it was already drafted, sitting in the junction's wall like a card in a slot.

Gerald read it.

Standard terms.

Shared drainage capacity. Water rights split 60/40 in UNDERFLOW's favor.

Duration: indefinite.

Gerald amended it to 50/50. He signed it. He fed it into the slot.

The System chimed. CITATION RESOLVED.

REMEDIATION COMPLETE.

CROSS-CONNECTION NOW COMPLIANT. UNDERFLOW DEBUFF EXPIRED.

And then the second chime. The one Gerald had never heard before.

PASSIVE RESISTANCE GRANTED. CATEGORY: DRAINAGE INFRASTRUCTURE. CORE GERALD HUTCHINS NOW HOLDS WATER-ATTRIBUTE RESISTANCE: +15%.

Fifteen percent. Small. Permanent. He filed it away in his mind like a new tool in a toolbox.

He turned to leave, and he did not leave.

Beyond the junction and the three authorized channels lay eleven more.

They were not in the survey UNDERFLOW had shared.

They were not on any form. They ran along the back wall of the chamber, hidden behind a false panel of compressed silt that Gerald only noticed because the System flagged it.

A structural anomaly.

Non-load-bearing partition with no maintenance access. A violation in itself, but a minor one.

Gerald removed the panel.

Eleven conduits. Each 4 inches in diameter. Each sealed. Each leading away from UNDERFLOW's footprint in a direction that did not match any Core registration he could find in the System directory.

He checked them one by one.

Conduit 1: destination unknown. Not registered to any Core. Conduit 2: destination unknown. Conduit 3: destination unknown.

He checked all eleven. None of them were registered. None of them appeared in the System's Core directory, and they were pipes going to places the System did not acknowledge existed.

Gerald stood in front of them for a long time, and he had worked in municipal building for thirty-one years. He had seen unauthorized connections. He had seen unpermitted work. He had seen contractors hide things behind drywall because they knew it was wrong and did not want to be found.

But he had never seen eleven unregistered conduits in a Core's dungeon. Not because the act was unusual.

Because the destination was impossible.

A conduit that led nowhere was a dead pipe.

A conduit leading to a location outside the System's directory was distinct; it was a pipe to a space that should not exist.

Gerald pulled up his audit notes. The System Inspector General's audit was scheduled for 41 days from now, and the audit would scan all registered conduits within a Core's footprint.

It would not scan unregistered ones. Not unless the audit team physically found them.

He looked at the eleven pipes. He looked at his remediation authorization, still active. He had building authority over UNDERFLOW's space for another sixty-one hours.

He could cite UNDERFLOW for the eleven.

That would be honest. That would be correct. It would also be a lie.

UNDERFLOW had told him he only built three, and the eleven were not his.

Gerald pulled out a blank Form 4-B.

He started with Target, because that was where the lie was.

Target: UNKNOWN. Violation: Unregistered Conduit, Category 7. Severity: 5.

The highest severity he had ever assigned, and the System did not accept it. TARGET NOT FOUND.

He tried again. Target: CONDUIT DESTINATION.

TARGET NOT FOUND.

He tried a third time. Target: SPACE OUTSIDE DIRECTORY.

The System paused. Then it responded with a line of text Gerald had never seen before.

UNREGISTERED SPACE DETECTED. INSPECTOR GENERAL NOTIFICATION: AUTOMATIC. AUDIT RESCHEDULED: 7 DAYS.

Seven days. Not forty-one. Seven.

Gerald stared at the message, and the audit was coming in seven days.

Not because he had found the conduits. Because the System had detected them.

The act of looking at them had triggered something.

He looked at the eleven pipes again. They were not hidden from the System.

They were hidden from Cores.

The System knew they were there. It had been waiting for someone to notice.

And now it was coming to look.

Gerald turned back toward the blue line that would take him home. He stopped. He turned back to the pipes.

He pressed his Core interface against Conduit 1 and listened.

Water moved inside.

But not water. Something else.

Something that carried a frequency the System logged as biological.

Living material. Moving through an unregistered pipe to an unregistered space.

Gerald pulled his hand back, and he walked out of UNDERFLOW's footprint without completing his survey. He did not finish counting channels. He did not note the undersized Channel 3. He did not mention the false panel.

He went back to his filing cabinet and opened a drawer that had been empty for thirty chapters. He pulled out a form he had never filled out.

Form 9-Z.

Emergency Structural Report.

The form that existed only when a Core suspected that the System itself was compromised.

He had found it in his first week. He had assumed it was a placeholder. A form for theoretical emergencies that would never occur.

The first box asked for his Core ID, and he wrote it without thinking.

His hands did not shake this time. That was worse.

The form was Form 11-C: Notification of Pre-Code Structure. He had never filed one. In thirty-one years the City had never produced a structure that predated the code it was built under.

The City was one hundred and forty years old. The code was one hundred and thirty-nine.

Gerald had once asked, at a training session, what had governed buildings in that first year. The instructor had said the question was out of scope and moved to the next slide. The slide had been about stairwell widths.
