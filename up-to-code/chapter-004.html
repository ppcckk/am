Chapter 4

Gerald's rating ticked to three.

The number pulsed in the upper left of his vision like a warning light on a dashboard. Three. The System's dissolution threshold was ten. He had seven points of margin to lose before he ceased to exist, and Core-9902 was looking at him with the particular attention a contractor gives a building inspector who's just found load-bearing walls where none should be.

"You're going to kill me," Gerald said. Not a question.

Core-9902's red lights pulsed in a slow rhythm. "I'm going to consider killing you," the Core corrected. "There's a difference. One is a decision. The other is a calculation."

"I've done both. I'd say the calculation is the dangerous one."

Gerald's mind was moving fast, which was unusual for him. Usually his thoughts arrived in triplicate, cross-referenced, stamped, and filed. Now they were arriving in fragments. Adjacent structure. Compliance overlay. Audit trigger. The words circled like wasps.

His rating dropped to two.

He could feel it physically. A thinning. Like the air in the corridor had been replaced with something less substantial. His hands looked slightly translucent at the edges.

"Two," Core-9902 said. "You've got about ninety seconds before the System resolves you. I could let that happen. I could also serve you a counter-citation right now for unauthorized presence in a Class 4 dungeon without a permit. That would be a separate violation. Would it not?"

Gerald didn't answer. He was reading the audit overlay. The System had formatted his own compliance report in the same way it formatted everyone else's, and it was listing the violations that were dragging his score down.

Violation 1: No fire suppression system. Violation 2: No means of egress. Violation 3: No structural enclosure. Violation 4: No ventilation. Violation 5: No electrical infrastructure.

Every single one was a violation that Core-9902's dungeon was supposed to provide. The System was literally scoring Gerald on Core-9902's building. That was the adjacency rule. He was being assessed as if he were a tenant in Core-9902's structure.

A tenant.

The word hit him the way a wrench hits a pipe. Hard and specific and ringing.

"File a variance," Gerald said.

Core-9902's lights flickered. "What?"

"Section 14.2 of the Compliance Framework. Variance for adjacency-induced non-compliance. If a structure's violations are demonstrably caused by the condition of an adjacent structure, the affected party may file for a variance and the audit is suspended pending a Hearing."

"You're a structure," Core-9902 said. "You're a Core. You don't have a building. You don't have walls. You can't file a variance because you don't have a variance to file. You don't have a structure to be non-compliant within."

"I'm being audited as a structure," Gerald said. "That's the adjacency rule. I'm a structure by the System's own definition. And if I'm a structure, I have the same rights as any structure under the audit framework. Including the right to file a variance."

His rating was one. The thinning was worse. He could see the floor through his own shoes.

Gerald raised his hand. The System's citation interface responded to gesture the way it responded to everything else. He pulled up the form. He'd never filled one out before. The form was simple. Structure ID, adjacent structure ID, cause of non-compliance, requested relief.

He filled it out in thirty seconds. The System accepted it.

The audit overlay froze. His rating stopped at one. The numbers held there like a clock that had been stopped by a hand pressed against the face.

Core-9902's lights went from red to a confused amber. "That's not how it works."

"It's how it's written."

"The variance is for physical structures. You don't have physical structure."

"The System assigned me a Compliance Rating. That means it considers me a structure. You can't have a rating without being a structure. That's the definition. I'm a structure. I'm filing a variance. The audit is suspended."

A new icon appeared in Gerald's vision. It was the gavel. The Hearing icon. It meant the System was convening a formal proceeding between two Cores to determine whether the variance was valid.

"You've made a mistake," Core-9902 said. "Hearings are adversarial. You'll need to argue structural logic. You'll need to demonstrate that your non-compliance is caused by my dungeon's condition, not your own failure to build. And you have no structure. You have nothing. How do you argue that you're compliant when you're not?"

"I'm not arguing that I'm compliant," Gerald said. "I'm arguing that I'm non-compliant because of you. That's a different argument. And it's a stronger one."

The gavel icon pulsed. The Hearing was beginning.

The dungeon reshaped itself. The corridor they stood in became a formal space. The walls straightened. The floor leveled. The red lighting shifted to a flat, institutional white that Gerald recognized from every municipal hearing room in Ashford. It was the System's own version of a government office. The same beige. The same flickering fluorescent. The same plastic chairs bolted to the floor.

Gerald sat down. Core-9902 did not sit. It had no body to sit in. Instead, its presence manifested as a column of amber light in the center of the room, the way a witness stands in a box.

A third voice entered. Flat. Bureaucratic. The System's adjudication layer.

"Hearing convened. Matter: Variance request filed by Core [UNASSIGNED] against Core-9902. Appellant alleges adjacency-induced non-compliance. Respondent contests. Appellant may present argument."

Gerald stood. His legs felt thin. His rating was still one. If he lost this, the audit resumed and he dissolved.

"The appellant is a structure," Gerald said. "The System has assigned me a Compliance Rating, which by definition requires structure status. I am non-compliant in five categories: fire suppression, egress, structural enclosure, ventilation, and electrical infrastructure. I am requesting a variance because all five deficiencies are caused by the Respondent's failure to provide them to adjacent structures."

The adjudication layer paused. Core-9902's column of light flickered.

"Respondent may present argument."

Core-9902's voice came from everywhere. "The appellant is not a tenant. There is no lease. There is no occupancy agreement. The adjacency rule does not create tenancy. It creates a scoring overlay. The appellant's non-compliance is not caused by my structure. It is caused by the appellant's failure to build."

"Objection," Gerald said. The word came out before he'd decided to say it. But he'd been saying it for thirty years. "The System's adjacency rule explicitly states that structures within proximity are scored on shared infrastructure. Fire suppression is shared infrastructure. Egress is shared infrastructure. The Respondent has not provided either. The appellant cannot be scored on failures the Respondent controls."

"Overruled," the adjudication layer said. "The adjacency rule applies to physical structures. The appellant has no physical structure. The variance does not apply."

Gerald's stomach dropped. He looked at the overlay. His rating was still one, but the audit was unpausing. The numbers were about to start moving again.

He needed something else. Something the System hadn't considered.

He looked at the Hearing room. The beige walls. The bolted chairs. The fluorescent lights.

"New argument," Gerald said. "The Respondent's dungeon has no legal means of egress."

Silence.

"Expand," the adjudication layer said.

"Occupancy load calculation," Gerald said. "Core-9902's dungeon contains approximately forty-seven spawned entities at any given time. That's its occupancy load. Section 7.3 of the Compliance Framework requires a minimum of one egress point per fifteen occupants. Forty-seven occupants require a minimum of four egress points. Core-9902 has one. The entrance. Which is also the entrance for adventurers."

"That's not a violation," Core-9902 said. "Dungeons don't need egress. They're not buildings."

"They are," Gerald said. "The System scores them as buildings. You just argued that the adjacency rule doesn't apply because I'm not a physical structure. But the System scored you as a physical structure when it assigned you a Compliance Rating. You can't have it both ways. Either you're a building, in which case you need egress for your occupants, or you're not a building, in which case the adjacency rule shouldn't have applied to me either and I shouldn't be scored at all."

The adjudication layer was silent for four seconds. In System time, that was an eternity.

"Argument accepted," the adjudication layer said. "Respondent's egress violation is a contributing factor to appellant's adjacency score. Variance granted in part. Audit suspended for thirty minutes. Appellant's rating is reset to the baseline for unassigned Cores: fifteen."

Gerald's rating jumped. One to fifteen. The thinning reversed. His hands went solid. He sat back down.

Core-9902's column of light went white-hot for a moment, then settled back to amber. "You found a new violation type," it said. Not angry. Almost impressed. "Egress. Nobody cites egress."

"Nobody spawns forty-seven things in a room with one door," Gerald said. "That's not a dungeon. That's a fire hazard."

The Hearing icon faded. The beige walls melted back into the corridor. The red lighting returned.

Gerald stood. He had thirty minutes before the audit resumed. He needed to build something. Anything. A wall. A door. A ventilation shaft. Something that would make him a structure and decouple him from Core-9902's compliance.

He opened his Core interface. The build menu was empty. He had no materials, no mana reserves, no designated location. He was a Core with nothing.

Then a new notification appeared. Not from the System's audit layer. From outside it.

A citation.

It was addressed to him. Core [UNASSIGNED]. Citation number 00004. Filed by Core-4471. Violation: Unauthorized construction activity within a Class 2 residential zone. Penalty: Immediate dissolution of any structures within cited zone.

Gerald read it twice. He had no structures. He had no zone. He hadn't built anything.

But the citation was valid. The System had accepted it. The seal was red.

He looked up. The corridor stretched in both directions, and somewhere in the distance, he could hear the sound of stone moving. Walls rearranging. Something building.

Core-9902's lights pulsed. "Core-4471," it said. "That's a new one. It's been here for about a week. It's been quietly building a dungeon three blocks from my perimeter. It's got good structural work. Clean geometry. Very little fire load."

"Why is it citing me?"

"Because you're the only thing in the area that isn't built yet. It's establishing territorial compliance. If you're a Core, you need a permit. If you don't have a permit, it can cite you for unauthorized activity. And if it cites you before you've built anything, it gets to define what your zone is."

Gerald understood. In his old job, this was a classic move. A developer would file a zoning complaint against an empty lot before the owner could build, forcing the zoning board to classify the lot in a way that benefited the developer. The empty lot lost because it had no plans to point to.

Core-4471 was doing the same thing to him.

"You have twenty-nine minutes before your audit resumes," Core-9902 said. "And you have a citation from a Core that's already built and you haven't. If you appeal the citation, you enter another Hearing. If you don't appeal, the citation executes and any structure you build within the next hour is automatically dissolved."

Gerald looked at the citation again. The zone designation. It was labeled as a Class 2 residential zone. That meant low-density. That meant he could only build a small structure. A cottage. A garage. Nothing that could function as a dungeon.

Core-4471 wasn't trying to destroy him. It was trying to make him irrelevant. A Core with a cottage-sized dungeon was a Core that couldn't compete. Couldn't spawn meaningful monsters. Couldn't generate mana.

Gerald pulled up the appeal interface. He had one option. He could argue that the citation was invalid because he wasn't a structure yet. But he'd just argued the opposite in the Hearing. He'd argued that he was a structure because the System scored him as one. If he argued he wasn't a structure now, he'd contradict his own testimony and the System would flag him for perjury.

He was trapped. Both paths led to a loss.

Unless.

Gerald looked at the citation's zone designation again. Class 2 residential. He pulled up the Compliance Framework's zoning definitions. Class 2 residential required a minimum lot size of 400 square meters. The citation specified a zone boundary. Gerald measured it against the corridor dimensions.

The zone was 380 square meters.

Twenty square meters short of the legal minimum.

Gerald smiled. It was the kind of smile that had made contractors in Ashford want to throw things.

He filed his appeal.
