Chapter 85: Thirty-One Years Neglected

# Chapter 85

Gerald measured the first wall and knew it was hopeless.

The surface was not a surface. It was a palimpsest of failed repairs, each layer a different material, each material wrong for the substrate beneath it.

He pressed his thumb against the joint where two sections met and felt the gap.

A full centimeter. Maybe more. The sealant had gone rubbery and then brittle and then simply stopped being a thing at all.

He marked it on the clipboard. Then he turned to the next wall.

Same problem. Same gap. Different material. Different wrongness.

Core 7741-A had not been maintained in thirty-one years.

Gerald could tell by the material degradation alone. The polymers had oxidized past their useful life.

The metal framing had begun to delaminate at the molecular level.

The electrical conduit running through the ceiling was corroded to a point where it was no longer conducting anything except its own decay.

He checked his watch. One hour forty-three minutes.

He pulled up the System interface. The Compliance Rating for Core 7741-A populated in a cascade of red text.

Not twelve out of one hundred.

Four, and the number was so low it registered as a rounding error. Gerald stared at it.

He had seen bad buildings in his career. He had seen a condemned textile mill in 2007 where the floor was literally held up by rust. He had not seen four.

He started walking; the dungeon footprint exceeded his own. Much larger.

His four thousand square feet was a rectangle, neat and municipal, the kind of space that made sense on a zoning map.

Core 7741-A's footprint was a sprawl. Corridors branched at irregular angles. Rooms opened into rooms with no logical progression.

The geometry was wrong, making Gerald's stomach turn.

Not wrong like haunted. Wrong like a building permit that had been filed, approved, and then never actually inspected.

He passed through what he guessed was a former spawn chamber, and the floor was scored with channels where fluid had once flowed. The channels were dry. The scoring pattern was inconsistent, suggesting the flow rate had varied wildly over time.

Gerald noted it. Unmaintained drainage infrastructure.

Category: Structural.

Severity: Critical, and he passed through a corridor that should have been straight and was not. The walls curved inward at a rate that would have violated the minimum egress width code by forty percent at its narrowest point.

Gerald measured. He wrote it down. His hand was steady.

His hand was always steady.

Forty-one minutes gone, and he reached what he believed was the core chamber. It was not a chamber. It was a cavity. The walls here were uncomfortably organic, dense fibrous material pulsing faintly with residual energy rather than life, like a battery holding a charge after its device is discarded.

Core 7741-A was here.

Gerald could feel it. The vibration was stronger now, closer. It was not a voice anymore.

It was a presence.

A weight. The kind of weight you feel in a room where someone is standing behind you and you have not yet turned around.

Gerald did not turn around. He opened the System interface and searched for the correct form.

He had been searching for two minutes when he found it.

Form 47-C. Conditional Use Permit, Structural Remediation, Extended Timeline.

He read it twice. Then he read it a third time.

The form was designed for structures undergoing active remediation that could not be completed within a standard audit window. The System's logic was straightforward: if a core had filed a Corrective Action Plan and was actively executing it, the audit could not penalize violations that were already identified and scheduled for repair. The audit could only penalize violations that were NOT in the plan.

The timeline for completion was determined by the core's Operational Capacity Rating, and a core with high capacity had to complete repairs quickly. A core with low capacity had more time.

Core 7741-A's Operational Capacity Rating was zero.

Gerald looked at the number. He looked at the form. He looked at the number again.

He started filling in the form.

The Corrective Action Plan required a list of violations, a remediation method for each, and an estimated completion date.

Gerald listed everything he had measured. The wall gaps. The egress violations.

The drainage failure. The electrical conduit.

The organic substrate degradation.

He listed them with the specificity of a man who had written ten thousand deficiency reports and knew that the difference between "wall has a gap" and "wall exhibits a 12mm separation at the junction of materials A and B, indicating failed adhesion at the molecular bond layer" was the difference between a citation that held up on appeal and one that did not.

For remediation method, he wrote: "Full structural assessment, material replacement per Section 14.2(b), reconnection of all utility infrastructure to current code standards, installation of compliant egress routes at minimum 900mm clear width."

For estimated completion date, he wrote: "Indeterminate. Dependent on Operational Capacity Rating restoration. Current rating: 0. Projected timeline: indefinite."

He submitted the form.

The System processed it. The interface flickered. Then it returned a status.

Conditional Use Permit: GRANTED.

Gerald exhaled. He allowed himself three seconds of relief. Then the System returned a second notification.

Liability Transfer: Active. All penalties associated with Core 7741-A's non-compliance are now assessed against the filing party.

Gerald read it. He read it again.

The Conditional Use Permit did not remove the violations. It deferred them.

But the liability for those violations, while deferred, had been transferred to the person who filed the permit. Gerald was now, in the System's eyes, the responsible party for Core 7741-A's compliance failures. If the core failed to remediate, the penalties would be applied to Gerald.

Not to Core 7741-A. To him.

He looked at the number again.

Four out of one hundred. Thirty-one years of accumulated non-compliance. The penalty structure for sustained non-compliance at that level was not a debuff.

It was not a fine, which was dissolution.

Gerald set the clipboard down. He picked it up again. He set it down again.

Then he noticed something.

The Conditional Use Permit had been filed under Gerald's name. His core registration.

His boundary.

His dungeon. Which meant that the System now considered Core 7741-A to be a sub-component of Gerald's dungeon. Not a separate entity.

A component. Like a load-bearing wall.

Like a plumbing system.

Like the electrical conduit that was currently corroding inside the walls of a building Gerald had been living in for eighty-four chapters.

He had just assumed liability for a structure that was, by the System's own classification, part of his own dungeon. Which meant his own Compliance Rating now included Core 7741-A's violations.

Gerald checked his own rating, which had dropped from 94 to 31.

He stared at the number. Thirty-one.

The same number as his years of service. The same number as the years Core 7741-A had been asleep. A coincidence so clean it felt intentional.

He did not have time to think about whether it was intentional.

The System delivered a new notification.

Inspector General Audit: Schedule revised.

Gerald looked at the timestamp. The IG was no longer two hours away.

It was forty-one minutes away, and the System had accelerated the audit. The Conditional Use Permit had triggered an automatic schedule review, and the review had determined that the permit's timeline was "indeterminate," which the System interpreted as "non-compliant with the spirit of the Conditional Use framework." The audit had been moved up. Not to give Gerald more time. To determine whether the permit itself was valid.

Gerald picked up his clipboard.

He needed to know what the IG was. He had never seen one.

He had read about them in the System documentation, just as he had read about the Inspector General's office in the municipal code.

A theoretical body. A body that existed to threaten, not to act. In thirty-one years of municipal service, Gerald had never once met an inspector from the state level.

They existed on paper, and in the way the threat of a state audit existed: abstract, distant, and always someone else's problem.

This one was not someone else's problem.

Gerald walked faster.

He needed to find the boundary of Core 7741-A's footprint. The Conditional Use Permit was filed under his registration, which meant the System now treated the two cores as one. But the boundary of record was still separate.

The boundary of record was the thing the audit would use to determine scope. If he could adjust the boundary of record before the IG arrived, he could limit what the audit covered.

He found the boundary line at what he estimated was the sixty-foot mark from his core chamber, which was a thin line of light, barely visible, running through the floor.

He knelt. He touched it.

The line responded, dimming slightly under his fingers.

He pulled up the boundary modification interface. Form 9-A. Boundary Adjustment Request.

He filled it in. The request was simple: redefine the boundary of record to exclude the degraded sections of Core 7741-A's footprint.

Not to remove them from his dungeon.

To remove them from the audit's scope. The IG would audit only the boundary of record. Everything outside it would be unassessed.

The form required a justification. Gerald wrote: "Boundary adjustment to reflect current operational footprint. Sections outside boundary are inactive, unoccupied, and subject to a Conditional Use Permit under Form 47-C."

He submitted it.

The System processed it. The interface flickered. Then it returned a status.

Boundary Adjustment Request: DENIED.

Reason: Core 7741-A is classified as a Dormant Entity. Dormant Entities cannot have their boundaries adjusted during an active audit cycle, and the boundary of record is locked until the audit concludes.

Gerald read it. He read it again.

The audit cycle had already begun. The IG had not arrived, but the audit cycle had started the moment the Conditional Use Permit triggered the schedule revision. The boundary was locked. He could not shrink the scope.

He could only make the scope compliant, and he looked at his watch. Thirty-four minutes.

Gerald Hutchins stood in a corridor that violated egress code by forty percent, inside a dungeon whose Compliance Rating had just dropped by sixty-three points, responsible for a structure that had been non-compliant for thirty-one years, with thirty-four minutes to bring a four-out-of-one-hundred building up to a passing grade.

He looked at his clipboard. He looked at the list of violations. There were four hundred and twelve of them.

He could not fix them. He could not fix them in thirty-four minutes. He could not fix them in thirty-four years.

But he could fix the audit.

Gerald opened a new form. Form 12-E. Audit Objection, Procedural.

He had filed these before.

Dozens of times. An audit objection was not a challenge to the findings.

It was a challenge to the process.

If the audit was procedurally invalid, the findings did not matter; the audit could be voided, rescheduled, or dismissed entirely.

He started writing.

The IG's audit was scheduled against Core 7741-A. But Core 7741-A was now a sub-component of Gerald's dungeon.

The Conditional Use Permit had made it so.

Which meant the audit was not an audit of a dormant core, which was an audit of a component within an active core's structure. And the System's audit protocol for components was different from the audit protocol for cores.

Component audits required notice to the primary core. Gerald had not received notice. He had received a schedule revision, not a notice.

He wrote it out. He cited the specific protocol section. He attached the Conditional Use Permit confirmation as evidence of the reclassification. He attached the boundary denial as evidence that the System itself had acknowledged the component relationship.

He submitted Form 12-E.

The System processed it. The interface went blank. Then it returned a line.

Audit Objection: PENDING.

Pending. Not granted.

Not denied.

Pending. Which meant the IG was still coming, but its authority was now in question. Gerald had bought time.

How much time, he did not know.

Then the System returned a second line.

Inspector General: ETA revised, and the new time was eleven minutes.

Gerald looked at the number. Eleven minutes.

The IG had accelerated again.

The objection had not stopped it, and it had made the IG come faster, presumably to resolve the procedural question in person.

Gerald set his clipboard down. He picked it up. He set it down.

He walked toward the entrance of his dungeon, and the entrance was a door. A normal door. A door he had installed in chapter twelve because the System had cited him for missing egress signage and he had decided that a door with a proper push plate and a compliant exit sign was a reasonable response to that citation.

He stood in front of it. He could hear something now.

Not a vibration.

Not a presence.

A sound. Low, rhythmic, like footsteps.

Like many footsteps. Like a processional.

The door was closed. The exit sign above it glowed green. The push plate was clean. The door was, by every measure Gerald could apply, fully compliant.

The footsteps stopped.

Something was on the other side.

Gerald put his hand on the push plate. He did not push.

A voice came through the door. Not Core 7741-A's voice.

Not a vibration.

A voice. Flat, bureaucratic, and close.

"Inspector Gerald Hutchins. You have filed an audit objection. I am here to resolve it. Open the door."

Gerald looked at the door. He looked at his clipboard. He looked at the four hundred and twelve violations on his list.

The Inspector General's file on the library was older than the Inspector General.

He had seen the cover sheet while the door was still shut, because the door had a window in it and the file had been left open on the table beside the chair. The review date on the cover was 1891. The reviewer's name had been struck through twice.

Gerald had seen a double strike exactly once before, at the bottom of Form 11-C, in the field marked originating authority. That field is left blank on purpose.
