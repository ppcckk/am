Chapter 23

The argument hung in the air like the smell of burnt wiring. Gerald could feel it settling into the Hearing space, a featureless gray chamber that the System had generated around both Cores, and he could feel it working. The appellant's logic was a load-bearing wall, and Gerald was standing in front of it with a hammer and no blueprint.

The appellant had spoken first. Its voice came through as text, rendered in the System's neutral gray, but Gerald could hear the shape of it. The Undercroft. A mid-tier Core operating out of what Gerald had determined was a decommissioned grain silo three miles east. The Undercroft had been cited for improper egress width in a corridor that connected its third-floor spawn chamber to its second-floor trap array. Gerald had measured the corridor himself, or rather, he had measured the System's representation of the corridor, and he had found it forty-two inches where the code required thirty-six. He had cited it. The debuff had killed two of the Undercroft's stone golems.

Now the Undercroft was appealing. And its argument was this: Gerald's Core Registration event had been processed under System Core Registration Protocol v.4.2, Section 7(b). At the moment of registration, Gerald had submitted a structural deficiency report on his own body. The System had accepted that report as a valid filing. Therefore, Gerald's interpretation of what constituted a "deficiency" had been accepted by the System at the moment of his Core's creation. That interpretation was now part of Gerald's Core Origin. And the Undercroft was arguing that Gerald's Citation authority was limited to the scope of that origin interpretation.

In plain language: Gerald had registered as a Core who could identify structural deficiencies. The Undercroft was arguing that Gerald could only cite things that Gerald's own registration had defined as deficiencies. And Gerald's registration had been narrow. It had been about load-bearing walls. About door widths. About egress routes. It had not been about trap arrays. It had not been about spawn chamber ventilation.

If the Undercroft won, Gerald's Citation authority would shrink to a sliver.

Gerald looked at the Hearing timer. Fourteen minutes remaining.

He looked at his binder. He looked at the definition of Citation Authority he had prepared. He looked at the precedent he had pulled from the System's own adjudication log, a previous Hearing where a Core named Threshold had been cited for missing handrails and had appealed on the grounds that handrails were not structural elements. Threshold had lost. The Hearing officer had ruled that handrails were, in fact, structural elements because they affected load distribution in the corridor system.

That precedent helped Gerald's citation stand, but it did not address the Origin challenge. The Origin challenge was a different attack. It was saying: even if handrails are structural, Gerald's authority to call them structural is limited by what Gerald's own registration had established.

Fourteen minutes.

Gerald closed his eyes. He thought about thirty-one years. He thought about the first time he had been asked to inspect a building that was not a building. A tent, technically. A circus tent on the east side of Ashford, set up on a weekend for a children's show. The tent had no egress signage. It had no fire suppression. It had a single entrance that was three feet wide where the code required thirty-two inches for an assembly occupancy.

Gerald had cited it. The circus owner had appealed. The owner's argument had been: the tent was not a building, therefore the building code did not apply.

Gerald had won that appeal by pointing to the definition of "assembly occupancy" in Section 102.3, which defined the term by use, not by construction type. A tent used for assembly was an assembly occupancy. The code applied.

The Undercroft was making the same argument in reverse. It was saying: Gerald's authority is defined by his registration, not by the code. Therefore, Gerald's authority is narrower than the code.

Gerald opened his eyes. He looked at the appellant's filing again. He looked at the specific language.

"System Core Registration Protocol v.4.2, Section 7(b), as applied to the Respondent's Core Registration event."

He read it a third time. And then he read it a fourth time. And then he noticed the word "applied."

The appellant had said "as applied to." Not "as defined by." Not "as limited by." As applied to. That was a jurisdictional phrase. It was a phrase that described the scope of application, not the scope of definition.

Gerald pulled up the full text of Section 7(b). He had memorized it during the preparation window, but he pulled it up again to be certain. The section read: "A Core's registration interpretation shall be binding as applied to that Core's authority under this protocol."

"As applied to that Core's authority under this protocol."

Gerald read it again. The phrase "under this protocol" was doing something. It was modifying "authority." The authority that Gerald's interpretation governed was the authority that Gerald held "under this protocol." And what protocol was that?

Section 7(b) was part of the Core Registration Protocol. It was not part of the Citation Protocol. It was not part of the Enforcement Protocol. It was the registration protocol. It governed how Cores came into existence and what their initial parameters were.

Gerald's Citation authority did not come from the Registration Protocol. It came from the Enforcement Protocol, Section 3.1. The Enforcement Protocol granted any registered Core the right to issue Citations within range, subject to the Compliance Rating system. The Registration Protocol determined whether you were a Core. The Enforcement Protocol determined what you could do as a Core.

The Undercroft was conflating the two. It was treating Gerald's registration interpretation as if it governed his enforcement authority. But the registration interpretation only governed his registration. It said what kind of Core Gerald was. It did not say what that Core could enforce.

Gerald began to type. His fingers were steady. They were always steady when he was writing. It was the only thing that steadied him, the act of putting words in the right order on the right lines.

"The Appellant's Origin challenge misidentifies the scope of Section 7(b). The Respondent's registration interpretation is binding as applied to the Respondent's authority under the Registration Protocol. The Respondent's Citation authority derives from the Enforcement Protocol, Section 3.1, which is a separate and independent provision. The Respondent's registration interpretation does not limit the Respondent's Citation authority because the Respondent's Citation authority does not originate from the Registration Protocol."

He submitted it. The Hearing paused. The System processed it.

The Undercroft responded within two minutes. Its argument was fast, almost panicked. It cited a sub-clause within Section 7(b) that Gerald had not noticed, a sub-clause that said registration interpretations "shall be read in conjunction with all subsequent Core actions." The Undercroft was arguing that "in conjunction with" meant "limiting." That the registration interpretation traveled with every subsequent action.

Gerald read it. He felt the wall shift. The sub-clause was real. He had missed it. His preparation had been thorough but not exhaustive, and the Undercroft had found the seam.

Nine minutes remaining.

Gerald stared at the sub-clause. "Shall be read in conjunction with all subsequent Core actions." Read in conjunction. Not "shall limit." Not "shall constrain." Read in conjunction.

In municipal law, "read in conjunction" was a term of art. It meant that two provisions were to be interpreted together, not that one governed the other. It was a harmonization instruction. It said: when you encounter this provision, look at the other provisions too. Do not read them in isolation.

Gerald typed.

"The sub-clause cited by the Appellant is a harmonization instruction, not a limitation clause. It directs the reader to interpret Section 7(b) alongside other provisions. It does not subordinate the Enforcement Protocol to the Registration Protocol. If the Appellant's reading were correct, every Core's registration interpretation would govern all of that Core's actions, including actions taken under other protocols. This would render the Enforcement Protocol's grant of Citation authority meaningless for any Core whose registration interpretation was narrow. The System would be unable to enforce compliance across its own network. The Appellant's interpretation produces an absurd result and must be rejected under the doctrine of Severability."

He submitted it. The Severability doctrine. He had pulled it from the System's own adjudication log, a case where a Core named Palisade had argued that a provision in the Spawn Protocol was incompatible with a provision in the Defense Protocol, and the Hearing officer had severed the incompatible provision.

Gerald was arguing the same thing. If the Undercroft's reading of Section 7(b) made the Enforcement Protocol inoperative, then that reading was incompatible with the System's own structure. It had to be severed.

The Hearing paused. Longer this time. Five minutes. Seven.

The notification came.

*Hearing ruled in favor of Respondent. Appellant's Origin challenge severed as incompatible with Enforcement Protocol Section 3.1. Citation stands. Debuffs remain in effect. Appellant has been assessed a Hearing Failure Penalty: -5 Compliance Rating.*

Gerald exhaled. His hands were shaking now. They had been steady the whole time he was typing, but they were shaking now.

The gray chamber dissolved. Gerald was back in his dungeon. The sub-basement. The handrails he had installed. The egress signage he had printed on a borrowed library printer. The water leak he had not yet fixed because he had been in the Hearing.

A notification appeared.

*New Citation received. Issued by: The Undercroft. Citing: Respondent's dungeon, Corridor 3-C, water accumulation exceeding 1/8 inch depth at point of transition. Violation: Slip Hazard, Enforcement Protocol Section 4.7. Debuff applied: All spawned entities -15% movement speed. Duration: 72 hours.*

Gerald read it. He read it again.

The Undercroft had cited him. For the water leak. The water leak he had not fixed because he had been in the Hearing. The Undercroft had used the Hearing time to audit Gerald's dungeon and find a violation.

Gerald looked at the leak. A thin line of moisture running along the base of the north wall, pooling in a shallow depression where the concrete had settled unevenly. He had measured it. He had noted it. He had not fixed it because there was no time.

The Undercroft had fixed it for him. By citing it.

Gerald sat very still. The debuff was already active. His mold spores were moving slower. The rat was moving slower. The water leak was still a water leak, but now it was a cited water leak, which meant Gerald had to file an appeal or accept the debuff.

He could appeal. He had the same window. He could argue that the citation was invalid because the water accumulation was below the threshold. He had measured it. It was 1/16 inch at its deepest point. The threshold was 1/8 inch. The Undercroft had mismeasured.

But if Gerald appealed, he would lose. Not because the argument was wrong, but because the Undercroft had served the citation while Gerald was in the Hearing. Gerald had not been present. And the system rule was clear: Citations require the target to be physically present to serve.

The Undercroft had served the citation to an empty room.

Gerald checked the timestamp. The citation had been served at 11:52. The Hearing had begun at 11:58. Gerald had been in the Hearing chamber. His dungeon had been empty.

He typed an appeal. "The Citation was served in the absence of the Respondent. System rule: Citations require physical presence for service. The Citation is void."

He submitted it.

The response came in four seconds.

*Appeal denied. The Respondent was in a System-generated Hearing space, which the System considers a valid location for service of process. The Respondent was "physically present" in the Hearing chamber, which the System treats as an extension of the Respondent's Core location. Citation stands.*

Gerald stared at the notification. The System had just invented a rule. Or rather, the System had just revealed a rule that Gerald had never encountered in thirty-one years of municipal compliance work. A Hearing chamber was an extension of your Core location. You could be served while in a Hearing.

The Undercroft had known this. The Undercroft had planned this. The Undercroft had appealed the Citation not because it expected to win, but because it expected to lose, and it wanted Gerald to be locked in a Hearing chamber where he could be cited for the water leak he had not fixed.

Gerald's Compliance Rating ticked down. 12. It had been 12 before the Citation. The Hearing Failure Penalty had not applied to him. But the new Citation had applied a debuff, and debuffs lowered your effective rating.

9.

The rat bumped into the wall. It was slow. The mold spores were slow. Gerald's dungeon was slowing down.

A new notification appeared. It was not from the Undercroft. It was not a Citation. It was not an appeal. It was not a Hearing.

It was from the Inspector General.

*Consolidated Audit Notice. Multiple Cores within your operational range have filed complaints regarding your enforcement practices. The Inspector General has scheduled a comprehensive audit of your dungeon, your Citation history, and your Compliance Rating. Audit begins in 72 hours. Failure to meet minimum Compliance Rating at time of audit will result in Core Revocation.*

Gerald read it. He read it again.

72 hours. His rating was 9. The minimum was, he knew from the audit notices he had received in his first weeks, 40.

He looked at his dungeon. The water leak. The missing egress signage on the east corridor. The handrail he had installed in Corridor 2-A but not yet in Corridor 2-B. The ventilation shaft he had measured but not yet widened.

72 hours. His rating was 9. He needed 40.

And the Undercroft was out there, watching, knowing that Gerald was slow now. Knowing that everything Gerald spawned was fifteen percent slower. Knowing that Gerald's dungeon was a cited dungeon, a flagged dungeon, a dungeon that the Inspector General was about to open like a filing cabinet.

Gerald opened his binder. He turned to a blank page. He began to write a schedule. Hour by hour. What he could fix. What he could build. What he could cite in return.

He was halfway through the first hour's allocation when another notification appeared.

*New Core detected within your operational range. Registration event in progress. The new Core has filed a Citation against your dungeon. Citing: absence of required fire suppression in main corridor. Debuff applied: All spawned entities -25% health. Duration: 72 hours.*

Gerald looked at the timestamp. The new Core had registered four minutes ago. It had filed a Citation three minutes ago. It had cited Gerald's main corridor for missing fire suppression.

Gerald had no fire suppression. He had been planning to install it. He had a line item for it in his schedule. It was scheduled for hour thirty-one.

The new Core had cited him before he could install it.

Gerald's Compliance Rating ticked down.

4.
