Chapter 26

The hacksaw caught on rebar at block thirty-one. Gerald twisted the blade sideways, felt it flex, and heard the thin metal scream against the wall. He pulled. The blade came free with a shower of orange sparks.

Block thirty-two. One point. Rating: 14.

He moved to the east wall. Three blocks. He cut them in sequence, each one taking ninety seconds, each one dropping a chunk of stone into the corridor floor with a sound like a dropped cinder block at a construction site. His hands were blistered. The hacksaw's handle was slick with sweat and stone dust.

Block thirty-five. Rating: 17.

He turned to the north wall and found it already cut. Someone had been here. Fresh saw marks, clean and precise, angled at forty-five degrees where Gerald's cuts were perpendicular. The cuts were smaller than his. More careful. Whoever had done this had a better tool and more patience.

Gerald stared at the saw marks. His stomach dropped.

He had not authorized construction work. He had not issued a permit. And now there was a hole in his north wall that he had not cut, which meant it was an unpermitted structural modification to his own dungeon, which meant it was a violation he had not self-reported, which meant the System could auto-detect it.

The notification appeared at the top of his vision.

VIOLATION DETECTED: Unauthorized structural modification. Category: Structural Integrity. Severity: Moderate. Penalty: -4 Compliance Points.

Rating: 13.

He needed twenty. He had three.

Gerald sat down on the floor. The stone was cold through his trousers. He put the hacksaw on his lap and looked at the north wall's saw marks again. The angle was wrong for a load-bearing removal. Whoever had cut this had been testing the wall, not demolishing it. A probe. A survey.

He stood up and walked to the south wall. The last section. Four blocks. He started cutting.

Block thirty-six. Rating: 14.

Block thirty-seven. Rating: 15.

He was halfway through block thirty-eight when the notification came.

AUDIT SCAN: Citation 5 of 5. Biological Entity Citation. Status: INVALID. Reason: Entity was System-generated. Licensing implied by spawn protocol. Penalty for invalid citation: -5 Compliance Points.

Rating: 10.

Gerald stopped sawing. He held the hacksaw in both hands and looked at the half-cut block. He did the math. He was at 10. He needed 20. He had two blocks in this wall and one in the west corridor. That was three more points. He would end at 13.

He was going to fail.

He put the hacksaw down.

The audit timer read 1:47:22. He had one hour and forty-seven minutes before the IG made its determination. He had no more walls to cut. He had no more time to build new compliance features from scratch. A fire suppression system took six hours minimum. A ventilation retrofit took twelve. He could not build his way out of this.

But he could inspect his way out.

Gerald closed his eyes and thought like an inspector. Not a dungeon core. An inspector. What categories of compliance existed that he had not touched? Fire code. Electrical code. Structural code. Egress. Signage. Occupancy load.

He had addressed fire code partially. He had addressed egress. Signage was done. Structural code was the one he had been actively violating by sawing walls. Electrical code. He had not touched electrical code.

He opened his eyes. He looked at the water leak in the northeast corner. The one that had been dripping since chapter three. The one he had never fixed because he had never addressed plumbing code.

Plumbing code.

He pulled up his compliance breakdown. The System listed categories the way a municipal database listed them. Fire Safety: 62%. Egress: 71%. Signage: 88%. Structural: 34%. Electrical: 0%. Plumbing: 0%. Accessibility: 0%.

Zero percent. Three categories at zero.

Gerald moved to the water leak. He pulled up the plumbing code requirements. The System listed them in the same dry, numbered format as any municipal code. Section 14.1: Drainage. Section 14.2: Potable water separation. Section 14.3: Ventilation of waste lines. Section 14.4: Fixture count relative to occupancy.

He had a water leak. That was a fixture. He had no drainage. He had no vent stack. He had no separation between the water source and the waste line, because he had no waste line.

He could not fix the leak. He did not have pipe. He did not have a drain. But he could do something else.

He could remove the fixture.

The System's plumbing code, Section 14.4, stated that a Core could reduce its compliance burden by reducing its fixture count. Fewer fixtures meant fewer requirements. A dungeon with zero plumbing fixtures had no plumbing code violations. It had a zero-percent compliance rating, but zero violations. The distinction mattered. Violations triggered penalties. Low ratings triggered debuffs. Debuffs were bad. Penalties were worse.

Gerald sealed the water leak with a patch of compressed earth. He packed it tight, smooth, flush with the surrounding stone. The leak stopped. The fixture was gone.

PLUMBING CODE: Fixture count reduced to 0. Violations: 0. Compliance rating recalculated.

The plumbing category went from 0% to N/A. Not a violation. Not a penalty. Just absence.

It did not give him points. But it removed a potential future penalty. And more importantly, it gave him a template.

He looked at electrical code. Section 19.1: Wiring. Section 19.2: Grounding. Section 19.3: Overcurrent protection. Section 19.4: Fixture count relative to square footage.

He had no wiring. No fixtures. No electrical system at all. Same logic. Remove the fixture, remove the requirement. But he did not have an electrical system to remove. It was already absent. Why was it at zero percent?

Because the System expected one. A dungeon of 4,000 square feet was required to have some form of illumination beyond natural light. That was a fixture. That was a requirement. And he had none.

Gerald pulled up the electrical code again. Section 19.5: Alternative illumination. "A Core may satisfy Section 19.1 illumination requirements through bioluminescent organisms, magical luminescence, or reflective surface arrangements, provided the combined output meets minimum lux requirements per occupied area."

Reflective surface arrangements. He had stone walls. Stone was reflective. Not well. Not enough. But the code said "provided the combined output meets minimum lux requirements."

He looked at his spawn list. Three mold spores. One rat. The mold was bioluminescent. He had noted this in chapter four and filed it under "unlicensed biological entity" before realizing the entity was licensed.

The mold. It was already producing light. Faint, green, barely enough to read by. But it was producing light. If he could increase the bioluminescence, if he could spread it across more surface area, he could meet the lux requirement without installing a single wire.

He pulled up the mold's stat block. It had a "Luminescence" attribute at 2/10. The code required a minimum of 5/10 across at least 60% of floor area.

He could not increase the mold's luminescence directly. He had no upgrade points. He had no mana. But he could change its environment. Bioluminescent organisms responded to moisture and substrate. The mold was growing on dry stone in a sealed corner. If he gave it moisture, if he gave it a better substrate, it would spread and brighten.

He walked to the sealed water leak. He cracked the patch. One drop of water. Then another. He directed the flow along a channel he carved into the floor with his bare hands, guiding it toward the mold colony.

The mold drank. It spread. Its glow intensified from green to a pale, sickly yellow-green.

BIOLUMINESCENT OUTPUT: 3/10. Still below threshold.

He needed more water. More surface area. He carved another channel. Another. He directed the leak across the floor in a branching pattern, like a river delta. The mold followed the moisture. It spread along the channels, glowing brighter as it colonized new ground.

4/10.

He needed one more point of luminescence and sixty percent floor coverage. The coverage was growing. The glow was not.

He looked at the rat. The rat was sitting in the corner, watching him with beady eyes. It was not bioluminescent. It was not useful. But it was warm. It generated heat. Bioluminescent organisms in cold environments produced less light.

He picked up the rat. It squeaked. He set it on the floor next to the largest mold colony. The rat sat there, confused, radiating its small body heat into the damp stone.

The mold brightened.

5/10.

The notification came.

ELECTRICAL CODE: Alternative illumination method detected. Bioluminescent coverage: 61%. Output: 5/10. Minimum met. Violations: 0. Compliance rating recalculated.

Electrical code went from 0% to N/A. Another category removed from the violation list.

He had not gained points. But he had removed two categories that the IG's scan could cite. The scan was looking for violations. Categories at zero percent were violations waiting to happen. Categories at N/A were not.

The audit timer read 0:34:11.

Rating: 10. He was still at 10. He had not gained a single point. He had only removed future risk.

Gerald sat down again. His hands were shaking. The blistered palms stung against the stone floor. He looked at his dungeon. The walls were thin. The floor was scored with channels. The mold glowed in the dark like a bruise. The rat sat in the middle of it all, grooming itself.

He had failed. The IG would scan his file, find the remaining violations, and apply the penalty. His Compliance Rating would drop below the threshold. His spawns would be debuffed. His dungeon would weaken. He would become vulnerable to the next rival that came through that north wall.

The north wall. The saw marks.

He looked at it again. The cuts were precise. Forty-five degrees. Clean. Someone had been here within the last hour. Someone with a better saw than his hacksaw. Someone who had cut through stone without triggering his own detection systems.

Because he did not have detection systems. He had no alarms. No tripwires. No magical wards. He had a rat and some mold.

The north wall was not just a violation. It was an entry point. Someone had already breached his dungeon. They were still inside.

Gerald stood up. He walked to the north wall and pressed his hand against the cut stone. It was warm. Not from his own body heat. From the other side. From whoever had made the cuts.

A notification appeared.

INTRUSION DETECTED: Unauthorized entity within dungeon perimeter. Entity type: Construct. Size: Humanoid. Threat level: Unknown.

A construct. Not a goblin. Not a rat. A construct. Something built. Something made with intention and precision. Something that could cut stone at forty-five-degree angles without triggering alarms.

Gerald pulled up the construct's details. The System gave him a name and a designation.

ENTITY: SURVEYOR-7. ORIGIN: CORE DESIGNATION "THE ARCHITECT." FUNCTION: Structural assessment. Status: Active.

The Architect. He had not heard of this core. He had not seen it in his citation list. It was new. It had come in during the audit window. It had cut into his dungeon while the IG was scanning his file.

And it was still inside.

Gerald looked at the cut in the north wall. The opening was two feet wide. Enough for a humanoid construct to pass through. Enough for something larger to follow.

He looked at the audit timer. 0:28:44.

The IG would finish its scan in twenty-eight minutes. If the Architect was still inside when the scan completed, the System would log an intrusion event. Intrusion events were not violations against Gerald. They were violations against the intruder. But they also triggered a "Dungeon Compromise" flag on Gerald's file, which the IG would see. A compromised dungeon was a dungeon that could not guarantee its own structural integrity.

The IG would see the flag. It would see the thin walls. It would see the N/A categories and the zero-percent categories and the invalid citation. It would see the intrusion.

Gerald had twenty-eight minutes to remove the construct from his dungeon. He had a rat, some mold, and a hacksaw.

He picked up the hacksaw.

He walked toward the north wall. The cut was warm. Something was breathing on the other side. He could hear it. A soft, mechanical breathing. Like a bellows. Like a pump.

He raised the hacksaw and stepped through the opening.

The surveyor was waiting for him. It was four feet tall, made of pale wood and brass, with a face like a drafting compass and hands that held a measuring tape. It did not attack. It did not flee. It held up the measuring tape and pointed it at Gerald's chest.

A notification appeared.

SURVEYOR-7 has initiated a STRUCTURAL ASSESSMENT of your dungeon. Assessment duration: 15 minutes. Assessment outcome will be filed with the Inspector General as a third-party compliance report.

Gerald stared at the construct. The construct stared at him with its compass-face.

The IG would receive the surveyor's report. The report would document every thin wall, every N/A category, every violation. It would be an independent assessment. It could not be appealed. It could not be retracted. It would be added to Gerald's file as a permanent record.

The surveyor clicked its measuring tape against the floor and began to write.

Gerald looked at the audit timer. 0:26:11.

He looked at the surveyor. He looked at the hacksaw in his hand.

He looked at the measuring tape.

He had been a building inspector for thirty-one years. He had inspected thousands of buildings. He had never once met a surveyor that worked for a rival. But he had met plenty of surveyors who worked for themselves. He knew how they operated. He knew what they valued.

He knew how to make a surveyor stop.

He set down the hacksaw. He straightened his back. He reached into his pocket and pulled out the only thing he had that mattered.

A citation form. Blank. Unfiled.

He wrote the header. He wrote the violation. He held it up to the surveyor.

"Form 7-C," he said. "Failure to present valid inspection credentials prior to commencing structural assessment. You are not licensed to survey in this jurisdiction."

The surveyor stopped writing. Its compass-face tilted. It looked at the form. It looked at Gerald.

The System processed the citation.

CITATION SERVED. Target: SURVEYOR-7. Violation: Unlicensed structural assessment. Penalty: Assessment suspended pending credential verification.

The surveyor's measuring tape retracted. Its writing stopped. It stood very still.

Gerald's hands were shaking. He had served a citation on a construct. He had not known if it would work. Constructs were not Cores. They were not biological entities. They were not people. But they were entities operating within a jurisdiction, and jurisdictions had rules, and rules had citations.

The audit timer read 0:24:03.

The surveyor would be suspended. The assessment would be paused. The IG would not receive a third-party report. Gerald had bought himself time.

But the citation required the target to be physically present to serve. He had served it. The surveyor was still here. The citation was valid. The assessment was paused.

Paused. Not cancelled.

The surveyor would need to present credentials. It did not have credentials. It would need to file an appeal. The appeal window was forty-eight hours.

Gerald had forty-eight hours before the assessment resumed.

He had twenty-four minutes before the IG finished its scan.

He had a Rating of 10 and a dungeon that was falling apart.

The surveyor stood in the middle of his thin-walled, mold-lit, rat-infested dungeon, holding its measuring tape like a weapon, and waited.

Gerald waited with it.

The audit timer counted down. The mold glowed. The rat groomed itself. And somewhere in the dark, the Inspector General turned the page.
