Chapter 63: No Permit

# Chapter 63

The first thing Gerald did was check the load path.

Not because it was heroic. Because it was protocol.

When a structure shakes, you check the load path.

You find where the force enters and where it goes. You trace it through the system until you find the failure point. Thirty-one years of this.

You don't stop doing it just because the structure is also your body.

The second shake was worse. The third was worse than that.

Gerald felt the floor of his central corridor lift two inches and drop, and the sound it made was not the sound of stone settling.

It was the sound of stone being removed. Pulled out from underneath. Like someone had reached into the earth and started eating the foundation.

He turned toward the entrance corridor. The rat was still on the clipboard, still rigid, still facing the direction of the entrance.

Gerald moved.

Not fast.

Gerald was not fast. But he moved with the specific economy of a man who had spent three decades walking job sites, stepping over rebar, ducking under scaffolding, and never once tripping over anything because tripping meant you were not paying attention and not paying attention meant you were going to get hurt.

He reached the entrance corridor and stopped, and the corridor was being taken apart. Not destroyed. Not breached.

Taken apart. The walls were separating along clean vertical lines, exactly at the joints where Gerald had installed the egress signage brackets.

The floor was lifting in sections, exactly at the points where he had anchored the handrail posts.

The ceiling was peeling back in panels, exactly where he had mounted the fire suppression heads.

Someone was dismantling his dungeon along the exact lines of his code-compliant modifications.

Gerald looked at the entrance, and a figure stood there. Not a Core avatar.

Not a spawn.

Not an adventurer. It was humanoid but wrong, too tall, too thin, with joints that bent in directions that made Gerald's eyes water. It wore a hard hat.

An orange hard hat, standard issue, with the System's inspector sigil on the front. It carried a clipboard.

It was a Condemnation Agent.

Gerald knew what this was.

Every municipal employee knew what this was, and a Condemnation Agent was dispatched when a structure's Compliance Rating fell below absolute minimum and no appeal was pending. It physically demolished the structure.

Not metaphorically. Not through System debuffs. It came in and took the building apart, piece by piece, according to demolition protocol, until nothing remained.

Gerald's Compliance Rating was twelve.

The Agent stepped into the corridor. It looked at Gerald. It did not have a face. It had a clipboard where a face should be, and on the clipboard was a line of text that Gerald could read from twenty feet away:

DEMOLITION AUTHORITY: STRUCTURE #0007-ASHFORD. REASON: COMPLIANCE FLOOR BREACH. STATUS: ACTIVE.

Gerald's mind went quiet. The kind of quiet that happens when you are in the middle of a job site and you realize the contractor is going to kill someone if you don't intervene right now.

Not panic.

Focus. The narrow, cold focus of a man who has seen the exact sequence of events that leads to a body bag and knows exactly which link in the chain to break.

He looked at the Agent. He looked at the clipboard. He looked at the corridor being dismantled around him.

"Your survey is incomplete," Gerald said.

The Agent stopped. The clipboard tilted.

Gerald stepped forward.

His hands were shaking. His voice was not.

"You are proceeding with demolition without completing a pre-demolition structural survey.

That is a Class 3 procedural violation. You cannot proceed until the survey is complete."

The Agent did not move. The shaking continued. The walls kept separating. But the Agent itself had stopped, and that meant the protocol was running, and that meant Gerald had bought seconds.

"I am the structure," Gerald said. "I am the primary occupant.

You have not documented occupancy.

You have not posted a notice of intent to demolish.

You have not provided the mandatory seventy-two-hour occupancy clearance period."

The clipboard updated. A new line appeared.

OCCUPANCY NOTED. CLEARANCE PERIOD: WAIVED. AUTHORITY: EMERGENCY CONDEMNATION.

"Emergency condemnation requires a documented imminent hazard," Gerald said. "You have not filed a hazard assessment. You cannot waive the clearance period without a hazard assessment. Check your protocol."

The Agent's clipboard went blank for three seconds. Then it filled again.

HAZARD ASSESSMENT: PENDING. DEMOLITION SUSPENDED PENDING COMPLETION.

The shaking stopped.

Gerald exhaled. His knees were not steady. He did not let them be steady. He looked at the Agent and he said, in the voice he used on contractors who tried to start excavation before the utility locate came back, "Take your time."

The Agent turned. It moved to the nearest wall section, the one where the egress signage bracket was exposed. It produced a measuring tool from somewhere in its impossible body. It started measuring.

Gerald watched. His mind was working. The Agent was following demolition protocol.

He knew demolition protocol. He had enforced demolition protocol for thirty-one years.

And demolition protocol had rules.

The Agent measured the wall section. It looked at the egress bracket. It looked at the wall.

It looked at the bracket again.

It paused.

Gerald knew what it was looking at, and the bracket was anchored into the wall at four points. But Gerald had installed it at an angle, because the corridor was not perfectly square, and he had shimmed the bracket to compensate for the irregularity. To a demolition surveyor, the bracket looked like it might be load-bearing.

It might be transferring load from the ceiling to the floor through the wall. Or it might be purely decorative, a non-structural attachment that could be removed without consequence.

The Agent could not tell. So it filed a Structural Clarification Request. Gerald saw the text appear on the clipboard:

CLARIFICATION REQUIRED: ATTACHMENT #4, EAST CORRIDOR. LOAD-BEARING STATUS: INDETERMINATE. PROCEEDING SUSPENDED PENDING RESOLUTION.

Gerald had built his dungeon with thirty-seven handrails, twelve egress brackets, and nine fire suppression heads.

Each one was anchored at an angle.

Each one had been shimmed. Each one created structural ambiguity because Gerald had built them to code, and code did not require you to make things perfectly square.

Code required you to make them compliant. Compliant and ambiguous were not the same thing.

The Agent moved to the next bracket. It measured. It paused. It filed another Clarification Request.

Gerald started walking. He moved through his dungeon ahead of the Agent, and he did not run.

He walked like he did on job sites.

Slow. Deliberate. Looking at everything.

He was memorizing the sequence.

The Agent would hit each of his modifications in order. Each one would generate a Clarification Request.

Each request would take time, and he had thirty-seven handrails. Twelve brackets. Nine suppression heads. Fifty-eight ambiguities.

He started counting.

The Agent worked methodically. It was slow. It measured each attachment, examined the anchor pattern, checked the shim, looked at the wall substrate, and filed its request. Gerald counted twenty-three Clarification Requests before the Agent reached the fire suppression heads.

At the twenty-fourth, the Agent stopped. It looked at Gerald. The clipboard updated.

CLARIFICATION REQUESTS: 23. RESOLUTION METHOD: FIELD DETERMINATION. AGENT AUTHORITY TO RESOLVE: ACTIVE.

Gerald's stomach dropped. Field determination. The Agent could make its own judgment.

It did not need to wait for a structural engineer, and could look at a bracket and decide it was non-structural and move on.

The Agent moved to the next handrail.

It looked at it.

It looked at Gerald. It reached out and touched the handrail.

It pulled. The handrail did not move. It was anchored into solid substrate.

Gerald had anchored it properly.

The Agent pulled harder.

The handrail bent. Gerald felt it in his spine. The handrail was part of him.

He felt the metal flex, like a bone bending without breaking.

The Agent filed its determination.

ATTACHMENT #24: NON-STRUCTURAL. DEMOLITION AUTHORIZED.

It pulled the handrail out.

Gerald screamed. Not a loud scream.

A short one.

The kind you make when you stub your toe and the pain is sharp and immediate and you cannot stop it. The handrail came free and the Agent set it aside, and Gerald felt the absence like a missing tooth. A gap where something used to be.

The Agent moved to the next one.

Gerald gritted his teeth. He looked at the rat.

The rat had moved, which was no longer on the clipboard.

It was on the floor, near the entrance, facing the Agent. Its body was trembling.

"Run," the rat said again.

Gerald did not run. He could not run. The Agent was inside him. Running meant moving his body, and moving his body meant moving the structure, and moving the structure meant the Agent would see him as a hazard and accelerate.

He stood still. He let the Agent pull out handrail twenty-five. He let it pull out twenty-six.

He let it pull out twenty-seven. Each one hurt.

Each one left a gap, and he counted them. He counted them because counting was the only thing that kept him from doing something stupid, and doing something stupid would get him demolished before the forty-eight hours expired.

At handrail thirty-one, the Agent paused. It looked at the wall. It looked at the clipboard. It looked at Gerald.

The clipboard read:

ANOMALY DETECTED.

ATTACHMENT PATTERN SUGGESTS INTEGRATED SYSTEM. NOT INDEPENDENT ATTACHMENTS.

POSSIBLE STRUCTURAL SUBSYSTEM.

RECLASSIFICATION: PENDING.

Gerald blinked. The Agent was reclassifying his modifications.

It was looking at the pattern of his handrails and brackets and suppression heads and seeing them not as individual attachments but as a system.

A connected network.

A subsystem.

If the Agent reclassified them as a structural subsystem, it could not demolish them individually, and it would have to demolish the entire subsystem at once. And to demolish an entire subsystem, it needed a subsystem-level hazard assessment. Which required more time.

Gerald said nothing. He let the Agent think.

The Agent thought for forty seconds. Then it filed:

RECLASSIFICATION DENIED. ATTACHMENTS DETERMINED INDEPENDENT. DEMOLITION CONTINUES.

Gerald closed his eyes.

Handrail thirty-two. Thirty-three. Thirty-four.

The pain was constant now, a background hum like a building with the HVAC running. His body was being taken apart and he was still standing because Gerald Hutchins had never in thirty-one years left a job site before the inspection was complete.

Thirty-five. Thirty-six. Thirty-seven.

All handrails gone.

The Agent moved to the egress brackets. It pulled the first one. Gerald felt the corridor wall shudder.

The second one.

The third. By the eighth, the wall section itself came loose, a slab of stone that had been held in place by the brackets, and the Agent set it down carefully.

It was following protocol, which was being careful.

Gerald counted the remaining attachments. Twelve brackets. Nine suppression heads. Twenty-one more.

He did the math. The Agent was pulling at a rate of roughly one per forty seconds.

Twenty-one more meant fourteen minutes, and he had forty-eight hours of docketed protection.

The docket was filed. It was active.

But the Agent had been dispatched before the docket was filed. The docket did not stop the Agent. It only stopped new actions against Gerald.

The Agent was already in motion.

Fourteen minutes.

Gerald looked at the rat. The rat was still trembling. Its eyes were closed. Its mouth was open.

The Agent reached for bracket number one.

"Wait," the rat said.

The Agent stopped.

The rat opened its eyes, and they were not rat eyes. They were flat and gray and they looked at the Agent with something that Gerald could not name. Not fear.

Not defiance.

Recognition.

"You are looking for the suppression system," the rat said.

The Agent's clipboard updated.

QUERY: SOURCE OF ENTITY COMMUNICATION.

The rat looked at Gerald.

"You installed them," it said. "The suppression heads. You installed them at the corners.

Four corners. Four heads.

You followed the code."

Gerald looked at the fire suppression heads.

Four of them. One at each corner of his central chamber.

He had installed them because the fire code required four heads per room over a certain square footage. He had followed the code.

"They are not suppression heads," the rat said. "They are containment nodes.

You built a cage.

You put something inside it. And it woke up when you put the fourth one in."

Gerald looked at the center of his central chamber. The floor there was bare stone. There was nothing there. There had never been anything there.

Except, and he looked at the floor.

He looked at the pattern of the handrails he had installed; the pattern that the Agent had just spent twenty minutes pulling out of him.

The pattern that went around the central chamber in a precise rectangle.

The pattern that the Agent had tried to reclassify as a subsystem and failed.

Gerald had not been building a dungeon, and he had been building a containment perimeter; he had done it by accident.

By following code.

By installing handrails at the required intervals and egress brackets at the required heights and suppression heads at the required positions, he had built a cage around something that was sleeping in the center of his dungeon.

Something that was not his spawn. Something that was not his creation.

Something that had been there before him, under the library, in the sub-basement, waiting.

The Agent reached for the first fire suppression head.

Gerald said, "Don't touch that."

The Agent's hand was on the suppression head. It looked at Gerald.

"That is not an attachment," Gerald said. His voice was steady.

His hands were not.

"That is a containment node. You just told me so. Your own protocol requires you to halt demolition of any structure that may contain hazardous material.

You have not filed a hazardous material assessment.

You cannot remove that node."

The Agent's hand stayed on the suppression head. The clipboard updated.

HAZARDOUS MATERIAL PROTOCOL: INITIATED. DEMOLITION SUSPENDED. ASSESSMENT REQUIRED.

The Agent stepped back.

Gerald looked at the floor. The bare stone in the center of his central chamber. The place where something was sleeping. The place he had accidentally caged with code-compliant handrails and fire suppression heads and egress brackets.

The floor cracked, and a sound came from beneath it.

Not a rumble. Not a shake. A breath.

A long, slow, patient breath that moved the air in Gerald's dungeon and made the rat press itself flat against the ground.

The rat opened its mouth.

"It knows you're here," it said.

The floor cracked again.

Wider.

Something pale and smooth and wet was visible in the gap. Something with no edges.

Something that did not look like it had ever been a building, or a body, or a thing that followed rules.

Gerald Hutchins looked at the thing beneath his floor. He looked at the Condemnation Agent, standing frozen, clipboard raised, waiting for a hazardous material assessment that would take hours. He looked at the rat, which was no longer trembling. The rat looked back at him with those flat gray eyes and said, in a voice that was not a rat's voice, "What do you want me to do?"

Gerald looked at the crack. The pale thing beneath it was moving.

Slowly.

Patiently. Like a building settling. Like something long waiting settling into a new position.

He opened his mouth. He closed it. He opened it again.

"File a Form 11-B," he said.

The rat stared at him.

"Unlicensed Biological Entity," Gerald said.

"That is what it is. That is what that thing is.

It is an unlicensed biological entity on my property.

I need to file the paperwork."

The rat said, "You want to file paperwork on that."

Gerald looked at the pale thing beneath the floor. He looked at his dungeon.

His handrails were gone.

His brackets were gone.

His suppression heads were the only code-compliant features left, and they were holding. Barely.

The containment perimeter was failing because he had let the Agent pull out the handrails, and he had let it pull them out because he had been counting. Because counting was what he did.

Because he had been so focused on surviving the Agent that he had not noticed he was dismantling his own cage.

The crack widened.

The pale thing rose. An inch. Two inches.

It had no shape, which had no edges. It had no name that Gerald knew.

But it had a footprint. And it was on his property. And it had no permit.

Gerald Hutchins reached for the clipboard. His hand shook. He picked it up. He uncapped the pen.

He wrote the first line shakier than the second.
