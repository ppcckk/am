Chapter 14: Code Compliance

# Chapter 14

The auditor stood in the corridor with its clipboard out. It was shorter than Gerald expected. Maybe three feet tall, wide as a filing cabinet, with a flat face that had no mouth but did have a horizontal eye slit that tracked Gerald with the patience of a man who had already made up his mind.

"Core Gerald Hutchins," it said. Its voice came from somewhere inside the cabinet-shaped body, flat and compressed. "I am here to conduct a Permit Verification Inspection on Occupancy Registration Form 7-44-C. I require the signing party to be present and coherent for signature confirmation."

Gerald looked at the clipboard. He looked at the auditor. He looked at the corridor behind it, where the walls were still showing the amber glow of Petra's diminishing light.

"Come in," Gerald said.

The auditor stepped inside. It moved like a filing cabinet on tracks. The floor tiles didn't creak.

Gerald closed the door, and he walked to his desk. Sat down. Pulled up the permit form in his System interface and laid it flat on the surface between them.

"Form 7-44-C," Gerald said. "Occupancy Registration.

Signed by Core Petra-Null.

Verified by me as the registering party. You want to confirm the signature."

"Correct."

"Before we proceed, I need to see your Scope of Authorization."

The auditor's eye slit flickered. "This is a Permit Verification Inspection. It falls under Compliance Audit jurisdiction."

"It does not." Gerald pulled up the System's inspection taxonomy.

He'd memorized this taxonomy years ago, back when he'd first realized the System was built on municipal code logic. He'd memorized it like the fire code.

Like the electrical code.

Like everything that kept a building standing.

"Permit Verification is a Type 3 inspection. Compliance Audit is a Type 1.

Different forms.

Different scope.

Different licensure requirement. " He turned the interface toward the auditor. " Your Scope of Authorization reads Type 1 only.

You are not licensed for Type 3."

The auditor was silent. Its clipboard hovered.

"I am authorized under Inspector General Directive 12 to conduct all inspections related to permit validity."

"Directive 12 covers Compliance Audits. It doesn't cover Permit Verifications.

I checked." Gerald didn't say that he'd checked the moment he saw K-7's signature on the perimeter alert.

He'd checked while walking to the door. Forty years of habit. You never open the door for an inspector without knowing what kind of inspection they're running.

"Permit Verification requires a Type 3 licensure. You don't have one. Therefore this inspection is unauthorized." Gerald tapped the desk. "I'm noting it as a procedural void."

The auditor's eye slit widened. The single horizontal line of it stretched, and for a moment Gerald saw something in it that might have been surprise. Or might have been recalculation.

"You are challenging the scope of a Compliance Audit?"

"I'm not challenging a Compliance Audit.

I'm noting that this is not a Compliance Audit. It's a Permit Verification.

And you're not licensed for Permit Verification." Gerald kept his voice flat.

The same voice he used when he told contractors their wiring was wrong. The same voice he used when he told developers their occupancy load was overstated. "If you want to verify this permit, you need a Type 3 license.

Or you need to reclassify this as a Type 1 Compliance Audit, which means you need to inspect the entire dungeon, not just the permit. And if you do that, you need to give me forty-eight hours notice.

Which you didn't."

The auditor's clipboard lowered.

"You are correct," it said.

Gerald didn't blink.

He'd been right before. He'd been right about a hundred inspections.

He'd been right about the fire exit in the Meridian Building. He'd been right about the load-bearing wall in the Kessler renovation. Being right was the job.

Being right was the only thing that mattered.

"I am withdrawing this inspection," the auditor said. "It is void."

"Noted," Gerald said.

The auditor turned. Its cabinet body pivoted on its tracks with a sound like a drawer sliding shut, and it walked toward the door.

It stopped.

"Core Hutchins."

"Yes."

"The permit is still unverified. Your occupancy registration remains provisional. It will be flagged for re-inspection within the audit cycle."

"I'm aware."

The auditor left. Gerald heard its footsteps recede down the corridor. Then silence.

He sat at his desk for a long time.

Then the System notification came.

COMPLIANCE DISPUTE LOGGED.

CORE GERALD HUTCHINS HAS CHALLENGED THE SCOPE OF AN INSPECTOR GENERAL AGENT. PER SYSTEM RULE 14.7, A CHALLENGED AUDIT TRIGGERS A FULL COMPLIANCE AUDIT OF THE CHALLENGING CORE'S DUNGEON. AUDIT WINDOW: 72 HOURS.

AUDIT SCOPE: ALL CATEGORIES.

AUDITING AUTHORITY: INSPECTOR GENERAL.

Gerald read it three times.

Seventy-two hours, and he opened his Compliance Rating panel. The numbers stared back at him.

Structural: 67 out of 100.

Fire Safety: 41. Ventilation: 73. Electrical: 58.

Accessibility: 22. Occupancy Load: 89.

Six categories.

Four of them were in violation territory.

The audit would check all of them. If the IG found violations during a full audit, the penalties weren't debuffs.

They were citations. Multiple citations. Possibly a shutdown order.

Gerald looked at his dungeon layout. He'd built it fast.

Too fast, and the corridors were narrow because he'd prioritized trap placement over circulation space; the fire exits were decorative because he'd assumed he'd fix them later. The ventilation was adequate for the current spawn count but would fail the moment he added the next wave.

The electrical system was a mess because he'd routed power through the same conduits as the water lines, which was a code violation he'd known about from day one but hadn't had time to fix.

And accessibility. God, accessibility.

He'd built a dungeon.

Dungeons weren't supposed to be accessible. But the System's code had an accessibility category, and his rating was 22. He'd never even looked at what that category required.

He pulled up the accessibility requirements.

Corridor width: minimum 36 inches. Door width: minimum 32 inches.

Turning radius: minimum 60 inches at all junctions.

Ramp slope: maximum 1 to 12.

Signage: tactile and high-contrast at all decision points.

His corridors were 28 inches wide. His doors were 24. His junctions were sharp ninety-degree turns with no turning radius at all.

His ramps were 1 to 4 slopes. He had no signage.

He couldn't fix all of this in 72 hours. He didn't have the materials.
