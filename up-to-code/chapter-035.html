Chapter 35: Code Categories Addressed

# Chapter 35

The pen moved because Gerald's hand had been moving since 1987. It did not matter that the pen was made of compressed mana or that the form was printed on a membrane that glowed faintly amber.

The form had sections.

Sections required answers. Gerald answered.

Section 14: Description of Construction Methodology.

He wrote: "Owner performed all structural, mechanical, and electrical work personally. No subcontractors engaged.

Work performed incrementally over approximately nine weeks.

Materials self-sourced from existing dungeon substrate and System-generated resources."

Section 15: Code Categories Addressed. This was the part that mattered.

Most cores would have listed whatever they thought sounded good.

Gerald listed everything, in the order the International Building Code presented them.

Structural. Fire and Life Safety.

Means of Egress. Plumbing. Mechanical.

Electrical. Energy Conservation.

Accessibility.

Interior Environment. Exterior Wall Assemblies. Roof Assemblies.

Special Conditions, and he paused at Special Conditions. His dungeon was underground.

That was a special condition.

He wrote: "Subterranean occupancy. Requires moisture barrier compliance per Section 1803.2.1 and radon mitigation per Section 1803.4. Owner has installed passive ventilation shafts at 200 cubic feet per minute per occupant, exceeding minimum requirement of 15 cfm."

He checked his math. He checked it again. He checked it a third time because the System had fined him for ventilation in Chapter One and he did not intend to be fined again.

Forty-one minutes remained.

Section 16: Certification of Compliance. This required a signature and a statement under penalty of System forfeiture.

Gerald read the penalty clause.

It said that if the certification was found to be materially false, the Owner's Core would be dissolved and its dungeon footprint would be reabsorbed into the System substrate.

Gerald signed, and he hit Submit.

The form vanished. The membrane where it had been lay flat and inert. Gerald stared at it.

Nothing happened. He counted to ten.

He counted to thirty.

He counted to sixty because that was how long he had been told to wait for a response on Form 11-B and nothing had happened then either, except a goblin.

Then the floor changed.

Not dramatically.

It was not alarming to an adventurer. But Gerald felt it the way he felt a building settle.

A subtle shift in the load path.

Something above had changed its mind about where its weight was going.

A section of wall three meters to his left went translucent. Not transparent.

Translucent like frosted glass.

Behind it, a figure stood, which was tall and thin and wore a suit that looked like it had been tailored from a building permit. Its face was a clipboard.

Not metaphorically. Its face was a clipboard. The features were there, but they were printed on the surface of a clipboard that served as the face.

Eyes were checkboxes. Mouth was a signature line.

"Core Hutchins," it said.

Its voice sounded like a stamp hitting paper. "I am Inspector General Unit Seven. I have received your petition.

I am conducting a Live Inspection."

Gerald's throat closed. He had known this was possible.

The addendum had said Approval is at the discretion of the Inspector General, which had not said the Inspector General would come here. It had not said the Inspector General would look like a filing cabinet that had learned to walk.

"You may proceed with your inspection," Gerald said. His voice came out flat. Controlled. The voice he used when a contractor tried to argue that a 32-inch door was close enough to 36 inches.

Unit Seven did not move its feet, and it slid across the floor at a constant rate, like a drawer being opened. It stopped at the nearest wall. It placed one long finger against the surface.

"Structural load path," it said. "Verify."

"Load-bearing walls are eight inches of compressed substrate with a compressive strength of 4,200 pounds per square inch," Gerald said. "I have the calculations."

"Show."

Gerald pulled up the calculations. They were on a System-generated clipboard that looked almost identical to Unit Seven's face, which was either a design choice or a coincidence. Unit Seven read them without moving its eyes. The checkboxes that served as eyes ticked to green.

"Fire suppression," Unit Seven said.

"Fourteen sprinkler heads on a wet-pipe system. Pressure at the most remote head is 18 psi. Minimum is 7."

Unit Seven slid to the next wall. It ran its finger along the baseboard. "Means of egress."

"Two exits. Both 36 inches clear width. Travel distance to nearest exit is 42 feet. Maximum for this occupancy classification is 75."

Unit Seven ticked. It moved to the ventilation shafts.

It moved to the electrical panel.

It moved to the handrails.

At each stop it asked one question and Gerald answered and Unit Seven ticked or did not tick. It ticked at all of them.

Then it stopped in the center of the room and turned to face Gerald.

"You have not been asked about Intent Compliance."

Gerald blinked. "Intent Compliance."

"System Code 12.4.1. A structure built to code but not built with the intent to comply with code is classified as a Procedural Good Faith Deficiency. The Owner must demonstrate that compliance was pursued as an end in itself, not as a means to an unrelated objective."

Gerald felt something cold move through his core. He understood the question. He had asked this question of a hundred contractors in a hundred hearings.

A contractor who installed a handrail because they were afraid of a fine had not built a safe building. They had built a building that happened to have a handrail.

The difference mattered because when the next situation arose, the contractor would not know what to do.

They would only know what they had been told to do.

"I built to code because I am a building inspector," Gerald said.

"I know the code. I know why the code exists.

Every requirement I implemented, I implemented because I understand the hazard it addresses.

The handrail exists because a person slipping on a wet surface without support will fall and break their neck. The sprinklers exist because fire spreads exponentially in enclosed spaces and human reaction time is insufficient to escape without automatic suppression. The egress width exists because a panicked crowd will compress to a minimum density and a door narrower than that density will cause crushing injuries."

Unit Seven was still. Its clipboard face was unreadable.

"Continue," it said.

Gerald continued. He walked the inspector through his dungeon and explained every element.

Not what he had built, but why.

The ventilation because humans produce carbon dioxide and enclosed spaces accumulate it until cognitive function degrades and people make poor decisions.

The moisture barrier because water degrades structural substrate over time and a building that is not protected from water will eventually collapse. The lighting levels because inadequate illumination increases fall risk and also because he had once cited a parking garage for a lux level that had contributed to a pedestrian being struck by a vehicle, and he remembered the name of the pedestrian.

He stopped. He had not meant to say that last part.

Unit Seven's checkboxes all ticked green simultaneously.

"Intent Compliance verified," Unit Seven said. "Emergency Self-Certification approved. You are now a Code Authority under System hierarchy Tier 3."

Gerald exhaled. His hands were shaking. He put them behind his back.

"Your Compliance Rating has been adjusted to 81," Unit Seven said. "Your previous rating was inflated by the absence of a certification record. Now that you are certified, your rating reflects only your actual compliance, not your procedural uncertainty."

"Thank you," Gerald said.

"You are welcome. You will have work orders assigned to you within one to three business days. These are mandatory. Failure to comply with a work order constitutes a violation under your own code."

Gerald stared. "Work orders."

"As a Code Authority, you are now available for assignment by the System. You will be expected to conduct inspections, issue citations, and preside over Hearings. You will not be compensated. You will not be able to decline."

This was not what Gerald had expected. He had expected approval. He had expected to be left alone. He had not expected to be conscripted.

"I understand," Gerald said.

Unit Seven slid backward. It was leaving. Its clipboard face was already becoming translucent, the wall behind it reasserting itself.

"One final matter," Unit Seven said.

Gerald stopped breathing.

"You have a queued Hearing. It was filed by Core Vethrax approximately fourteen minutes ago, while you were completing your petition. The Hearing is a Code Authority Appeal.

As a Tier 3 Code Authority, you are now eligible to preside. The System has assigned it to you."

"Vethrax," Gerald said. He knew the name. He had cited Vethrax in Chapter Twenty-Two for a fire code violation that had killed two of Vethrax's elite spawns. Vethrax had sworn to unmake him.

"The appellant is physically present in your dungeon," Unit Seven said. "Per System Code 6.2.1, a Hearing requires the appellant to be physically present to serve the appeal. The appellant entered through your east egress at approximately the time you signed Section 16."

Gerald's head turned. The east egress. He had not heard anything. He had been so focused on the form.

The corridor was dark. His dungeon's lighting was compliant, 30 foot-candles at floor level, but the corridor was long and the darkness at its far end was not a lighting failure. It was a presence. Something stood there that was large enough to block the light.

"You have thirty minutes to convene the Hearing," Unit Seven said. "Failure to convene within the window will result in an automatic ruling in the appellant's favor and a Compliance Rating reduction of fifteen points."

The wall solidified. Unit Seven was gone. The floor was normal. The air was still.

Gerald stood in the center of his dungeon and looked at the corridor and understood that something was already inside his building, and that he had not detected it, and that it had been there for fourteen minutes, watching him fill out paperwork.

He straightened his back. He reached for the System interface and opened the Hearing protocol, and the first line read: State your name and authority.

Gerald walked toward the east egress. He did not run. He did not call out. He entered slowly with hands visible, as he did in any building with suspected violations, since inspections never surprise occupants.

The thing at the end of the corridor shifted its weight. It was waiting for him.

Gerald kept walking.
