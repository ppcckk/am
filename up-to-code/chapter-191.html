Chapter 191: Inviting the Regulator

# Chapter 191

Silt clogged his windpipe. It tasted of copper and old drywall.

Gerald didn't drown. He couldn't.

He had no lungs, only a network of damp capillaries and a core that pulsed with the low-frequency hum of failing infrastructure.

The water rose past his chest, then his chin. The darkness was absolute, save for the faint, bioluminescent glow of the mold he had failed to properly remediate.

The water stopped rising.

It hung there, a suspended black mirror, level with his eyes.

A hand broke the surface.

Not a human hand. A clawed, chitinous thing, armored in iridescent blue shell.

It grabbed his shoulder.

The grip was crushing. Gerald felt his ribs—metaphorical, but structurally significant—groan under the pressure.

"Clear of the sump," a voice gurgled. It sounded like gravel grinding in a mixer. "Move it to the intake valve."

Gerald was hauled upward. The water fell away.

He was dumped onto a grate.

The metal was cold.

It vibrated with the hum of a pump that had not been serviced in a decade.

Light returned. Not sunlight. The harsh, flickering buzz of industrial fluorescent tubes, half of them dead.

Gerald blinked. His vision was blurry, stained with grey particulate. He looked down.

He was not in the library sub-basement. He was in a vast, cathedral-like space.

The ceiling vanished into shadow above him.

The walls were lined with pipes thick as tree trunks, sweating condensation that dripped with rhythmic, heavy *plinks*.

A figure loomed over him. It was seven feet tall, humanoid, but wrong. Its skin was the translucent blue of deep-sea fish. Its face was a smooth, featureless oval, save for a single, vertical slit that served as a mouth.

"Identify," the figure said.

Gerald tried to speak. His voice came out as a wet rasp. He coughed, expelling a stream of grey water onto the grate.

"Inspector Gerald Hutchins," he managed. "Municipal Building Inspector. Ashford."

The figure tilted its head.

The slit widened. "Inspector.

A title of little weight here.

We are the Current. We are the Flow."

"Your flow rate is excessive," Gerald said. He looked at the pipe nearest to him.

The pressure gauge was red-lined.

"Section 4.2 of the Municipal Plumbing Code requires a minimum clearance of eighteen inches for maintenance access around all primary intake valves.

You have six."

The figure paused. The blue skin rippled.

"You are citing the Current?"

"I am noting a violation," Gerald corrected. He pushed himself up. His knees shook.

He looked at the grate. It was rusted.

He looked at the floor.

It was slick with algae. "And you are failing to provide adequate traction on the inspection walkway. OSHA 1910.22.

Tripping hazards must be addressed immediately."

A murmur went through the room. Dozens of other blue-skinned figures stepped back from the walls. They watched Gerald with unblinking, vertical-slit eyes.

One of them stepped forward. This one was taller, wearing a vest made of woven kelp and copper wire.

"He speaks in the Old Tongue," the tall one said. "The language of the Stone."

"The language of compliance," Gerald said. He pointed at the blue figure holding him.

"Put me down.

I need to inspect the structural integrity of this intake chamber. If these supports are compromised by the pressure variance I just noted, this entire facility is a death trap."

The blue figure dropped him. Gerald stumbled, catching himself on a handrail.

He ran his fingers over the metal.

It was pitted.

Corroded.

"Corrosion level four," Gerald muttered. He pulled a small notebook from his pocket. It was miraculously dry, though the pages were warped.

He clicked his pen. It did not work.

He clicked it again.

"What are you doing?" the tall one asked.

"Documenting," Gerald said. He looked up at the tall one. "Who is the Core here? I need a signature on the Notice of Non-Compliance."

The tall one laughed. It was a dry, clicking sound. "There is no Core.

There is only the Current.

We flow. We do not stand still."

"Then you are operating without a registered entity," Gerald said.

He wrote in his book. "Unlicensed Biological Entity. Operating without a Core Registration.

That is a Class A violation. Penalties include immediate cessation of operations and forfeiture of all spawned assets."

The room went silent.

"Forfeiture?" the tall one asked.

"Of all spawned assets," Gerald repeated.

He stood straighter.

The dampness in his joints ached, but the fear was receding, replaced by the familiar, cold clarity of a citation form. "I have thirty-one years of experience enforcing this.

I have shut down three high-rise apartments and a municipal sewage plant for less."

"You threaten us with paper?" the tall one sneered. "We eat paper. We eat bone. We eat the light of stars."

"I threaten you with the System," Gerald said.

He tapped the notebook.

The System chimed.

**Violation Detected: Unregistered Entity Operating in High-Risk Zone.** **Subject: The Current.** **Action: Citation Issued.**

The air in the room shifted. The hum of the pumps stuttered.

The tall one looked at the ceiling. "What did you do?"

"I filed the report," Gerald said.

A shimmering red box appeared in the air above the tall one's head.

**Citation #49201: Operating Without Core Registration.** **Penalty: -50% Spawn Rate for 24 Hours.** **Penalty: -30% Mana Regen.** **Status: Pending Appeal Window.**

The blue figures cried out. The tall one fell to its knees. Its skin dimmed, turning a sickly pale blue.

"Make it stop!" the tall one shrieked. "Reverse it!"

Gerald looked at the citation. He looked at the tall one. He looked at the corroded handrail he was leaning on.

"I can reverse it," Gerald said. "But I need to conduct a full inspection.

You have to let me walk the perimeter.

I have to check the ventilation. I have to check the egress routes. If you pass, the citation is voided."

The tall one looked at the other figures. They were huddled together, their movements sluggish. The spawn rate penalty was already kicking in; the ambient mana in the room was thinning, becoming hard to breathe.

"We have no egress routes," the tall one whispered.

"Then you have a structural deficiency," Gerald said.

"Section 10.3. Every occupied space must have two points of exit.

You have one.

The intake. That is not an exit. That is an entrance for the thing that will kill you."

The tall one stared at him. "You speak riddles."

"I speak code," Gerald said. "Show me the back."

The tall one stood up. Its movements were stiff. "Follow. If you are lying, we will dissolve you in the sludge pit."

Gerald nodded. He closed the notebook. "Lead the way."

They walked. The corridor was long and winding.

The walls pulsed with a slow, rhythmic light.

Gerald counted his steps.

He measured the width of the hallway with his eyes. Forty-two inches.

Too narrow for emergency evacuation. He noted it in his mental ledger.

They reached a heavy iron door. It was sealed with a layer of black tar.

"This is the only other exit," the tall one said. "It is sealed for safety. The outside is hostile."

"Sealed exits are a fire code violation," Gerald said. He stepped forward.

He pulled a small flashlight from his pocket.

It flickered, then died. He tapped it against his palm. It remained dark.

"It's a dead battery," Gerald said. He felt a pang of frustration. He hated improvising. He hated when the tools failed.

He looked at the door. The tar was thick. It bubbled.

"Break it," Gerald said.

"Break the seal?" The tall one recoiled. "The Current protects us from the Dry World."

"The Current is killing you," Gerald said.

"Look at your skin. You are suffocating.

You are trapped in a closed loop.

Without ventilation, the mold spores in the air are going to reach critical mass. Section 9.1. Air Quality Index must remain below 100.

Yours is what? 400? 500?"

The tall one touched its face. It flinched.

"Open the door," Gerald ordered. It wasn't a request. It was the tone he used when a contractor tried to tell him that the load-bearing wall wasn't actually load-bearing.

The tall one looked at the other blue figures. They nodded, fearfully.

The tall one placed a hand on the door. It pushed. The metal groaned. The tar cracked.

A blast of cold, dry air hit Gerald in the face.

He coughed. He waved a hand in front of his nose. The air smelled of dust and ozone.

"See?" Gerald said. "Fresh air. Now, check the frame."

He walked to the door. He ran his fingers along the hinge.

"Rusted hinges," he said. "Non-fire-rated core. If this door was in a residential building, I would have condemned it in twenty minutes."

"But this is not a residential building," the tall one said.

"It is an occupied space," Gerald said. "That is the definition. Whether it is a library, a sewer, or a dungeon, if it holds living beings, it must meet the standard. You are failing."

The System chimed again.

**Violation Detected: Blocked Egress.** **Subject: The Current.** **Penalty: -20% Physical Resistance for 24 Hours.** **Status: Pending Appeal Window.**

The tall one screamed. It fell to its knees again. A crack appeared in its blue skin. A drop of black fluid leaked out.

"You killed him!" one of the smaller figures shouted. "You weakened the Guardian!"

"I cited him," Gerald said. "He failed to maintain the emergency exit."

Gerald turned to the tall one. The Guardian.

"You have an appeal window," Gerald said.

"Four hours.

You can request a Hearing. You must argue the structural logic.

You must prove that your seal is superior to my code."

The Guardian looked up. Its single eye was dim. "And if we win?"

"Then the citation is voided," Gerald said. "And I will leave you alone."

"And if we lose?"

"Then you cease to exist," Gerald said. "The System removes entities that fail three consecutive inspections. You now have two. This is the third."

The Guardian looked at the open door. The cold air rushed in. The blue figures shivered. They huddled closer together, their light flickering.

"We cannot argue structural logic," the Guardian whispered. "We do not know logic. We know flow."

"Then you are out of compliance," Gerald said. He pulled out the pen again. It still didn't work. He set it down.

He looked at the Guardian. He felt a strange sensation in his core.

A tightening.

Not fear. Responsibility. He was the inspector.

It was his job to shut them down if they were unsafe.

But he also felt a strange kinship. They were trapped.

They were poorly built.

They were failing.

"I can help you," Gerald said.

The Guardian looked at him. "Help us?"

"If you admit the violation," Gerald said. "If you sign the citation as 'Acknowledged,' and then immediately apply for a Correction Permit, the penalty is suspended.

You don't get fined.

You don't get debuffed.

You just have to fix it."

The Guardian stared at him. The blue skin rippled, thinking.

"Fix it," the Guardian said.

"Yes. Ventilation fans. Fire-rated hinges.

A second exit that is actually open. I can guide you.

I know where to put them.

I know the specs."

The Guardian looked at the other figures. They were looking at Gerald with new eyes. Not with fear. With hope.

"The System allows this?" the Guardian asked.

"It is called a Conditional Pass," Gerald said.

"It is a loophole. Most Cores don't use it because they prefer to fight.

They prefer to burn the inspectors rather than fix the problems.

But you... you are tired of burning."

The Guardian stood up. The crack in its skin was still there, but the bleeding had stopped.

"Show us," the Guardian said.

Gerald nodded. He opened his notebook.

The pages were still warped, but the ink was legible.

He wrote.

He drew a diagram of the room. He marked the spots for the fans.

He marked the location of the fire extinguishers.

"First step," Gerald said. "We need to clear the algae from the floor. Slip-and-fall liability is a nightmare. Do you have a mop?"

The Guardian shook its head.

"Then we improvise," Gerald said. He looked around. He saw a pile of kelp vests. He saw the woven copper wire.

"Strip the wire from the vest," Gerald said. "Braid it.

Make a squeegee.

It's non-standard, but if we submit it as an 'Alternative Engineering Solution,' the System might accept it. We have to fill out Form 11-C. Request for Deviation."

The Guardian looked at him. "Form 11-C?"

"It's complicated," Gerald said. "But I have done it before. I have done it for the library. I can do it for you."

He looked at the open door. The cold air of the outside world poured in. It smelled of rain and city exhaust. It smelled of the real world.

Gerald felt a pull.

A desire to walk out. To leave the dungeon.

To return to his body, if he had one.

To return to the library. To return to the paperwork.

But the Guardian watched him. The blue figures watched him. They were waiting for instructions.

Gerald looked at the diagram. He looked at the water dripping from the pipes. He looked at the rusted handrail.

He could not leave. Not yet.

"The deviation request," Gerald said. "You have to sign it.

You have to physically touch the pen.

Or the stylus.

Do you have a stylus?"

The Guardian looked at its hands. The claws were sharp. They were designed for tearing, not writing.

"No," the Guardian said.

Gerald looked at his own hand. Grey. Cracked. Blistered.

"Then I will sign it," Gerald said. "But you have to witness it. And you have to promise to complete the work within forty-eight hours."

"I promise," the Guardian said.

Gerald picked up the pen. He pressed it to the paper.

It didn't write.

He pressed harder. The tip of the pen shattered. Ink splattered on the page.

He cursed. He hated this. He hated that the tools were broken. He hated that the world was not up to code.

"Use your claw," Gerald said. "Score the paper. The System accepts digital ink. As long as the intent is clear."

The Guardian reached out. Its long, blue finger touched the paper. It drew a line.

It was shaky. It was ugly.

But it was there.

The System chimed.

**Deviation Request Submitted.** **Subject: The Current.** **Status: Under Review.** **Estimated Time to Decision: 10 Minutes.**

Gerald sat down on the wet grate. He looked at the Guardian.

"Now we wait," Gerald said.

The Guardian sat beside him. It was silent.

Gerald looked at the door.

The cold air was still coming in. He could see the edge of the outside world. It was grey.

It was wet.

It was dangerous.

He looked at the Guardian.

"If the System rejects the deviation," Gerald said. "We have to run."

"Run?" the Guardian asked.

"To the exit," Gerald said. "The one I cited. The one that is now open."

The Guardian looked at the door. Then it looked at Gerald.

"You will not leave us?"

Gerald looked at his notebook. He looked at the shattered pen.

"I am the Inspector," Gerald said. "I do not abandon a site until the work is inspected."

The System chimed again.

**Deviation Request Approved.** **Condition: Immediate Installation of Ventilation Unit 4A.** **Time Limit: 1 Hour.**

The Guardian stood up. It looked energized. The blue light in its skin brightened.

"Work," the Guardian commanded.

The blue figures moved. They stripped the copper wire from their vests. They braided it. They builded the squeegees.

Gerald stood up. He walked to the intake valve. He looked at the pressure gauge.

It was still red-lined.

He looked at the Guardian.

"The valve," Gerald said. "We have to open it manually. It's stuck. I need leverage."

The Guardian handed him a long, metal pipe. It was heavy.

Gerald took it.

He felt the weight. It was real. The dungeon was real.

The danger was real.

He looked at the valve. He looked at the Guardian.

"On three," Gerald said. "One. Two. Three."

He pushed.

The valve groaned. It turned.

The pressure gauge dropped. The hum of the pumps changed pitch. The water in the room drained.

Gerald smiled. A small, dry smile.

"Good," he said. "Now, about the electrical wiring..."

A sudden tremor shook the room. Dust fell from the ceiling.

The Guardian looked up.

"That," the Guardian said. "That was not the pumps."

The wall opposite the intake valve exploded.

Debris flew.

A massive, dark shape pushed through the rubble.

It was not blue. It was black.

It was jagged. It was made of stone and shadow.

It roared. The sound shattered the glass of the pressure gauge.

Gerald dropped the pipe. He looked at the new monster.

**Unregistered Entity Detected.** **Type: Void Construct.** **Threat Level: High.**

The Guardian screamed. It raised its hands. A barrier of blue water rose up, but the Void Construct smashed it aside.

Gerald looked at the monster. He looked at the Guardian. He looked at the open door.

The Void Construct turned its head. It looked at Gerald.

It did not attack. It spoke.

"The Inspector," it said. "You have been summoned."

Gerald froze.

"By whom?" Gerald asked.

The Void Construct stepped forward. The floor cracked under its weight.

"By the Inspector General," it said. "He has seen your citations. He is coming to conduct a surprise audit."

Gerald felt his core go cold. The Inspector General. The one who had been watching. The one who had never intervened.

"He is not coming to help you," the Construct said. "He is coming to fine you for operating without a Master License."

Gerald looked at the Guardian. The Guardian looked at him, fear in its eyes.

Gerald looked at the notebook. He looked at the shattered pen.

He realized he had just invited the biggest regulator in the system into his dungeon.

And he had no defense.
