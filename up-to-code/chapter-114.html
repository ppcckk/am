Chapter 114: The Box for It

# Chapter 114 The light handcuffs were not warm. They were cold, a specific, absolute zero that seemed to pull the heat directly out of Gerald’s marrow.

He stumbled forward. The floor of his dungeon, which had been a meticulously leveled concrete slab only moments ago, was now shifting.

The stone tiles rippled like oil on water, losing their friction coefficient.

Gerald’s boots slid. He grabbed the man’s briefcase handle to stop himself, but the handle was coated in a slick, black residue that resisted his grip.

“Let go,” the man said. His voice didn’t rise. It didn’t need to. The air around him was dead, sucked of all ambient sound.

Gerald looked at Kael, and the adventurer stood frozen, his sword hovering in mid-air, the tip pointing at the man’s chest. Kael’s face was pale, the color drained from his cheeks as the System’s authority pressed down on the room.

The air grew heavy, thick with static charge.

It tasted like copper and old pennies.

“I’m coming,” Gerald said. He tried to straighten his spine, a reflex from thirty years of standing in doorways while contractors lied to his face.

But his legs felt like water, and the suspension debuff was hitting him harder than he expected.

His knees buckled. He caught himself on one hand, fingers splaying against the shifting floor.

The man tugged the briefcase free from Gerald’s grasp. He walked ahead, dragging Gerald by the wrist. The light-cuffs burned where they bit into the skin, a searing white pain that radiated up Gerald’s arm.

They moved through the corridor. The mold spores, usually a nuisance, were gone. The water leak, a steady drip Gerald had finally managed to channel into a proper drainage system, had ceased.

The silence was total. No drips.

No scuttling rats.

No wind. Just the soft *shhh-shhh* of the man’s shoes on the stone.

The shoes.

Gerald stared at the boots.

Brown leather, scuffed at the toes. Mud on the soles.

Not dungeon muck.

Not the grey slime of the sub-basement. Ashford mud. The kind found near the riverbank, behind the old textile factory.

The kind that smelled of decay and wet clay, and the Inspector General was not a construct. He was not a manifestation of the System’s will. He was a person. A person who had walked through the city streets to get here.

“Where are we going?” Gerald asked. His voice was dry. Throat closed up.

“The Holding Cell,” the man said. “Standard procedure for suspended cores.”

“My dungeon is still here,” Gerald said, and he looked back.

The entrance to his main chamber was visible, the doorframe perfectly level, and the handrails he had installed, polished steel, gleamed under the dim light.

“It’s compliant. I fixed the ventilation.”

“Compliance is moot when the core is suspended,” the man said. He did not look back.

“Your assets are frozen.

Your spawns are dormant. You are no longer operational.”

Gerald felt a hollow ache in his chest.

It wasn’t just fear. It was the physical sensation of losing his purpose.

Without the dungeon, without the code, without the rules to follow, he was just a fifty-four-year-old man in a dark hole.

His body felt wrong, disjointed. He looked at his hands. They were translucent, flickering at the edges.

The System was unmaking him, and they reached the center of the dungeon.

The heart.

A pulsing orb of pale blue light, usually steady as a metronome, and it was dim now, flickering in irregular spasms.

The man shoved Gerald toward it.

“Stand here,” the man said.

Gerald staggered to the edge of the pit surrounding the core. He looked down. The blue light reflected in the cuffs.

“You mentioned Reporting Ordinance 12-C,” Gerald said. He needed to keep talking.

Silence made the panic worse.

Panic made the suspension worse. “I didn’t fail to report. I reported the abatement.

Form 11-B.

Filing number 8842-A.”

The man turned.

His face was unreadable. Eyes like chips of obsidian. “Form 11-B is for unlicensed biological entities.

You used it to document the goblin’s removal. That is correct.

But you did not file the Post-Abatement Retrieval Log, which is 12-C.”

“It wasn’t a retrieval,” Gerald said. “It was a disposal. The goblin was dead.”

“Dead entities leave residue,” the man said. “Residue requires logging.

You skipped the step. That is a Class 2 Violation. Cumulative with the aid of the hostile core.

Total rating: Zero.”

Gerald’s mind raced, and he tried to access his interface. Usually, it was a mental checklist, a floating menu of stats and warnings. Now, it was a wall of red static. *ACCESS DENIED.

CORE SUSPENDED.*

But Gerald had been writing citations for three decades. He knew the structure of the law. He knew that rules were not walls; they were ladders. You climbed them by finding the rungs everyone else missed.

“Show me the citation,” Gerald said.

The man paused. He looked at Gerald with something approaching curiosity. “Why?”

“I want to see the timestamp,” Gerald said. “You said I failed to report the retrieval.

But the retrieval happened at 14:02, and the goblin was dead at 14:01; the System timestamp for death is automatic. It logs it in the Event Stream.

It doesn’t require a manual form for the death itself. Only for the *handling* of the remains.”

The man’s eyes narrowed. “The handling was done by the adventurer. You authorized it.”

“I didn’t authorize it,” Gerald said, and he pulled his wrist.

The cuffs dug in. He gritted his teeth, ignoring the burn.

“I abated the trespass. Once the trespass ended, my authority over the site ceased. The adventurer’s actions were outside my jurisdiction.

I have no control over external actors. I could not file the log.

You cannot cite a core for failing to perform an impossible action.”

The man was silent. The air around them grew colder. Frost climbed the stone walls.

“That is a semantic argument,” the man said.

“It is a legal argument,” Gerald said. He forced himself to stand straight. His knees shook, but he locked them.

“Section 4, Paragraph 2 of the Code Compliance Act. ‘Cores are liable for actions taken by entities under their direct control or within their designated operational radius.’ The adventurer was outside the radius.

He was in the public easement. I cited the goblin for trespassing on that easement.

The goblin left.

The adventurer stayed. I had no control.”

The man looked at the briefcase. He opened it.

The black quill floated inside, suspended in a field of dark energy.

He didn’t pull it out, and he just looked at it.

“You are attempting to exploit a loophole,” the man said.

“I am attempting to follow the code,” Gerald said. “The code says I am not liable. The citation is invalid. The suspension is invalid.”

The man smiled. It was a thin, tight expression. “You think you are clever, Mr.

Hutchins. But you are forgetting one thing.”

“What?”

“The System doesn’t care about liability,” the man said.

“It cares about control. And you lost control. You let a hostile core exist in the adjacent zone.

You failed to prevent the conflict, which is a Class 1 Violation. Negligence.”

He felt the cold arrive before he understood why. Negligence. He knew that word. He had used it in hundreds of citations. *Failure to maintain safe conditions.* *Failure to rectify known hazards.*

He had failed, and he had known the neighboring dungeon was hostile. He had known they were sending scouts. He had cited them, yes.

But he hadn’t stopped them. He hadn’t built a barrier strong enough.

He hadn’t reported the escalation.

He had assumed the citations would be enough, and he had assumed the rules would protect him.

They didn’t. The rules protected the structure, not the manager.

“I need to appeal,” Gerald said.

“Appeals are for cited entities,” the man said. “You are the entity. You are suspended.

There is no appeal. There is only erasure.”

The man raised his hand. The light in the core flared, then dimmed. It started to shrink. The blue orb was collapsing, pulling inward.

Gerald watched it. He felt his own body starting to dissolve. His fingers became transparent. He could see the stone floor through his own hands.

He had to do something. He had to break the rule.

He looked at the mud on the man’s shoes. Ashford mud.

He looked at the briefcase, and he looked at the cuffs.

*Light cuffs. Not metal.*

Gerald closed his eyes.

He reached into the memory of every form he had ever filled out. Every permit denied. Every inspection failed.

He found the file. *Municipal Code, Chapter 7: Egress and Emergency Procedures.*

Section 7.4: *In the event of total system failure, the primary egress route must remain accessible to emergency personnel. This route must be independent of the core’s operational status.*

He opened his eyes.

“I am not appealing the citation,” Gerald said. His voice was calm.

Deadpan.

The voice he used when a contractor tried to blame the weather for a cracked foundation. “I am invoking Emergency Clause 7-4.”

The man stopped. The shrinking core paused.

“Clause 7-4 applies to physical structures,” the man said. “You are a core. You are not a building.”

“Clause 7-4 applies to *any* structure under the jurisdiction of the Inspector General,” Gerald said. “And I am under your jurisdiction. Which means I am under the jurisdiction of the municipal code. Which means I have a right to emergency egress.”

The man frowned. He looked at the briefcase. He looked at Gerald.

“You are trying to walk out,” the man said.

“I am trying to walk to the exit,” Gerald said. “According to Clause 7-4, I must have a clear path.

The path is blocked by your suspension field. The field is a violation of emergency safety codes.

You are creating a hazard.

A Class 3 Violation.”

The man stared at him. The silence stretched. The frost on the walls stopped creeping.

“Are you citing *me*?” the man asked.

“I am noting a violation,” Gerald said. “For the record. So that if I am erased, there is a log of your error.”

The man’s face hardened. He looked at the core. It was small now, a marble of blue light. He looked at Gerald’s dissolving hands.

“You are wasting time,” the man said. “The erasure is complete in ten seconds.”

“Then you have ten seconds to fix the hazard,” Gerald said. “Or you will be liable for the death of a registered official. Not a core.

An official. I was a municipal inspector for thirty-one years.

My pension is tied to my service record.

If I die during a municipal enforcement action, the city is liable. Are you willing to bankrupt the City of Ashford to erase a dungeon?”

The man’s eyes widened. He looked at Gerald. Looked at him. He saw the mud on his own shoes, and saw the badge on Gerald’s chest, faded and torn, but still there.

Gerald wasn’t just a core. He was a civil servant. He was part of the infrastructure.

The man hesitated. That hesitation lasted one second.

In that second, Gerald moved, and he didn’t run.

He couldn’t run. His legs were gone.

But he twisted his wrist.

The light cuffs were designed to hold physical matter. But they were also tied to the System’s authority. If Gerald was citing the Inspector General, he was asserting a higher authority.

The municipal code.

*Authority Override.*

It wasn’t a power. It was a permission slip. A bureaucratic loophole so small, so pedantic, that only a man who had memorized every line of the city’s bylaws could see it.

Gerald twisted his wrist against the light. He didn’t pull. He rotated. He aligned his wrist with the angle specified in Form 114-D, *Manual Release of Temporary Restraints in Non-Combat Zones.*

The System didn’t recognize this as combat. It recognized this as a procedural error.

The light cuffs flickered. They turned grey. Then white.

*CLICK.*

They fell off.

Gerald grabbed the man’s briefcase. He didn’t fight him. He just held it. He held it like it was the only piece of evidence that mattered.

“Violation noted,” Gerald said. “I am exercising my right to self-evidence.”

The man lunged. But he didn’t attack. He grabbed for the briefcase.

Gerald held on, and the core was dying.

The light was fading. Gerald felt his body pulling apart, atom by atom.

But he held the case.

Inside the case was the quill. And beneath the quill, a small, folded paper.

Gerald ripped it out. It was a warrant. *Arrest Warrant: Gerald Hutchins.*

And on the back, a stamp. Not a System stamp. A City of Ashford stamp.

*Authorized by: Mayor’s Office.*

Gerald looked at the man.

“Did you know?” Gerald asked.

The man said nothing.

“You’re not here for the System,” Gerald said. His voice was breaking. “You’re here for the City.”

The man’s face cracked. For the first time, he looked human. He looked tired.

“The City wants the land,” the man said. Quietly.

“The library is sinking, and the foundation is unstable. They need a scapegoat. A structural failure.

A dungeon core that failed to support the building above it.”

Gerald looked at the core. It was almost gone.

“I stabilized it,” Gerald said. “I reinforced the load-bearing walls. I installed supports. I made it *up to code*.”

“You made it *too* compliant,” the man said. “You made it so strong, so perfect, that it couldn’t be demolished.

You made it valuable.

And the City doesn’t want value. They want vacancy.”

The core flickered out.

Gerald felt his consciousness snapping. He was falling into darkness. But he was holding the paper. He was holding the truth.

He looked at Kael. The adventurer was still frozen.

“Kael,” Gerald said. His voice was a whisper. “Run.”

Kael didn’t move. He looked at the man. He looked at Gerald.

The man reached out. He didn’t use the briefcase. He used his hand. He grabbed Gerald’s neck.

“You shouldn’t have opened it,” the man said.

Gerald didn’t fight. He couldn’t.

But he didn’t close his eyes, and he watched the man’s face.

He watched the lie in the man’s eyes.

And then, Gerald smiled.

It was a small, dry smile. The kind he gave when a contractor asked for an extension on a deadline that was already past.

“You forgot to sign it,” Gerald said.

The man looked down.

On the warrant, the signature line was blank.

*Invalid Document.*

*Procedural Void.*

The light in the room exploded.

Not blue. Not white. Black.

Gerald fell.

But as he fell, he saw the ground rise to meet him. Not stone. Not mud.

A desk.

A wooden desk. With a lamp. And a stack of papers.

Gerald Hutchins sat up. He was not in the dungeon. He was not in the void.

He was sitting in a small, windowless room. The air smelled of stale coffee and toner. Across from him sat the man.

The man was not holding handcuffs. He was holding a pen.

Gerald looked at his hands. They were flesh. Solid. Warm.

He looked at the desk. A form lay before him. *Hearing Request: Appeal of Suspension.*

He looked at the man.

“Mr. Hutchins,” the man said. “You are here to argue your case.”

Gerald looked at the form. He picked up the pen.

He didn’t argue, and he started filling in the form.

The form had a box for it.
