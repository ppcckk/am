Chapter 17

The ledge was thirty-one inches. Gerald measured it again. Thirty-one inches. He pressed his thumb against the stone and felt the cold dampness seeping through the limestone, and he thought: thirty-one inches is not thirty-four inches, and the code does not care about your feelings.

He pulled his measuring tape back. The hook end caught on a rough patch of wall and he had to work it free with his fingernail. Sixteen hours of work and he still could not claim the handrail credit. The ledge was a natural feature, not an installation. If he could prove it functioned as a handrail under Retroactive Compliance, he would get two points for the structural category. But the height was wrong. Code required thirty-four inches minimum. He was three inches short.

He sat down on the floor. His mana was at nine percent. Every measurement cost him something. Not mana directly, but the System tracked his activity as "management actions" and each one drew from the same pool. He had been working for eleven hours since the notification. He had spent four percent on measurements, two on filing the water leak claim, and one on a failed attempt to reclassify his ceiling crack as a "controlled expansion joint."

Rating: forty-nine. He needed sixty.

He opened the System interface and scrolled through the code sections again. He had read them all. He had read them twice. He knew the building code of the System better than he knew the municipal code of the City of Ashford, which was saying something, because the municipal code was four hundred pages and the System's code was only two hundred and twelve.

Section 2.7. De minimis.

He stopped scrolling. Read it again.

"Any deficiency with a remediation cost below five mana points is considered de minimis and shall not be factored into Compliance Rating calculations."

He had read this before. He had dismissed it because he assumed it applied only to violations he had already been cited for. But the language was broader than that. "Any deficiency." Not "any cited deficiency." Any deficiency at all.

He looked at his dungeon. Really looked. Not as a dungeon core looking at his territory, but as an inspector looking at a building. And what he saw was not a dungeon. It was a sub-basement with a list of problems.

Missing egress signage. Remediation cost: two mana. De minimis.

Inadequate lighting. The only light source was a bioluminescent mold colony that provided roughly 0.3 foot-candles. Code required one foot-candle minimum in circulation areas. Remediation cost: one mana to install a second colony. De minimis.

Missing handrails. The ledge was thirty-one inches. Remediation cost: four mana to build a proper handrail at the correct height. De minimis.

No fire suppression. Remediation cost: fifteen mana. Not de minimis. He would have to address this the hard way.

No ADA-compliant ramp. His entrance had a four-inch step. Remediation cost: eight mana. Not de minimis.

But the three de minimis items. If he filed for de minimis exemption on all three, they would stop counting against his rating. He pulled up the filing form. Form 7-C. De Minimis Exemption Request. He had never filed one before because he had never needed one. In thirty-one years of municipal work, he had filed approximately four hundred de minimis exemptions. The form was muscle memory.

He filed.

The System processed. A notification appeared.

**NOTICE: De Minimis Exemption granted for 3 deficiencies. Compliance Rating adjusted: 49 → 55.**

Six points. He needed five more.

He sat with that for a moment. Fifty-five. Not enough. But closer than he had been an hour ago. He allowed himself one breath of something that was not quite satisfaction but was adjacent to it.

Then a new notification. Red border. Complaint class.

**COMPLAINT FILED AGAINST CORE "HUTCHINS, G."** **Filed by: Core "Vellum"** **Allegation: Unpermitted Structural Alteration** **Code Reference: Section 19.4 (Corridor Length Requirement)** **Status: Priority Review Triggered** **Effect: -10 Compliance Rating during audit review** **Duration: Until resolved or audit concludes**

Gerald read it twice. Then he read it a third time.

Priority Review. He had never triggered one before. He had issued Citations, yes. He had won Hearings. But a Complaint was different. A Complaint was a third party asking the System to take a closer look. And if the auditor arrived to find an active Complaint on the file, the audit was automatically elevated. The Compliance Rating would be calculated with the penalty applied. Fifty-five minus ten. Forty-five.

He would be demoted. Not in seventy-two hours. In forty-two.

He opened the Complaint details. Section 19.4. He knew this section. Corridors exceeding twelve feet in length without a change in direction or demonstrated purpose were classified as Unpermitted Structural Alterations. His loop corridor was forty feet. It was unpermitted. He had never filed for a variance because he had never thought it needed one. The loop corridor was a circulation path. It served a purpose. He moved through it every day.

But the code said twelve feet. His corridor was forty.

He checked the filing date. The Complaint had been submitted six minutes ago. Vellum. He had never heard of this Core. He searched his records. No prior interaction. No Citation history. No Hearing record.

He read the Complaint's code reference again. Section 19.4. He pulled up the current version of the code. Version 3.4. He scrolled to Section 19.4.

The text was different from what the Complaint cited.

The Complaint cited: "Any corridor exceeding twelve feet in length without a change in direction or purpose shall be classified as Unpermitted Structural Alteration."

The current version read: "Any corridor exceeding twelve feet in length without a change in direction shall be classified as Unpermitted Structural Alteration. Exception: corridors exceeding twelve feet are exempt where they serve a circulation function demonstrably beneficial to dungeon navigation."

The exception had been added in Version 3.3. The Complaint cited the pre-amendment text. Vellum was using outdated code.

Gerald pulled up Form 12-D. Motion to Dismiss. He filled it out with the precision of a man who had filed ten thousand motions in his life. He cited the version discrepancy. He cited the current text. He cited his own circulation logs, which showed he used the loop corridor an average of fourteen times per day.

He filed.

**MOTION TO DISMISS: GRANTED.** **Complaint dismissed on procedural grounds (outdated code reference).** **Priority Review penalty: REMOVED.** **Compliance Rating: 55 (unchanged).**

He exhaled. Not relief. Not yet. He had won a technicality. Vellum would file again. Vellum would use the correct code version next time. He needed to deal with Vellum directly.

He opened the Complaint metadata. Every filing carried a location tag. The System required it. You could not file a Complaint against a dungeon without specifying the target's coordinates. Vellum's coordinates were embedded in the filing.

Gerald looked at the coordinates.

They were inside his dungeon.

He stood up. He walked to the loop corridor. He walked the full forty feet, past the section where the wall was patched, past the section where he had carved his exemption notations, past the section where the compressed dust patches marked his first successful Citation. He walked to the far end of the loop.

The wall on the north side. He pressed his palm flat against it.

Something pulsed.

Not his own mana. Not the ambient energy of his dungeon. Something else. Something foreign and small and rhythmic. A heartbeat. A core pulse. Six feet from his own core. Embedded in his wall.

Vellum was inside him.

Gerald pulled his hand back. He stared at the wall. The stone was seamless. He had inspected this wall a hundred times. He had measured it. He had noted its thickness, its composition, its load-bearing capacity. There had never been anything wrong with it.

Because Vellum had not been here before. Vellum had grown into the wall. Slowly. Quietly. Extending its own structure into Gerald's stone until it was indistinguishable from his own. The loop corridor's far end. The section Gerald had always assumed was solid bedrock. It was not bedrock. It was Vellum.

He calculated. Vellum's core was six feet from his own. He checked the growth rate. The wall section where Vellum was embedded was approximately two feet thick. Vellum's pulse was strongest at the near edge. If Vellum was growing at the rate the System's growth tables suggested for a Class 2 Core, it would reach Gerald's core in thirty-eight hours.

The audit was in forty-two.

Two Cores in one footprint. The System's rule was absolute. Automatic Class 1 demotion for both. No hearing. No appeal. No exceptions.

Gerald looked at the wall. He could destroy it. Smash the section where Vellum was embedded. That would sever Vellum's connection to Gerald's structure. But the wall was load-bearing. He had verified this himself. Destroying it would create a structural deficiency. The remediation cost would be significant. His Compliance Rating would drop.

He was trapped. Destroy Vellum and lose points. Leave Vellum and lose everything.

He pressed his palm against the wall again. The pulse was steady. Calm. Patient.

Then a new notification appeared. Not a Complaint. Not a Citation. Something he had never seen before.

**TRANSFER REQUEST INITIATED** **From: Core "Vellum"** **To: Core "Hutchins, G."** **Request: Full footprint transfer** **Response window: 6 hours** **If no response: Automatic acceptance**

Gerald stared at the notification. Vellum was not just growing toward his core. Vellum was trying to take his dungeon. The Transfer Request was a System mechanic he had read about in the code but never encountered. A Core could request ownership of another Core's footprint if it could demonstrate "structural integration exceeding 40%." Vellum had been growing inside his walls for days. The integration percentage was probably already above the threshold.

Six hours. If Gerald did not respond, the transfer would be automatic. His dungeon would belong to Vellum. His Compliance Rating, his citations, his exemptions, all of it would transfer. He would be nothing. A hole in the ground with some rats in it.

He looked at the wall. The pulse continued. Steady. Patient.

He had six hours. And the audit was in forty-two. And Vellum was inside his walls. And the only way to prove the transfer was invalid was to demonstrate that the structural integration was not consensual. He would have to prove Vellum had invaded without permission. He would have to prove the wall was his. He would have to prove it before the transfer window closed.

He opened his inventory. Mana: eight percent. He had one option. He could file an Emergency Injunction. Form 9-F. It required a structural survey proving the contested section was original to his dungeon. He would need to measure the wall. He would need to prove its composition matched his dungeon's baseline. He would need to do it in six hours with eight percent mana.

He pulled out his measuring tape.

The hook end caught on the wall. He worked it free.

He started measuring.
