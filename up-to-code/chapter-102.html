Chapter 102: Bureaucratic Egress

# Interlude: The Delve, Part Two

The hearing was held in the map room, which was a room with a map in it, and the map was of the dungeon, and the dungeon was not on the map.

Halla had expected a table. There was a table. It had been pushed against the wall, and the wall had a water stain shaped like a boot, and the table had four legs of which three were the same length.

The Inspector stood at the end of the room with his hands folded. He was fifty-four years old and looked it in the way that men who measure things for thirty-one years look it: not tired, exactly, but finished, the way a wall is finished.

"You filed under structural non-compliance," he said. "Affecting egress."

"I did."

"That's a real category. Most people file under monster-related grievance because it has a lower burden of proof, and then they lose, because monsters are a permitted feature of the environment." He set his clipboard on the map. "You've done this before."

"Nine years."

"Then you know that the citation has to be served face to face. In person. By hand."

"I know."

"And that I have to be the one who receives it, and that I have to be standing on the parcel described in the citation, and that the parcel has to be correctly identified by legal description rather than by common name."

"I know that too."

"Then you know why nobody wins these." He picked the clipboard up again. "The last party that tried served a citation describing the room as 'the big one with the pit.' I had to return it unopened."

"Sub-basement, west stair landing, four feet six inches at the turn, parcel number—"

"Stop." He held up one hand. "You don't have the parcel number."

"I have the parcel number."

"You've been in my dungeon for one afternoon, and you have the parcel number."

"Ovick has the parcel number," Halla said. "Ovick has been in your dungeon for six weeks, and Ovick is level four, and Ovick has been filing the same form every Friday since the second week, and you have been returning it INSUFFICIENT every time without telling him what's insufficient, which is not a denial, and which under the Access Code means the file is still open."

The Inspector was quiet for a moment. Behind him the map did what maps in this dungeon did, which was rearrange itself slightly, as if embarrassed.

"Section two," he said at last. "The return has to state a reason."

"It didn't."

"It didn't," he agreed. "That's a defect in my own procedure, and you've found it, and I'd like to note for the record that I'd rather you hadn't."

"Note it."

He wrote something on the clipboard. Halla could not see what. She had learned, in nine years, not to lean.

"Fine," he said. "I'll take the citation. Read the description."

She read it. It was thirty-one words long and she had spent eleven hours on it, cross-referencing the entry stair against the municipal standard for egress width in occupancies of fewer than fifty persons, and she read it without stopping and without adding anything, because a citation with an added word is a citation that gets returned.

The Inspector listened with his eyes closed.

"Landing width," he said, when she finished, "measured how?"

"String. From the inside face of the newel to the inside face of the opposite wall."

"String that was not issued by this office."

"String that was calibrated against the stamped rule in the front hall at nine-forty this morning, in the presence of the stack of paper, which did not object."

The Inspector opened his eyes.

"That was well done," he said. "And it doesn't matter, because I'm going to counter-cite."

"On what."

"On the party. Occupancy variance at the turn, unauthorized measurement, and—" he turned a page—"entering a map room with a wounded party member while the room's occupancy was already at three, which is a violation of the posted limit, regardless of whether the three occupants were alive."

"They were dead."

"Occupancy doesn't care."

"That's obscene."

"It's administrative," the Inspector said. "There's a difference, and the difference is the whole job."

He signed the counter-citation and held it out. Halla did not take it. Taking it was acknowledgment, and acknowledgment started a clock, and the clock was nine days.

"You can refuse to accept," he said. "Then it's served by posting, and posting means I nail it to the door of your guild hall, and your guild master sees it, and the guild master has opinions about parties that get posted."

She took it.

"Good," he said. "Now the interesting part."

---

The interesting part was that a hearing with two citations in it resolves to a single finding, and the finding is not guilt or innocence but a *balance of hazards*. The System ran this itself. It ran it the way a spreadsheet runs: without malice, and without any interest in who deserved what.

The room dimmed slightly. A panel of light appeared over the map, and on it were two columns.

LEFT: egress width, four feet six, required five feet, deficiency six inches, class B, penalty to the Core: three points of Compliance Rating, payable immediately.

RIGHT: occupancy variance at the turn, one person over, class C, penalty to the party: a fine of forty marks and one week of excluded access.

"Those don't balance," Tem said.

"They don't have to balance," the Inspector said. "They have to both be true."

The System accepted both. The panel went white, then gray, then folded itself up like a letter and was gone.

"Three points," the Inspector said. He did not sound pleased. He sounded like a man reading a meter. "That puts me at eighty-one. Below the threshold for the eastern expansion, which means the eastern expansion is now suspended pending re-inspection, which means four rooms I had scheduled for this month will not exist this month."

"Sorry," Halla said, and meant it in the way one means it about weather.

"Don't be. You did the job correctly." He picked up his clipboard, and then he stopped, and stood still for a moment in a way that Halla had not seen before, and that she would think about later.

"Section two," he said. "You wanted the file open."

"It's open."

"The file has a question in it that I can't answer, and I'd like it answered by someone who isn't me." He turned the clipboard around. "The map room has a stair. It goes down. It is not on the plan, it has no parcel number, and when I enter the legal description of the space it opens into, the System returns a string I don't recognize."

"What string?"

The Inspector held the clipboard up so she could read it.

It said: `PARCEL: NOT APPLICABLE — SUBJECT OUTSIDE JURISDICTION`.

---

They came up the eleven steps at six-forty in the evening with a counter-citation in Halla's coat and a suspended expansion behind them and a stair in their heads that went down further than the dungeon went.

Ovick was waiting at the front desk with his book under his arm. He had been waiting since four.

"Any luck?" he said.

"Three points off him," Halla said. "The eastern rooms are suspended."

Ovick looked at her for a moment, and then he opened his book and made a note, and the note took a while.

"That's the first time anyone's cost him anything," he said. "Six weeks, and that's the first time."

"It's three points."

"It's three points off a man who has never lost one." Ovick closed the book. "What did he give you?"

Halla took the counter-citation out of her coat and looked at the back of it, because the Inspector had written something there while she was reading the parcel description, in pencil, in the small square hand of a man who had filled out a great many forms.

It said: *Ask the guild archivist what was in this building before the library. Do not file a request. Requests go on the record.*

She folded it and put it away.

"Nothing," she said. "He gave us a receipt."
