Chapter 41

Gerald measured the thing in the wall again. Four inches north. Three inches up. The rate was accelerating, which meant the substrate was denser than he'd assumed, which meant the load path was shifting faster than his calculations allowed.

He pulled his tape measure back. The blade snapped against the concrete. He reset it and extended it again, pressing the hook against the exposed edge where the thing had breached the surface. The edge was gray-green, like wet plaster. It pulsed once. Gerald noted the time.

"Seventeen hours to the nearest column," he said.

"Seventeen hours and twelve minutes," 7714 confirmed.

Gerald wrote the number on his clipboard. He had three clipboards now. One for spawn logs, one for citations, and one he'd labeled EMERGENCY in black marker because the System did not provide an emergency clipboard and he had not known what else to do.

The thing in the wall pulsed again. Gerald did not look at it. He was already at the north column, measuring its circumference with a string. Forty-one inches. It should have been forty-four. The thing was stealing material from the column, converting it into itself.

Gerald sat down. He opened his clipboard to a fresh page and drew a cross-section of his dungeon. North wall. Three columns. The thing was between column one and column two, closer to two. If it reached column two, column two would fail. If column two failed, the ceiling slab would crack along its span. The slab was four thousand square feet. It would not collapse all at once. It would sag. Then it would crack. Then it would come down in sections, and each section would crush whatever was below it.

Gerald looked at his spawn list. Three mold spores. A water leak. A rat that had died on day four and whose carcass he had not been able to remove because removing a biological entity without a Form 11-B constituted an unauthorized disposal, and unauthorized disposal triggered a fine.

He could not evacuate anyone. There was no one to evacuate. There was only him, and him was not a physical body. Him was the dungeon. If the ceiling came down, him was gone.

He stood up. He walked to the east wall. He pressed his palm flat against the concrete and felt the thing's pulse through the floor. It was slow. Patient. It had all the time in the world and it knew that Gerald did not.

Gerald went to his filing cabinet. It was a real filing cabinet. He had dragged it down from the library above during his first week, before he understood that the System would not provide furniture. It held every form he had ever filed. Form 11-B. Form 7-D. Form 4-A. The Citation ledger. The Compliance log. And, in the bottom drawer, a folder labeled NOTES ON SYSTEM AUDIT PROCEDURES, which he had assembled from fragments of 7714's responses over forty chapters of asking questions that 7714 could not answer.

He opened the folder. He read it again. He was looking for a single sentence.

An audit is triggered when the System identifies an imminent life safety hazard within a registered Core's spatial boundaries.

He had read it before. He had dismissed it before. He had dismissed it because he had assumed "life safety" meant the System would not consider a Core to be a life. He was a Core. His dungeon was his body. There were no occupants. There was no one to endanger.

But that was not quite right.

Gerald pulled out his spawn list. The mold spores were alive. The water leak was not. The rat was dead. None of them qualified as "life" in the System's sense. The System had confirmed this on day twelve when Gerald asked whether his own dissolution into dungeon form constituted a life safety event and 7714 had said no, a Core is not a life, a Core is a structure.

But adventurers were lives.

Gerald went to his spawn menu. He had not opened it in six days. He scrolled past the options he could not afford and found what he was looking for. The entry point. Every dungeon had one. A threshold. A point of ingress that the System maintained as a public-facing interface, like a front door.

Gerald opened it. He changed the difficulty rating from 3 to 1. He changed the recommended party size from 4 to 1. He changed the reward tier to its lowest value. He posted a notice on the System's public dungeon board, which he had never used before because he had always found it vulgar, and the notice read:

ASHFORD MUNICIPAL LIBRARY SUB-BASEMENT DUNGEON. DIFFICULTY: 1. REWARD: 12 COPPER. NO COMBAT SPAWNS. NO TRAPS. NO PUZZLES. OPEN FOR EXPLORATION.

He closed the menu.

Nothing happened.

He waited. He checked the entry point. A small rectangle of light in the south wall, the only feature in his dungeon he had not personally constructed. It flickered. Someone was looking at his listing.

An hour later, a woman walked in. She was maybe twenty-five. She had a short sword and a leather vest and the expression of someone who had been told this was a real dungeon and was already disappointed.

Gerald watched her through his dungeon-sense. She walked to the center of the floor. She looked around. She said, "This is a basement."

Gerald could not respond. He had no mouth. He had never had a mouth.

The woman walked to the north wall. She stopped. She looked at the thing. The thing pulsed. The woman stepped back. She drew her sword.

"Okay," she said. "That's not a basement."

Gerald needed her to stay. He needed her to be present when the hazard became imminent. He needed her to be a life inside his spatial boundaries when the thing reached the column.

He could not speak to her. He could not spawn anything to keep her. He could not create a reward, because the reward tier was already at minimum and he could not afford to raise it.

The woman sheathed her sword. She turned toward the entry point.

Gerald did the only thing he could do. He cited her.

Not for trespass. Not for anything she had done. He cited her for the thing in the wall. He issued a Citation to the structure itself, naming the north wall as a non-compliant load-bearing element with an active biological intrusion, and he cited it for failure to provide adequate egress signage in the event of structural compromise.

The Citation went out. The System registered it. And then, because the Citation identified an imminent life safety hazard and there was a life present within the spatial boundaries, the System flagged it.

An audit was triggered.

Gerald felt it like a pressure change. The air in his dungeon thickened. The lights he had installed dimmed by one notch. 7714's voice came through flat and formal.

"Inspector General inbound. ETA four minutes. Please remain at your primary interface point."

Gerald walked to the center of his dungeon. He stood on the concrete he had poured. He held his clipboard.

The thing in the wall pulsed faster. It knew.

Gerald pulled his tape measure from his belt. He went to column two. He measured it. Thirty-nine inches. Two more than the last reading. The thing was accelerating.

He had four minutes.

He went to his filing cabinet. He pulled out Form 4-A, the structural deficiency report he had filed on day one, the one that had triggered his entire existence. He held it. It was his proof that he had always been compliant. That he had always been trying. That the thing in the wall was not his fault.

The air changed again. The pressure dropped. The lights went out.

When they came back on, there was someone standing in Gerald's dungeon.

It was a man. He wore a gray suit. He had a clipboard. He looked exactly like Gerald.

Not similar. Exactly. Same age. Same height. Same thinning hair combed over a receding crown. Same wire-rimmed glasses. Same expression of mild administrative exhaustion.

"Core 4471," the man said.

Gerald said nothing.

"You triggered an audit. I'm here. Show me the hazard."

Gerald pointed at the north wall. The thing pulsed. The man walked to it. He pressed his palm against the concrete, the same way Gerald had. He closed his eyes. He held his hand there for a long time.

The man opened his eyes. He looked at Gerald.

"This is not a violation," he said.

Gerald felt something cold move through his dungeon. "Excuse me."

"The entity within the north wall is a permitted structural element. It is load-bearing by design. It was placed here by a registered Core with proper authorization. Your dungeon was constructed around it. You did not discover it. You inherited it."

"I didn't inherit anything. I built this dungeon. I poured every cubic yard of concrete in this space."

"Yes. You poured concrete around a pre-existing load-bearing element. That is not a violation. That is standard practice."

Gerald's clipboard lowered. "The thing in the wall. It's moving."

"It is settling. All load-bearing elements settle."

"It's eating my columns."

"It is distributing load. That is what columns do."

The man made a note on his clipboard. He looked at Gerald with an expression Gerald recognized. It was the expression Gerald wore when someone filed a variance request that was technically valid but morally offensive.

"Your Form 22-C is not needed," the man said. "There is no conflict. You are compliant."

Gerald stared at the man. The man stared back. They were the same face.

"What are you?" Gerald said.

"I am the Inspector General."

"No. What are you?"

The man smiled. It was Gerald's smile. The one he used when he was about to say something that would make someone else's day worse.

"I am Core 0001," the man said. "The first registered Core. The one who wrote the building code you've been following for forty chapters."

Gerald looked at the thing in the wall. It was smiling. It had been smiling since the moment it arrived. It had been smiling because it knew that Gerald would try to remove it. It had been smiling because Gerald would never be able to remove it. Because Gerald had built his entire existence around the code, and the code said the thing was allowed.

"You put it in my dungeon," Gerald said.

"I put it in every dungeon," the man said. "It is the load-bearing element. It is the thing that holds the System up. You just happen to have a very literal implementation."

Gerald looked at his clipboard. Form 4-A. The structural deficiency report. The one that had made him a Core. He looked at it and understood, for the first time, that it had not been an accident. That the anomaly in his reflection had not been a glitch. That the System had been waiting for him to file it because it needed someone who would take the code seriously enough to maintain the load path.

He looked at the man. The man looked at him.

"You're going to make me fix it," Gerald said.

"I'm going to make you maintain it. You're the only one who's ever done it properly."

The thing in the wall pulsed. It was not settling. Gerald could see that. It was spreading. The man was lying. Or the man was wrong. Or the man was testing him.

Gerald opened his clipboard to a new page. He wrote: FORM 22-C. CROSS-REGULATORY CONFLICT PETITION. He wrote it by hand because the System would not issue it and he did not care. He wrote it the way he wrote everything. Neat. Legible. In triplicate.

He held it up.

"I am filing a variance," he said. "The permitted element is actively compromising the structure it is meant to support. Compliance with the permit causes a direct and unavoidable violation of structural integrity. I am citing myself for maintaining a load-bearing element that is not, in fact, load-bearing."

The man's smile did not change. But something behind his eyes did.

"That's not how a variance works," the man said.

"It is," Gerald said. "I checked. Section 4.7. A permitted condition may be reclassified as non-compliant if it demonstrably fails to perform its intended structural function. The thing in my wall is not performing its intended function. It is consuming the structure. That is a failure. I am citing it."

The man looked at the wall. He looked at the column. Thirty-eight inches. The thing was eating faster now. It had heard Gerald. It knew what he was doing.

"You cannot cite a permitted element," the man said.

"I just did."

The System registered the Citation. The air changed. The pressure dropped again. 7714's voice came through, and for the first time in forty-one chapters, 7714 sounded uncertain.

"Core 4471. Your Citation has been registered. It has been registered against... Core 0001."

Gerald looked at the man. The man looked at Gerald.

"You just cited the Inspector General," 7714 said.

Gerald looked at his clipboard. He uncapped his pen. He wrote the date. He signed his name. He stamped it.

"Service complete," he said.

The man's smile was gone. The thing in the wall was not smiling anymore. It was pulling back. Contracting. The concrete around it cracked, and from inside the crack came a sound like a structure adjusting its weight, like a building settling after a long time in the wrong position.

Gerald looked at column two. Thirty-seven inches. It should have been forty-four.

The thing in the wall was not eating faster. It was eating faster because Gerald had cited it and it was angry.

The man in the gray suit stepped toward the entry point. He paused. He looked back at Gerald.

"You have forty-seven hours to withdraw that Citation," he said. "If you do not, the Hearing will proceed. And you will be arguing structural logic against the entity that designed the System's physics."

He walked out.

Gerald stood in the center of his dungeon. The thing in the wall pulsed. The column measured thirty-six inches.

He opened his clipboard. He turned to a fresh page. He wrote: HEARING PREPARATION. Then he stopped. He crossed it out. He wrote: CODE REVIEW. Then he stopped again. He crossed that out too.

He wrote: HOW TO WIN AN ARGUMENT WITH THE PERSON WHO WROTE THE RULES.

He stared at it. He crossed it out.

He wrote: WHAT IS THE INTENDED STRUCTURAL FUNCTION.

He underlined it twice. He began to write.
