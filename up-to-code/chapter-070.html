Chapter 70: Objection to the Rat

# Chapter 70

Gerald wrote the number.

He wrote it in the same handwriting he had used for thirty-one years of inspection reports, the same careful block letters that made the letters look like they had been measured with a ruler. Each character sat inside its own imaginary grid square.

The System's personnel directory had listed the name on the seventh page of the file, under "Deputy Inspectors, Zone 4," and the number had been right there.

Inspector #8812. Deputy Auditor, General Compliance Division. Available for reassignment.

He slid the clipboard back toward the audit instrument.

The instrument picked it up. The instrument read it. The instrument paused for exactly four seconds, which Gerald had learned was how long the System took to process a name that was not already in the active queue.

Then the instrument set the clipboard down and said, in its filing-cabinet voice, "Accepted. Recusal granted. Assignment transferred to Inspector #8812, Deputy Auditor, General Compliance Division. Notification sent."

Gerald exhaled.

The audit instrument folded itself. It did not disappear, exactly. It compressed. It became a smaller object, roughly the size of a stapler, and it sat on the concrete floor of the sub-basement corridor with a faint hum that Gerald felt in his molars.

He had bought time. The assignment was no longer Core #4471. The assignment was now someone else, someone from the General Compliance Division, someone who presumably had no personal stake in Gerald's dungeon.

He turned back to his spawn population problem, and the rat was still sitting against the wall.

The rat was now a registered spawn. The rat was now part of the population count, which meant the fifty percent cap applied to it.

Gerald had three mold spores, one water leak, one registered rat.

Five entities. The cap required that no entity type could exceed fifty percent of the total spawn population.

He had one rat. That was twenty percent. That was fine.

He had three mold spores. That was sixty percent. That was not fine.

Gerald looked at the mold spores. They were small, pale, and growing in a patch along the north wall where the condensation pooled. He had been meaning to address the condensation for weeks. He had not addressed the condensation because addressing the condensation required fixing the water leak, and fixing the water leak required a plumbing permit, and the plumbing permit required a structural assessment, and the structural assessment required a Core that was not currently being audited.

He needed to reduce the mold spore count or increase the total spawn population.

He looked at the rat, and he looked at the mold spores.

He lifted his clipboard to draft a Form 11-C: Voluntary Spawn Retirement Request, and the mold spores had not been spawned by Gerald.

They had been spawned by the environment.

The System had registered them automatically upon Core Registration, which meant they were technically Gerald's responsibility but had been generated by conditions Gerald had not created.

If he could argue that the mold spores were not "spawned" but "inherited," they might not count toward the population cap.

He had not tried this argument before. He did not know if it would work. But he had spent thirty-one years arguing with property owners about whether a pre-existing condition was the new owner's problem or the previous owner's problem, and the answer was always the same: it was whoever held the permit's problem. Gerald held the permit.

He set the form aside. He would file it later.

The notification came at 4:47 in the morning. Gerald was not asleep. Gerald had not been asleep for two days.

He was sitting at his workbench, which was actually a folding table he had dragged in from the library's storage closet, measuring the clearance distance between his handrail installation and the north wall. The handrail was at 34 inches.

The code required 34 inches.

He had measured it eleven times because the System's compliance scanner used a slightly different reference point than his tape measure, and he was trying to figure out which measurement the scanner would use.

The notification appeared in his peripheral vision, and he did not look at it immediately. He finished his measurement.

He wrote the number down.

Then he looked.

[RECIPROCAL AUDIT PROTOCOL ACTIVATED] [Condition: Proposed alternative auditor is a registered Core] [Effect: The audited Core gains the right to audit the proposed auditor within the same window] [Note: Both audits must be completed before the window closes]

Gerald read it twice, and he read it a third time.

The proposed auditor was a Core. Inspector #8812 was not just an auditor.

Inspector #8812 was a dungeon core who happened to also hold an auditor designation.

The System allowed this, and the System had always allowed this. It was in the personnel directory because the System's General Compliance Division was staffed entirely by Cores who had passed the auditor qualification exam.

Every auditor was a Core. Every Core auditor had a dungeon.

This meant Gerald now had the right to audit Inspector #8812.

It also meant that Inspector #8812's audit of Gerald was now subject to Gerald's counter-audit. If Gerald could find violations in Inspector #8812's dungeon, those violations could be used to reduce or void the findings of Inspector #8812's audit of Gerald.

It was a balance. A trade. Gerald could cite the auditor, and the auditor could cite Gerald, and the System would weigh both sets of findings and produce a combined result.

Gerald set down his tape measure, and he needed to know what Inspector #8812's dungeon looked like. He needed to know its layout, its spawn list, its compliance history. He needed this information within seventy-two hours, because the audit window was still running, and if Gerald could not complete his counter-audit before the window closed, the auditor's findings would stand unopposed.

He opened the personnel file again. He turned to the entry for Inspector #8812.

The entry gave a name: Dale Voss. It gave a Core number: #2201. It gave a location: Zone 7, Sector 14, beneath the Ashford Industrial Park.

Gerald stared at the Core number, and he knew that number.

He had cited Core #2201 forty-three chapters ago, and had cited it for inadequate egress width in its primary corridor.

The citation had been appealed.

The appeal had been heard. Gerald had won that hearing, which had been one of the first times the System had allowed him to prove a violation on the record, and the resulting debuff had killed two of Core #2201's elite spawns.

Core #2201 had appealed again, and the second appeal had been heard by a different panel. Gerald had lost that one.

The debuff had been reversed.

Core #2201 had received a bonus for successful appeal.

The bonus was listed in the personnel file. Gerald read it.

[BONUS: Priority Audit Authority. The holder may audit any Core that has previously issued a Citation against them. Findings are final and not subject to appeal.]

Gerald sat still, and the bonus meant that if Core #2201 audited Gerald, Gerald could not appeal the findings. The findings would be final.

There would be no hearing.

There would be no counter-argument.

Whatever Core #2201 found, it would stand.

But the Reciprocal Audit Protocol meant Gerald could audit Core #2201 in return; Gerald's findings would be subject to appeal, just like any normal audit. Core #2201 could appeal Gerald's findings. But Gerald's findings would at least exist in the record, and the System would weigh them against Core #2201's findings.

Unless Core #2201's findings were final and Gerald's were not.

Gerald did the math. If Core #2201's findings were final and Gerald's were appealable, then the System would have to reconcile a final finding against a provisional one. The provisional one would carry less weight. Gerald would be at a disadvantage.

He needed his findings to be final too, and he looked at the notification again. He looked at the bonus description. "Findings are final and not subject to appeal." The bonus applied to audits conducted by the holder against a Core that had previously cited them.

Gerald had cited Core #2201. The bonus applied.

But Gerald had not yet audited Core #2201.

The Reciprocal Audit Protocol had activated, but Gerald had not begun his audit. Should he start, the findings would be standard. Appealable.

Less weighted.

Unless he could find a way to make his findings final.

He looked at the personnel file again. He looked at the bonus. He looked at the condition: "any Core that has previously issued a Citation against them."

The condition was one-directional. It applied to Core #2201 because Gerald had cited Core #2201, and did not apply to Gerald because Core #2201 had not cited Gerald.

Core #2201 had not cited Gerald.

But the Reciprocal Audit Protocol was an audit, not a citation, and it was the System's audit, assigned to Core #2201. It was not Core #2201's citation. It was the System's citation, delivered through Core #2201.

Gerald picked up his pen.

He wrote on a fresh page: "Form 11-D. Citation of Auditor for Conflict of Interest. Target: Inspector #8812 / Core #2201. Basis: Reciprocal Audit Protocol activation constitutes prior citation against the auditor, triggering Priority Audit Authority condition in reverse."

He stared at the form, and he was arguing that the Reciprocal Audit Protocol itself constituted a citation against Core #2201. If that argument held, then Core #2201 had been "previously cited" by Gerald, and the Priority Audit Authority bonus would apply to Gerald as well. Gerald's findings would become final.

It was a stretch. It was a large stretch.

The Protocol was not a citation.

It was an audit right. But the System's language was precise, and Gerald had spent thirty-one years reading precise language, and he had found, more than once, that precise language could be bent in directions its authors had not intended.

He filed the form.

The System accepted it, and the notification came back in three seconds.

[FORM 11-D DENIED. Reciprocal Audit Protocol does not constitute a Citation under Section 4.2(b) of the Compliance Code. Priority Audit Authority condition not met for Gerald Hutchins.]

Gerald read it. He read it again.

Section 4.2(b), and he knew Section 4.2(b).

It defined Citation as "a formal notice of violation issued by a Core with standing authority over the target." The Protocol was not a notice of violation. It was a right.

He had known that. He had hoped the System would not notice that he knew that.

The System had noticed, and he had sixty-eight hours now. He had no way to make his findings final. He had to find enough violations in Core #2201's dungeon to outweigh a final audit report. He had to find so many violations that even a final finding against him would be reduced to a manageable level.

He needed to get into Core #2201's dungeon. He needed to see it. He needed to measure it. He needed to walk its corridors and check its handrails and count its exits and verify its ventilation.

And Core #2201 knew he was coming, and the second notification came at 5:12 in the morning.

[AUDIT SCHEDULED: Inspector #8812 / Core #2201 will arrive at Gerald Hutchins' dungeon in 14 hours. Early arrival authorized under Priority Audit Authority.]

Fourteen hours. Not seventy-two. Fourteen.

Gerald looked at his dungeon. He looked at his handrails, which were compliant. He looked at his egress signage, which was compliant. He looked at his fire suppression, which was not yet installed because he could not get the plumbing permit.

He looked at the water leak. He looked at the mold spores. He looked at the rat, which had moved to the south wall and was grooming itself with the complete indifference of a creature that had no idea it was part of a compliance problem.

Fourteen hours, and he picked up his clipboard. He opened a new page. He started writing.

The third notification came at 5:14.

[ADDITIONAL NOTICE: Inspector #8812 / Core #2201 has filed a Form 11-D. Citation of Gerald Hutchins for Prior Citation Conflict. Status: Pending.]

Core #2201 was trying the same argument. The same stretch. The same Section 4.2(b) problem. But Core #2201 had thirty-one years of municipal knowledge that Gerald did not have, and Gerald had thirty-one years of municipal knowledge that Core #2201 did not have, and the question was whether one of them had found a loophole the other had missed.

Gerald read the notice. He looked at the rat.

The rat looked at him; he took his pen and wrote a response.

The response was an objection to the rat.
