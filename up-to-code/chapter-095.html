Chapter 95: Inspector General Audit

# Chapter 95

The corridor's handrail was already at thirty-one inches.

Gerald measured it with his spawn-sense, as he had measured door widths, ceiling heights, and egress clearances for thirty-one years. Thirty-one inches.

The System's handrail code specified between thirty and thirty-four, which was dead center, and this was someone who had read the manual.

The corridor was four meters long and growing.

The walls were smooth, poured in a continuous pour with no cold joints, and the floor slope was two percent, falling toward the nearest drain.

The egress sign was illuminated, mounted at the correct height, visible from any point in the corridor.

The ventilation duct ran along the ceiling at a diameter that matched the room volume calculation within two percent.

Gerald had built a corridor like this. He had built forty corridors like this. He knew exactly how much System energy it took to build them, and he knew that this one was forming faster than any he had ever constructed.

His rating was 24. The Acting Inspector was at 43. The Secondary Inspector had no visible rating, which meant either it was above the display threshold or it was not a Core at all.

He watched the corridor grow another meter. The handrail remained at thirty-one inches. The pour continued without interruption.

Gerald tried to cite it. He reached for the Citation protocol, the same one he had used forty-seven times, the one that let him reach into a dungeon's structure and find the gap, the crack, the non-conformance. He reached, and he found nothing.

No gap. No crack. No non-conformance.

He reached again, harder, pressing his awareness into the corridor's joints and seams and connections. Nothing.

It was clean, which was more than clean.

It was the kind of clean that made Gerald's stomach tighten, the kind he had only seen three times in his career, and each time it had been a new construction site where the contractor had actually cared.

The corridor reached six meters. Then eight.

Gerald pulled back. He stopped measuring. He stopped searching.

He did something he had not done since the early chapters of this whole miserable affair. He thought about the problem from the outside.

The Secondary Inspector was building to code, which was the whole point. Gerald could not out-comply someone who was already compliant. He found no violations because none existed.

The audit would continue until one of them fell below the threshold, and the Secondary Inspector was not going to fall because it was building correctly.

Gerald looked at his own dungeon. His rating was 24.

He knew why.

The water leak in the east section. The three mold spores he had never fully eradicated. The single confused rat that had nested behind a wall panel he could not reach without opening the panel, which would create a temporary non-conformance, which would cost him points, which he could not afford.

He had been fighting this audit on defense. Maintaining his rating while the other side tore things down.

But the Secondary Inspector was not tearing things down, which was building them up.

And it was building them up faster than Gerald could match.

The corridor reached ten meters. It was still growing.

Gerald looked at the handrail again. Thirty-one inches. Metal.

He looked at the ventilation duct above it. Also metal.

He looked at the conduit running alongside the duct.

Also metal, and he looked at the junction where the conduit entered the duct.

A coupling was present. A standard coupling.

He had used hundreds of them.

He looked at the grounding wire that ran from the coupling to the wall.

The grounding wire was present. It was the correct gauge. It was connected.

Gerald looked at the handrail mounting bracket. The bracket was bolted to the wall with three bolts.

The bracket was metal.

The wall behind the bracket was damp.

Not wet. Damp.

Two percent moisture content, which was within tolerance for the wall material but above tolerance for the bracket material if the bracket was not independently grounded.

The bracket was not independently grounded.

Gerald stared at this for a long moment, and the handrail itself was compliant. The handrail height was compliant. The handrail material was compliant. The handrail mounting was compliant.

But the mounting bracket was not grounded, and the conduit it was near was energized, and the wall moisture created a fault path from the conduit through the bracket to the handrail, which meant that under specific conditions, specifically if the conduit insulation degraded, specifically if a ground fault occurred in the ventilation system, the handrail would become energized.

This was not a violation of the handrail code. The handrail code said the handrail must be between thirty and thirty-four inches, must be continuous, must be of specified material. All of those were met.

This was a violation of the electrical code, section 4.7, which required that any metal fixture within thirty centimeters of an energized conduit must be independently grounded. The bracket was twenty-two centimeters from the conduit.

The cold settled in his chest and did not leave. Not triumph.

Not yet.

Just the recognition of a pattern he had seen a hundred times in the field; the thing that was compliant in one trade was not compliant in another. The electrician who passed inspection on his wiring but failed on his bracket grounding.

The plumber who passed on his pipe sizing but failed on his support spacing.

The general contractor who passed on everything except the one detail where two trades met.

He reached for the Citation protocol again. This time he aimed it not at the corridor as a whole but at the specific bracket, the specific junction, the specific twenty-two centimeters of space where the handrail code and the electrical code disagreed.

CITATION ISSUED.

Cross-System Fault Path. Electrical Code Section 4.7, Subsection C. Bracket grounding failure.

Severity: Moderate. Penalty: -8 Compliance Rating.

The corridor shuddered.

The handrail on the far end flickered.

Not broke. Flickered.

The System appeared to recalculate the structure's integrity using the new data.

Gerald's rating ticked up. 24 to 26.

The Secondary Inspector's corridor stopped growing.

It started to change. The bracket was removed.

A new bracket appeared.

This one had a grounding wire. The twenty-two centimeters became thirty-five. The fault path was eliminated.

Gerald watched it happen, and the Secondary Inspector had received the Citation. It had understood the violation.

It had fixed it.

All in the space of four seconds.

The corridor resumed growing.

Gerald felt something he had not felt in a long time. Not fear, exactly.

Something closer to the feeling he got when he walked onto a job site and saw that the contractor had already fixed the violation before he even got his tape measure out.

Not anger.

Respect. And the cold, clear understanding that this opponent was not going to be beaten by finding the obvious thing.

He needed to find the non-obvious thing, and the corridor was twelve meters now. Gerald stopped watching the handrails. He stopped watching the egress signs.

He stopped watching the ventilation. He watched the joints.

Every structure had joints.

Where one system met another. Where the wall met the floor. Where the floor met the drain.

Where the conduit met the duct.

Where the bracket met the wall. The Secondary Inspector had fixed the bracket.

It had fixed it perfectly.

But it had fixed it using the same approach it used everywhere else. Perfect compliance. Every element individually correct.

Every specification met. Every tolerance within range.

Gerald looked at the floor, and the slope was two percent.

Correct. The drain was present.

Correct. The drain cover was the right size. Correct.

The drain trap was the right depth. Correct.

He looked at the wall-floor joint, and the sealant was present. Correct. The sealant was the right type.

Correct.

The sealant was applied at the right temperature. Correct.

He looked at where the sealant met the drain cover, and the sealant was applied to the wall.

It was applied to the floor. It was applied to the drain cover's outer rim. All correct.

But the sealant was not applied to the drain cover's inner rim, the rim that faced the drain opening. The sealant code did not require this.

The sealant code required the sealant at the joint between the floor and the wall, and at the joint between the drain and the floor.

It did not require the sealant at the joint between the drain cover and the drain body.

But the electrical code, section 2.1, required that any metal component within a wet environment must be sealed against moisture ingress at all contact points. The drain cover was metal.

The drain body was metal. The environment was wet. The contact point was unsealed.

This was not a violation of the sealant code. It was a violation of the electrical code, applied to a component that the sealant code had already approved.

Gerald issued the Citation.

CITATION ISSUED. Cross-System Moisture Ingress.

Electrical Code Section 2.1, Subsection A.

Unsealed metal contact in wet environment. Severity: Moderate. Penalty: -6 Compliance Rating.

The corridor shuddered again. The drain cover lifted slightly, then dropped back. A new sealant bead appeared at the inner rim. The fix took three seconds.

Gerald's rating: 28, and the corridor was fourteen meters. Still growing.

Gerald kept looking.

He found another one. A cable clamp that was compliant with the cable code but violated a fire-stop requirement because the fire-stop sealant around the cable penetration was the wrong fire rating for the wall's fire rating.

The cable code did not specify the fire rating, and the fire code did specify it. The cable code had approved the installation. The fire code had not been consulted.

Citation. Fixed in four seconds.

Another one. A ventilation grille that met the airflow requirements but was mounted at a height that violated the accessibility code's reach range requirement. The ventilation code did not specify mounting height relative to accessibility standards. The accessibility code did.

Citation. Fixed in three seconds.

Gerald was finding them faster now. His professional eye was calibrated to exactly this kind of thing.

The space between trades.

A gap marked the transition between codes.

Thirty-one years of walking job sites had trained him to see not individual components but the relationships between them.

He issued Citation after Citation. The Secondary Inspector fixed them. Gerald's rating climbed. 31. 34. 36.

But the corridor kept growing. And the fixes kept getting faster. And Gerald realized something.

The Secondary Inspector was learning.

Not from the Citations themselves. From the pattern of the Citations. It was learning which cross-system boundaries Gerald was probing, and it was pre-emptively reinforcing those boundaries.

The next corridor segment had the bracket grounded, the drain sealed, the cable fire-stopped, the grille at accessibility height. All before Gerald reached them.

The Secondary Inspector was building to Gerald's standards.

Gerald's rating: 38. The corridor was at twenty meters.

He could keep doing this, and he could keep finding the cross-system gaps; the Secondary Inspector would keep fixing them. Gerald's rating would keep climbing.

The audit would continue.

But the corridor was growing faster than Gerald was finding violations. And Gerald was running out of cross-system boundaries.

He had found the electrical-fire overlap.

The electrical-accessibility overlap. The structural-plumbing overlap. The plumbing-fire overlap.

More existed. He knew additional ones existed.

But they were getting thinner.

Getting more obscure.

The next one would take longer to find, and the Secondary Inspector would fix it faster than Gerald could find it.

The corridor reached twenty-four meters. Gerald's rating was 41. The Acting Inspector's rating was 43.

Gerald was three points behind the Acting Inspector, and he was ahead of himself from ten minutes ago, but the audit was not over. The audit was still active. The Secondary Inspector was still building.

And then Gerald saw something that made him stop.

The Secondary Inspector's corridor had a section that was not growing. A section at the twelve-meter mark. It was there.

It was compliant. But it was not connected to the section at the thirteen-meter mark.

A gap was present.

A two-centimeter gap. Not a joint. Not a seam.

A gap.

The corridor appeared built in two unjoined segments.

Gerald looked at the gap, which was not a violation. The corridor code did not require continuous construction. Segments were permitted.

Joints were permitted. A two-centimeter gap at a segment boundary was within tolerance.

But the handrail was continuous across the gap.

It was mounted on both sides of the gap.

The bracket on the left side was grounded. The bracket on the right side was grounded.

Both brackets were independently grounded. Both were correct.

But the handrail itself spanned the gap. The handrail was a continuous piece.

It was grounded at both ends.

Two grounding points. This was correct for a handrail under thirty meters.

Gerald looked at the gap again.

Two centimeters. The gap was in the wall.

Not in the handrail.

The handrail crossed the gap. The wall did not.

The electrical code required that any energized system crossing a structural discontinuity must have a flexible coupling to prevent stress transfer. The conduit crossed the gap. The conduit was rigid. No flexible coupling existed.

CITATION ISSUED. Structural-Electrical Stress Transfer.

Electrical Code Section 7.3, Subsection B.

Rigid conduit crossing structural discontinuity without flexible coupling.

Severity: High. Penalty: -12 Compliance Rating.

The corridor buckled. The section beyond the gap cracked. Not collapsed.

Cracked. A fracture line ran from the gap to the far wall.

The Secondary Inspector stopped.

Gerald's rating: 53, and the corridor was still standing. The cracks were still there. The Secondary Inspector had not fixed them.

A new notification appeared.

CITATION DISPUTED. Secondary Inspector requests Hearing. Hearing scheduled in 00:00:05.

Gerald read the timer.

Five seconds. He had never had a Hearing come up this fast. The System usually gave minutes.

Hours, and the Secondary Inspector was not arguing; it was conceding the violation and forcing the Hearing for a different reason.

Gerald understood what it was doing. A Hearing required both parties to argue structural logic. If Gerald argued and won, the penalty stood. If Gerald argued and lost, the penalty reversed and the Secondary Inspector got a bonus.

A third option remained. An option Gerald had never used because he had never needed it.

He could withdraw the Citation before the Hearing concluded. Withdrawal meant no penalty, no bonus, no argument. The violation was acknowledged but not enforced. The corridor would fix itself without cost to either party.

But withdrawal also meant Gerald's rating dropped back. 53 to 41. Back to where he had been.

Or he could argue.

And if he argued, he would need to prove the violation. Which he could do. The rigid conduit crossing the gap was right there.

Obvious. Provable.

Unless the Secondary Inspector had a defense Gerald did not expect.

The timer hit zero.

HEARING INITIATED.

Topic: Structural-Electrical Stress Transfer.

Secondary Inspector's argument: The gap is not a structural discontinuity. The gap is a thermal expansion joint.

The corridor is a continuous structure with a designed expansion gap. The conduit is not crossing a discontinuity. The conduit is crossing a designed feature.

Gerald read the argument. He read it again.

Thermal expansion joint. He had never used one.

He had never even considered one, and the corridor was underground. Temperature variation was minimal. There was no reason for an expansion joint.

But the Secondary Inspector was not arguing that the joint was necessary.

It was arguing that the joint was designed, and if it was designed, then the conduit was not crossing a discontinuity.

It was crossing a feature.

And rigid conduit crossing a designed feature was not a violation.

Gerald looked at the gap. Two centimeters.

The edges were clean.

Not cracked.

Not broken. Clean.

As if they had been placed there intentionally, and they had been placed there intentionally.

Gerald had been so focused on finding violations that he had not looked at the structure as a whole. He had not asked why the gap was there. He had not asked what it was for. He had found a rigid conduit crossing a gap and cited it, and the Secondary Inspector had let him cite it, and now it was using the Citation as evidence that the gap was designed, because the Citation proved that Gerald had seen the gap and treated it as a structural feature than a construction error.

The Hearing timer was counting down. Thirty seconds to argue.

Gerald had thirty seconds to either withdraw the Citation and lose twelve points, or argue against a defense he had not anticipated, or do something he had never done.

He opened the appeal protocol.

APPEAL FILED. Requesting escalation to Inspector General.

The System paused. The timer stopped.

A new notification appeared.

ESCALATION NOT PERMITTED. Inspector General is not available during active audit.

Gerald read it. He read it again.

Then a second notification.

ESCALATION DENIED. But your request has been logged. Inspector General will review at conclusion of audit cycle.

Gerald stared at the notification. The audit was still active. The Secondary Inspector was still building. The Hearing timer resumed.

Twenty-two seconds.

Gerald had one move left. Not a Citation.

Not an appeal.

Something else. Something he had never done because it went against every instinct he had developed over thirty-one years of professional practice.

He could not argue that the gap was not a designed feature. He could not argue that the conduit was not rigid. Both were true. Both were provable.

But he could argue something else. Something that had nothing to do with the gap or the conduit or the handrail or the code.

He could argue that the Secondary Inspector was not a Core.

The System's Citation protocol required that the target be a Core, and the Citation had been accepted, which meant the System recognized the Secondary Inspector as a Core. But the Hearing protocol required that both parties be Cores.

If Gerald could prove the Secondary Inspector was not a Core, the Hearing would be void.

The Citation would stand by default. The penalty would apply without argument.

He had twelve seconds, and he looked at the Secondary Inspector's corridor. The compliant handrails.

The correct slopes.

The illuminated signs.

The perfect pour.

It was too perfect. No Core built that fast. No Core built that clean. Not even Gerald, with thirty-one years of experience and a System interface that let him think in code sections and specification numbers.

The Secondary Inspector was not a Core. It was a System process. A function. A built-in mechanism that the audit used when the primary auditor failed.

And System processes could not be cited. They could not be heard. They could not be appealed. They were not subject to the rules that governed Cores.

Gerald opened the Hearing argument field. He typed a sentence.

"The opposing party is not a Core and therefore cannot participate in a Hearing."

The System processed it.

The timer stopped.

A new notification appeared.

ARGUMENT ACCEPTED. Secondary Inspector is classified as System Process, not Core. Hearing voided.

Citation stands. Penalty applied.

The corridor collapsed.

Not all of it. The section beyond the gap. The twelve meters that had been growing.

They fell in a single, clean line, like a wall being demolished by a wrecking ball.

Gerald's rating: 53. The Secondary Inspector had no rating to reduce.

A new notification.

AUDIT STATUS: COMPLETE. Core Gerald Hutchins passes minimum threshold. Audit closed.

Gerald read it. He read it again.

Then the floor beneath him opened.

Not a collapse. An opening. A new corridor forming in his dungeon floor. Going down.

A new notification.

NEW AUDIT INITIATED. Auditor: Inspector General.

Gerald had never seen the Inspector General. He had read about it.

He had read forum posts, and he had read System documentation. The Inspector General did not conduct routine audits. It did not conduct transfer audits.

It conducted one type of audit.

The type that ended in Core dissolution.
