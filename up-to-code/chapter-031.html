Chapter 31: The Reverse Audit

# Chapter 31

The number was dropping.

Gerald watched his Compliance Rating tick downward in the corner of his interface. 87. 84. 81. Each decrement arrived with a soft chime that the System had designed, he suspected, to be soothing. It was not soothing. It was the sound of a man's work being unmade in real time.

He had filed Form 9-Z eleven minutes ago. The response had been immediate.

Not an acknowledgment.

Not a receipt. A protocol.

CHAIN OF CUSTODY INITIATED. ALL CORES WITHIN 200M RADIUS MUST SUBMIT STRUCTURAL LOGS WITHIN 48 HOURS. NON-COMPLIANCE = FORCED DISSOLUTION.

And beneath that, in smaller text that Gerald had read three times:

BASELINE RECALCULATION: PENDING. IF SYSTEM INTEGRITY IS CONFIRMED COMPROMISED, ALL COMPLIANCE RATINGS IN AFFECTED RADIUS REVERT TO UNVERIFIED STATUS.

Unverified status.

Gerald knew what that meant. He had seen it happen to buildings after a fraudulent inspection scandal in 2009.

The whole structure went back to zero.

Every permit, every inspection stamp, every compliance sign that had hung in a lobby for decades. Gone. Because the authority that granted them was in question.

That was what was happening to him. His passive resistances.

His fire rating from the sprinkler system.

His structural reinforcement from the load-bearing wall he had spent nine chapters reinforcing.

All of it was being treated as provisional because he had accused the System of being compromised, and until that accusation was resolved, nothing he had built could be trusted.

81. 78, and he needed to prove the System was compromised. Or he needed to prove it wasn't. Regardless, he had to submit logs that avoided triggering the baseline reversion.

He opened his structural logs. Forty-three pages of measurements, annotations, and compliance notes. The work of thirty-one chapters.

Every corridor width, every handrail height, every ventilation duct diameter. He had measured them himself.

With a tape measure.

With a level. With a clipboard.

The logs were clean. That was the problem. Clean logs proved nothing about the System. They only proved that Gerald had done his job well, which was irrelevant if the System that graded his job was suspect.

He needed to do something else.

Gerald reopened the Chain of Custody protocol, and he read it as he did any regulatory document, hunting for the exception clause. An exception clause always existed.

Municipal code had one for every rule.

Fire code had three for every rule. The System's code was no different.

There. Buried in subsection 4(c):

LOGS SUBMITTED IN GOOD FAITH WITH KNOWN ANNOTATIONS ARE EXEMPT FROM BASELINE RECALCULATION PENDING FINAL DETERMINATION.

Good faith. Known annotations.

Gerald read it again, and the language was specific.

Not "logs submitted accurately." Logs submitted in good faith with known annotations. That meant he could flag something in his own logs, mark it as an annotation, and the System would hold his rating stable while it investigated.

But it also meant he had to flag something real. A known annotation was not a vague concern. It was a specific, documented structural finding that the submitting Core acknowledged as relevant to the investigation.

Gerald closed his logs. He opened a new document. He started typing.

NON-CONFORMING INSTALLATION NOTED: CONDUIT NETWORK, UNDERFLOW FOOTPRINT, CHANNELS 1 THROUGH 11. MATERIAL CLASSIFICATION: BIOLOGICAL. ROUTING: UNREGISTERED. LEGACY STATUS: UNKNOWN.

He paused. Legacy status. That was the key.

If the conduits were legacy installations, they were not evidence of System compromise, and they were evidence of grandfathered non-compliance, which was a different problem entirely.

A problem Gerald could cite for.

But if they were not legacy, if they had been installed after the current code version went into effect, then the System had allowed an unregistered biological transport network to exist in plain sight. That was compromise. That was what Form 9-Z was for.

He did not know which it was. He needed to find out.

Gerald pulled up the System's code version history. He had never looked at it before. The version numbers were abstract.

Build 1.0 through Build 4.7.

Each build had a changelog. Gerald scrolled through them, as he had through 1990s municipal code amendments, seeking the language defining grandfathered status.

Build 3.2. Subsection 14.1. New requirement: all biological transport within dungeon footprints must be registered under Form 7-C (Biological Conduit Declaration). Effective immediately upon build deployment.

Gerald checked the date. Build 3.2 had deployed fourteen months ago.

He checked UNDERFLOW's registration date. The core had been active for twenty-two months.

The conduits were installed before Build 3.2. They were legacy. They were grandfathered. They were not evidence of System compromise.

Gerald sat back. His hands were steady. His rating was still dropping. 74. 71.

He had been wrong. Form 9-Z was a false report. The System was not compromised. The conduits were legal under the code that existed when they were installed.

But they were not legal under the current code. Build 3.2 required registration. UNDERFLOW had never filed Form 7-C. The conduits were non-conforming legacy installations that should have been retroactively registered when the new code took effect.

That was a violation. Not a System compromise. A code violation.

Gerald deleted Form 9-Z. He could not unfile it, but he could amend it. He opened the amendment interface. He typed:

AMENDMENT TO FORM 9-Z: WITHDRAW ACCUSATION OF SYSTEM COMPROMISE. RECLASSIFY AS: NON-CONFORMING LEGACY INSTALLATION, REF. BUILD 3.2 SUBSECTION 14.1. REQUEST TRANSFER TO STANDARD CITATION PROTOCOL.

He submitted the amendment, and the response came in four seconds.

AMENDMENT ACCEPTED.

FORM 9-Z CLOSED. CHAIN OF CUSTODY PROTOCOL REMAINS ACTIVE. BASELINE RECALCULATION SUSPENDED.

CORE RATING: HELD AT CURRENT VALUE PENDING LOG REVIEW.

Gerald looked at his rating. 69. It had stopped dropping. It was not going back up. It was frozen at 69, which was lower than it had been an hour ago, but stable.

He had twenty-two points to claw back in forty-seven hours.

He started writing his logs.

Not the forty-three pages of clean measurements.

A new set. Annotated.

He went through every corridor in his dungeon and noted every junction where a pipe or conduit from an adjacent footprint could theoretically intersect his space. He noted the wall thicknesses. He noted the vibration readings from the water mains.

He noted the temperature differentials that suggested hidden flow behind his finished surfaces, which was what he had done for thirty-one years.

Not heroics. Not combat. Documentation.

The ability to look at a wall and know what was behind it.

The ability to listen to a pipe and know whether it was compliant. The ability to write a finding so precisely worded that no one could argue with it.

He was good at this. He had always been good at this. The System had not changed that. It had only changed the scale.

At hour fourteen, a notification appeared that he had not expected.

CHAIN OF CUSTODY LOG SUBMITTED: CORE DESIGNATION [NULL]. CLASSIFICATION: UNDECLARED SUBSTRATE. STATUS: FORCED REGISTRATION.

Gerald read it twice. An unregistered core. Something in the 200-meter radius that the System had not previously logged; the Chain of Custody protocol had found it by forcing a census.

He checked the radius map.

The 200-meter circle from his Core footprint covered the library sub-basement, the parking garage, the adjacent municipal building, and the stretch of road between them. He knew every dungeon core in that zone. He had cited four of them.

He had filed joint compliance reports with two others.

There was no fifth. He checked the coordinates the System logged for the [NULL] core. They were beneath the parking garage. Specifically, beneath the section of parking garage that had been condemned after the 2019 flooding and was currently under reconstruction.

Gerald had inspected that site. He had written the condemnation report. He had specified the remediation requirements. He had signed off on the reconstruction plans three months ago.

There had been nothing there.

No dungeon footprint.

No core signature. No biological entity.

Just a flooded concrete slab and a contractor's invoice for new drainage, and the System had found something that was not there when Gerald looked.

He pulled up the [NULL] core's submitted logs. They were sparse.

Three pages.

The core had registered under duress, and its logs reflected that. No compliance measures. No ventilation.

No egress.

A Compliance Rating of 3 out of 100, which was lower than Gerald's had been at his worst.

But the logs contained something else. A annotation in the structural section:

SUBSTRATE TYPE: NON-DUNGEON. CLASSIFICATION: UNKNOWN. SYSTEM CANNOT ASSIGN CATEGORY.

Gerald stared at that line.

The System could not assign a category. That meant it was not a dungeon core. It was not a registered entity of any kind.

It existed in the System's space but defied the System's taxonomy.

He went back to the radius map. The coordinates were precise. They placed the [NULL] core exactly beneath the parking garage's east wall. The same wall where Gerald had noted, in his 2019 inspection report, a "minor subsidence anomaly, non-structural, recommend monitoring."

He had written that sentence, and he had recommended monitoring.

He had not investigated further because the anomaly was non-structural. It did not threaten the building's integrity.

It did not violate any code.

It was not his job.

Gerald opened his 2019 report. He read his own handwriting.

He remembered the day, and he remembered thinking it was a drainage issue; remembered moving on to the next item on his checklist.

A notification appeared.

CHAIN OF CUSTODY LOG SUBMITTED: CORE DESIGNATION: UNDERFLOW. STATUS: COMPLIANT SUBMISSION.

UNDERFLOW had submitted its logs.

Gerald pulled them up. Forty pages.

Dense.

Detailed. The conduits were listed. All eleven.

Registered as legacy installations under the grandfathering clause. UNDERFLOW had filed its retroactive Form 7-C.

It had complied.

Gerald sat with that for a moment.

UNDERFLOW had complied, which meant Gerald's citation against UNDERFLOW for the conduits would be dismissed, and the conduits were now registered.

Legal. Grandfathered.

But UNDERFLOW's logs contained something else. The biological transport section contained a destination field under the Conduit 7 entry. The destination was listed as:

SUBSTRATE REF: [NULL]

UNDERFLOW's conduits were feeding something. Not a dungeon. Not a core. The [NULL] entity beneath the parking garage.

Gerald's interface chimed.

CHAIN OF CUSTODY PROTOCOL: ALL LOGS RECEIVED. CROSS-REFERENCE COMPLETE.

ANOMALY FLAGGED: UNDECLARED SUBSTRATE RECEIVING BIOLOGICAL INPUT FROM REGISTERED CORE.

CLASSIFICATION: UNKNOWN. INSPECTOR GENERAL NOTIFICATION: IMMEDIATE. AUDIT RESCHEDULED: 48 HOURS.

Forty-eight hours, and the audit was now in forty-eight hours. Not seven days.

Not forty-one days.

Two days.

And Gerald had a Compliance Rating of 69.

He needed to find the [NULL] entity. He needed to understand what it was.

He needed to determine whether it was a code violation or something the System had no framework for.

Because if the System had no framework, the Inspector General would not cite it.

The Inspector General would not audit it. The Inspector General would do something else.

Something Gerald had never seen the System do, and he stood. He walked toward the parking garage. He did not run.

Gerald did not run. He walked at the pace of a man who had walked to inspection sites for thirty-one years and who intended to walk to this one too.

He reached the east wall of the parking garage at hour nineteen; the reconstruction crew had gone home for the night. The concrete slab was exposed. Gerald pressed his Core interface against it.

Something moved below. Not water. Not biological material. Something that responded to the interface's frequency by pressing back.

Gerald pulled his hand away. His rating ticked down. 68.

The thing below the parking garage knew he was there.

It had filed the paperwork already.
