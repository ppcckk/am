Chapter 79: Citing the Void

# Chapter 79

The pen was a standard-issue ballpoint. Gerald had found it in the desk drawer of his old office, the one that had dissolved along with everything else, and it had somehow persisted in his inventory with the quiet stubbornness of a thing that had no business existing. He used it to fill in the dimension fields on Form 7-A because the System's auto-fill had offered a suggestion that was wrong by two inches, and Gerald Hutchins did not accept suggestions that were wrong by two inches.

Three minutes and twelve seconds, and he wrote: Footprint area, 2,100 square feet. Depth below claimant's lowest floor plane: 14 inches at nearest point, 6 feet 3 inches at deepest point.

Access: none.

Egress: none. Ventilation: none.

He paused at the ventilation field. The correct entry was "none." The correct entry was also an admission that he was claiming a space that violated at least four separate air quality codes. He wrote "none" because it was true, and because the form would be rejected if he lied, and because the System's rejection penalties were worse than its compliance penalties.

Two minutes and forty seconds, and he turned to Section 4: Assumption of Liability. The language was dense. Municipal forms were always dense, but this one had a density that felt intentional, like someone had taken the municipal code and added three paragraphs of legalese that served no purpose except to make the reader's eyes slide off the page.

Gerald did not let his eyes slide off the page.

He read it. All of it.

"Claimant assumes all latent liabilities associated with the claimed footprint, including but not limited to: structural defects, environmental hazards, active code violations, and enforcement actions pending at time of dormancy."

Latent liabilities. Active code violations. Enforcement actions pending.

He looked at the wall. The seam was almost gone. A thin line of light, like a hairline fracture in glass, barely visible now.

Two minutes and ten seconds.

Gerald thought about Core 7741's message.

The 40% drag. A perfect score dragged down by a neighbor's corpse.

He thought about what that meant for the audit, and he thought about what it meant for him, if Core 7741 got stronger instead of weaker.

He thought about the liability clause. He thought about what "active code violations" might mean in practice. He thought about what "enforcement actions pending" might mean.

He thought about the fact that he had been a building inspector for thirty-one years, and in thirty-one years he had never once inherited a dead man's violations. He had cited them. He had shut them down. He had never inherited them.

He signed the form.

The System accepted it with a chime that sounded, to Gerald, like a gavel.

Core 4491's footprint was now his, and the notification came immediately. His Compliance Rating dropped from 71 to 38.

The math was not clean.

It was 71 minus 3, but the System applied a weighted average that accounted for the size of the inherited footprint relative to his own, and the result was 38.

He noted the number. He did not react to it.

His mold spores dimmed. He registered it like a building inspector notes a draft: as data, not sensation, and the water leak slowed to a trickle. The rat, which had been gnawing at a support post in the east corridor, stopped gnawing and lay down in the middle of the floor with its eyes half-closed.

Gerald walked to the seam. It was gone. In its place was bare concrete, identical to the rest of his dungeon floor.

He knelt. He pressed his palm flat against the surface.

He felt the temperature differential, and the concrete was cold here. Colder than it should have been for a space that had been sealed and unoccupied.

He pulled out his tape measure.

He measured the cold patch. Fourteen inches from the shared wall.

Two feet wide, and he measured the depth by tapping the floor and listening to the resonance. Hollow. The void below was approximately four feet deep at this point, consistent with his Form 7-A entry.

He opened his inventory and withdrew a hammer and a chisel. He had added them to his inventory three chapters ago, after the incident with the load-bearing wall in the north corridor. He had not expected to use them on his own floor.

He started at the edge of the cold patch. He chiseled carefully.

Municipal code required that any structural modification to a sub-basement floor be preceded by a survey of the space below.

He was not surveying, and he was opening a hole. He noted this discrepancy.

He continued chiseling.

The concrete gave way after six inches. Below it was packed earth, dry and pale, like the ground beneath a foundation slab that had never been properly sealed.

He dug deeper.

At eighteen inches he hit wood. A support beam, old, dark, the kind of timber used in residential construction before the 1970s. He brushed the dirt away from it.

The beam was cracked along its length, a split that ran from end to end like a zipper opening.

He ran his fingers along the crack. The wood was soft at the fracture point. Rot. Not wet rot, dry rot, the kind that crept in and then took the whole thing at once.

He dug around the beam. Exposed more of it. Exposed the walls of the void below, and there, in the far wall of the void, he saw the crack.

Not in the wood. In the earth.

A fissure in the packed soil, running vertically, approximately four feet long and two inches wide at its widest point.

The soil around it was discolored, a grayish-green that Gerald recognized immediately. Sulfur. Hydrogen sulfide.

The byproduct of anaerobic decomposition in waterlogged soil, and he pulled his nose back; the smell was faint but present. He opened his inventory and withdrew a gas detector he had fabricated from a System component and a length of copper wire, a project he had completed during the audit preparation phase. He held it near the fissure.

The reading was 47 parts per million, and the OSHA limit for hydrogen sulfide was 10 ppm for an eight-hour workday. At 100 ppm it became immediately dangerous. At 500 it was lethal.

Gerald pulled back. He noted the reading. He noted the source.

Core 4491 had not died of a cause, which had died of a slow poisoning.

The fissure had been releasing hydrogen sulfide into its space for an unknown duration, and the System's ventilation compliance rating had been too low to mitigate it. The rating of 3 was not a mystery.

The rating of 3 was a death certificate.

But the fissure was not natural. Gerald had spent thirty-one years looking at cracks in foundations.

He knew the difference between a settlement crack and a stress fracture and a hydraulic fracture, which was none of those. The edges were clean. The angle was wrong for natural settling.

It was too straight, too deliberate.

Someone had made this crack.

He climbed back up. He sat on the edge of the hole he had made and opened his inventory and withdrew his notebook.

He wrote: Fissure, 4 feet vertical, 2 inches wide, clean edges, consistent with directed excavation.

Source of H2S. Cause of Core 4491 death.

He wrote: Unauthorized structural modification by adjacent party.

He underlined "adjacent party." He underlined it twice.

Then the System sent him a notification he had not expected.

"Compliance Violation Detected: Unauthorized Load Transfer. A structural element in Claimant's footprint is bearing load from an adjacent claimed space without proper load path design.

This violation has been active for 847 days.

Retroactive assessment applied.

Compliance Rating adjusted: 38 to 29."

Gerald read the notification twice.

Unauthorized load transfer. His dungeon was putting weight on Core 4491's structure. Not the other way around.

Not the dead Core pressing up into his space. His space was pressing down into the dead Core's space.

For eight hundred and forty-seven days, and he did the math. Eight hundred and forty-seven days. He had become a Core approximately two hundred and sixty days ago, and the load transfer had been active for five hundred and eighty-seven days before he existed.

Something in his dungeon had been putting load on Core 4491's structure before Gerald Hutchins was a Core. Before Gerald Hutchins was a dungeon. Before the System arrived at the Ashford Municipal Library.

He stood up. He walked to the north corridor. He checked his structural map, the one he had built over forty chapters of obsessive measurement.

He looked at the load paths, and he traced them downward.

The load path from the ceiling of his dungeon did not terminate at the foundation slab. It passed through the slab. It continued downward. It terminated at a point in the void below, at a point directly above the cracked beam, directly above the fissure, directly above the source of the hydrogen sulfide.

Something above was bearing weight. Something in his dungeon was heavy enough to crack a beam in the space below. And it had been bearing that weight for five hundred and eighty-seven days before Gerald arrived.

He walked to the point on his map. It was in the west corridor. The section he had converted into a storage area for excess spawn materials. The section where he kept the calcified remains of three failed spawn attempts and a pile of System-generated gravel that he had not yet found a code-compliant use for.

He went to the west corridor, and he measured the floor. He measured the ceiling. He measured the walls.

Everything was within tolerance. Everything was up to code.

He looked at the pile of gravel.

He looked at the calcified remains, and he looked at the floor beneath them.

He knelt.

He pressed his palm flat against the concrete.

It was warm. Warmer than it should have been.

Warmer than the rest of his dungeon floor. He pressed harder. The concrete flexed.

Not much. A fraction of an inch.

But concrete should not flex.

Concrete should not flex at all, and he stood up. He opened his inventory. He withdrew the hammer and the chisel again.

He started chiseling.

The concrete broke easily. Too easily. Like it was already tired. Like it had been bearing weight it was never designed to bear, for a long time, and it had finally stopped pretending it could.

Beneath the concrete was a cavity.

Not earth. Not packed soil.

A cavity, smooth-walled, geometric, with clean corners and flat surfaces.

A room. A room that had been here before Gerald. A room that had been here before the System.

A room that was directly beneath the point where his load path terminated.

And in the room, on the floor, sitting in the center like an object placed there with intention, was a Core.

Not dormant. Not sealed. Not dead.

Active.

Its Compliance Rating was 100.

And it was looking at him.

Gerald Hutchins stood over the cavity with his chisel in his hand and his knees on the broken concrete, and the Core on the floor looked up at him with something that was not eyes but was unmistakably looking, and it sent him a message.

Not through the System channel. Not through the notification system. Directly. Into the part of Gerald that was a Core and not a man.

It said: "You're late. I've been waiting for someone to cite me."
