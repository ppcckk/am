Chapter 78: Form 7-A

# Chapter 78

The Inspector General's auditor did not have a face. It had a geometry. A rotating polyhedron of light that hovered at the junction where Gerald's east corridor met the shared wall, its vertices casting hard shadows that moved independently of the light source. Gerald watched it scan the wall and tried not to think about the fact that he had been standing in the same position for eleven minutes.

"Shared infrastructure detected," the auditor said. Its voice came from everywhere and nowhere.

"Ventilation breach at coordinates 4-7-12.

Breach located within shared boundary. Assigning violation to both parties."

Gerald's Compliance Rating flickered. 61 dropped to 58. Then 55. The egress subcategory, already his weakest at 52, slid to 39.

He knew the number before the System displayed it.

He had felt the change in air pressure within his corridors, sensing the draft before spotting the gap. The ventilation breach was real.

He could smell it.

Stale, sulfurous, wrong. Coming from the other side of the wall. From Core 7741's side.

"Objection," Gerald said.

The polyhedron rotated. "State basis."

"The breach is exclusively on the adjacent party's side of the boundary. I am requesting a Demarcation Review under shared infrastructure protocol."

The auditor paused. Gerald watched its vertices slow, as if processing.

In thirty-one years, Gerald had filed four hundred and twelve Demarcation Requests.

He had never filed one on a wall that wasn't made of concrete.

But the principle was the same, and a shared wall was not a shared problem unless the violation crossed the line.

And Gerald knew, with the bone-deep certainty of a man who had spent three decades pressing his palm against walls and feeling for hollow spots, that this breach did not cross the line.

"Demarcation Review initiated," the auditor said. "Both parties must submit boundary coordinates within ninety seconds."

Core 7741 submitted first. Gerald saw the coordinates appear in his peripheral vision, a translucent overlay on the shared wall. Core 7741 had placed the boundary line six inches inside Gerald's corridor.

Gerald stared at it. Then he walked to the wall.

He pressed his hand flat against the surface, and he felt the temperature gradient. He felt the vibration of Core 7741's ventilation fan on the other side, six inches away, pushing air through a duct that had been cut into the wall's interior. The duct was entirely within Core 7741's spatial footprint.

The breach was entirely within Core 7741's spatial footprint.

But Core 7741 had moved the boundary. If Gerald accepted the coordinates, the breach would be inside the shared zone. If he disputed them, he had to prove the true line.

Gerald pulled out his measuring tool. Not a System tool.

A physical tape measure, the kind he had carried since 1994, the one with the worn rubber grip and the blade that had been replaced twice.

He hooked it on the corridor floor at the point where his handrail bracket met the wall. He ran the tape to the baseboard on the opposite side of the corridor.

"Seventy-two inches," he said. "My corridor is seventy-two inches wide.

The handrail bracket is mounted at the four-inch mark from the shared wall.

The ventilation duct penetrates the wall at the three-inch mark from the shared wall.

That is one inch inside my corridor."

The auditor rotated faster. "Core 7741's submitted boundary places the shared zone at six inches. Your measurement places it at one inch.

Discrepancy noted. Reconciling."

"Reconcile by physical verification," Gerald said. "Cut the wall. Look at the duct."

The auditor did not cut the wall. It phased through it. Gerald watched the light pass through solid stone like it was water. Two seconds later it reappeared on Gerald's side.

"Physical verification complete. Ventilation duct is located at the 2.8-inch mark from the shared boundary as defined by Core 7741's submitted coordinates. Under those coordinates, the duct is within the shared zone. Violation stands."

Gerald closed his eyes.

Core 7741 had done it again. The boundary coordinates were the weapon. If Gerald had submitted his own coordinates, he would have placed the boundary at the one-inch mark.

The duct would have been on Core 7741's side.

But Core 7741 had submitted first, and the System had accepted the first submission as the default reference frame.

He had one move left.

Gerald had learned it from a parking garage dispute in 2011. Two property owners. Shared retaining wall.

One side had a crack. The other side's engineer had submitted boundary coordinates that placed the crack in the shared zone.

Gerald had resolved it by invoking a rule that predated the System by a century.

"Core 7741 submitted coordinates based on a spatial reference frame that is not my own," Gerald said. "I am requesting that the Demarcation Review use the absolute System coordinate grid rather than either party's local reference frame."

The auditor stopped rotating, which was new. Gerald could tell because the light dimmed for half a second, a hesitation that had not appeared in any of his previous Demarcation Reviews. The System was processing.

"Absolute grid demarcation is not standard protocol," the auditor said.

"Standard protocol requires both parties to submit coordinates. Only one party submitted. The default reference frame was unilaterally established. I am requesting the correction."

A pause. Three seconds. Gerald counted them.

"Absolute grid demarcation authorized. Recalculating."

The shared wall glowed.

A line of light appeared within the stone, precise and vertical, running from floor to ceiling. Gerald watched it settle.

It landed at the 3.1-inch mark from the wall's interior face. The ventilation duct was at 2.8. The line was 0.3 inches to the right of the duct.

The duct was entirely on Core 7741's side.

"Violation reassigned to Core 7741 exclusively," the auditor said. "Gerald Hutchins, Core 7741, egress subcategory violation dismissed. Compliance Rating restored to 61."

Gerald exhaled. The air in his corridor tasted like iron and old paper, the way it always did after an audit. He unclipped his tape measure and put it back in his pocket.

Core 7741's message arrived thirty seconds later.

Core 7741: "Clever.

You know that absolute grid demarcation is a one-time provision. You can't invoke it again for this shared wall. The line is now fixed.

If I create another breach on my side, it will be inside the shared zone by definition.

I just need to be careful about where I put it."

Gerald did not respond. He was looking at the line of light in the wall, and it was still visible, a faint seam where the System had drawn its boundary. He noticed something about it.

The line was not vertical, which was angled.

Not much. A fraction of a degree. But Gerald had spent his career looking for fractions of a degree.

A wall that was out of plumb by a fraction of a degree was a wall that was settling. A wall that was settling was a wall that was going to fail.

He walked to the floor where the line met the baseboard. He knelt. He pressed his face close to the stone and followed the seam downward, where it disappeared into the foundation.

The line did not stop at the foundation. It continued. Downward. Into the earth beneath his dungeon.

Gerald had never mapped below his dungeon's floor, and he had no reason to.

The System's spatial footprint ended at the lowest point of his registered area. Everything below was unregistered, unmeasured, unowned.

Standard practice.

But the Demarcation line had been drawn using the absolute System coordinate grid. And the absolute grid extended downward. It always had. Gerald had just never needed to look.

He pressed his palm against the floor. He felt the line.

It was faint, a vibration in the stone, a hum at a frequency just below hearing, which went down. And it did not stop at the bottom of his dungeon; it continued into a space that Gerald had never mapped.

A space that was not his.

A space that had coordinates.

"Auditor," Gerald said.

The polyhedron was still hovering. "Query acknowledged."

"The absolute grid demarcation line extends below my registered spatial footprint. There is unregistered space beneath my dungeon. Is that space assigned to another Core?"

The auditor rotated. This time. Gerald watched it think.

"Query processed.

Demarcation line intersects with a dormant spatial footprint. Footprint was registered under Core 4491. Core 4491 status: deregistered.

Deregistration date: 1,247 days ago. Deregistration was not completed.

Spatial footprint was not released to the unassigned pool."

Gerald stood up. His knees ached. He was fifty-four and he had been kneeling on stone.

"Define 'not completed,'" he said.

"Core 4491's deregistration protocol was initiated but not finalized. The Core's spatial footprint remains embedded in the System's coordinate grid. No entity is currently operating within the footprint. The footprint is classified as abandoned infrastructure."

"Abandoned infrastructure is subject to absorption by an adjacent active Core," Gerald said.

He knew this. Every inspector knew this.

An abandoned structure on a municipal parcel could be absorbed into the adjacent parcel's jurisdiction, and it was how old buildings became part of new ones. It was how a city grew.

"Correct," the auditor said. "Absorption is automatic upon adjacent Core registration. Core 4491's spatial footprint was absorbed by Core 7741's spatial footprint upon Core 7741's registration. Core 7741 inherited Core 4491's compliance history."

Gerald felt cold. "Core 4491's compliance history."

"Core 4491's final Compliance Rating at time of deregistration: 3."

The number hung in the air. Gerald looked at the auditor. The auditor did not blink. It had no eyes.

"Core 7741's inherited compliance history includes Core 4491's full violation record," the auditor continued. "Core 7741's current Compliance Rating of 61 reflects an average of its own performance and Core 4491's inherited record. Core 4491's rating of 3 is weighted at 40% of the composite."

Gerald did the math. If Core 4491's rating of 3 was weighted at 40%, and Core 7741's actual rating was X, then 0.4 times 3 plus 0.6 times X equals 61. X equals 100. Core 7741's actual compliance rating was 100.

Core 7741 was not gaming the audit system. Core 7741 was already perfect.

It had been perfect for two audits, and the 61 was not a ceiling. It was a floor. Core 7741 was hiding its true score behind the dead weight of a Core that had been deregistered but never properly closed out.

And Gerald had just used the absolute grid demarcation to find it.

The auditor spoke again. "Note: Core 4491's spatial footprint was absorbed by Core 7741, not by Gerald Hutchins, Core 7741. However, the Demarcation Review has established that Gerald Hutchins' spatial footprint is adjacent to Core 4491's footprint. Under municipal absorption doctrine, an adjacent active Core may file a Claim of Abandoned Infrastructure and petition for transfer of the dormant footprint."

Gerald looked at the message channel. Core 7741 had sent another message.

Core 7741: "You found it.

I was hoping you wouldn't. You have until the end of the audit window to decide whether to file the Claim.

If you do, you inherit Core 4491's rating of 3.

If you don't, I keep it. And I keep the 40% drag on my own perfect score. You should understand what that means for me.

You should understand what it means for you. The audit window closes in four minutes."

Gerald looked at the wall. The line of light was fading. The seam was closing. In four minutes it would be gone, and the absolute grid demarcation would be used up, and Core 4491's footprint would remain buried under Gerald's dungeon floor like a body under a foundation slab.

He pulled out his tape measure, and he knelt again.

He ran the tape from the baseboard to the point where the line of light had been. Fourteen inches.

The dormant footprint started fourteen inches from the shared wall and extended downward into unmeasured space.

Gerald Hutchins had been a municipal building inspector for thirty-one years, and he had filed four hundred and twelve Demarcation Requests. He had never once been asked to claim a dead man's property.

He opened his Form 7-A. Abandoned Infrastructure Claim.

He had never filled one out, and he had seen it in the municipal code index. He had never needed it.

His hands were steady. His hands were always steady.

The audit window closed in three minutes and forty seconds.

His pen had not moved in six minutes.
