Chapter 88: Inverted Fire Resistance

# Chapter 88

The salamander was screaming.

Gerald heard it before he smelled the burning, and a thin, high sound, like a kettle at the exact moment before the whistle.

He turned from the north wall where he'd been measuring the thickness of the green material and saw his fire-resistant spawn curled in the corner of Corridor Seven, its scales blackening at the edges.

Not from an external heat source. From inside. Its own fire resistance had become a fire vulnerability.

The System had inverted his bonus.

He grabbed the extinguisher he'd installed at the four-meter mark, the one that met NFPA-10 Class A specifications, and sprayed the salamander. It didn't help. The creature wasn't burning from the outside. It was burning from the inside, its thermal regulation reversed.

Gerald set the extinguisher down. He watched the salamander die.

He noted the time. 0347 hours, and he noted the location. Corridor Seven, 4.2 meters from the fire suppression unit. He noted the cause.

He did not note his feelings about it.

He walked to his desk. The citation was already there. It had arrived while he was at the north wall, printed itself onto the form paper that the System provided, and slid into the outgoing pile as if it had always been there.

Form 7-A. Excessive Compliance.

Category: Fire Safety.

Current Rating: 94/100.

Regulatory Ceiling: 85/100. Excess: 9 points.

Penalty Applied: Inversion of category bonus. Duration: Until compliance is brought within ceiling parameters.

Gerald read it twice. He set it down.

He picked up his tape measure and walked to the fire suppression unit.

He measured it. He measured the clearance around it. He measured the distance to the nearest egress sign.

He measured the height of the mounting bracket.

Everything was correct. Everything was correct to the millimeter.

He could not lower his fire safety compliance, and he tried; stood in front of the extinguisher and considered removing it. His hand shook.

His stomach turned. He had installed that extinguisher because the System had cited him for missing fire suppression, and he had installed it correctly because correct was the only way he knew how to install things.

To remove it would be to admit that correct was wrong.

That the rules were wrong.

That thirty-one years of measuring door widths had been a waste.

His stomach turned again, and he sat down. He opened the municipal code. Not the System's code.

His code. The one he'd carried in his briefcase for three decades, the one with the coffee stain on page 214 and the pencil notes in the margins.

He flipped to the section on maximum standards.

In municipal law, a maximum standard came in two flavors. A prohibition maximum meant you could not exceed it. Period.

The work was illegal, and a penalty maximum meant you could exceed it, but you would be fined. The distinction mattered because a prohibition maximum could not be challenged on proportionality grounds.

A penalty maximum could.

Gerald looked at the citation again. The System had applied a penalty. Inversion of the bonus was a penalty.

Not a prohibition. The System had not said his fire safety compliance was illegal.

It had said it was excessive, and it had applied a proportional consequence.

Proportional.

Gerald pulled out Form 14-F.

Proportionality Review Request. He'd never used this form either.

He'd seen it in the municipal code, buried in a subsection about administrative appeals. Section 11.7. Applicable when a regulatory penalty is disproportionate to the violation it addresses.

He started filling it out.

Violation cited: 9 points above ceiling. Penalty applied: Complete inversion of a 94-point category bonus.

Gerald wrote: The penalty of 94-point inversion is disproportionate to a 9-point excess.

A proportional penalty would be a 9-point reduction, bringing the effective rating to 85/100; the applied penalty exceeds the violation by a factor of 10.4.

He signed it. He put it in the outgoing pile.

Then he waited.

The System's response came in eleven minutes.

Form 14-G. Proportionality Review: Granted.

Penalty Modified.

Original penalty (inversion) vacated. Modified penalty: 9-point reduction to category bonus. Effective fire resistance: 85%.

Duration: Until compliance adjusted or ceiling revised.

Gerald exhaled, and he went to Corridor Seven.

The salamander was dead. He noted the time of death.

He noted the cause. He noted that the System's modified penalty would prevent further losses but could not reverse them.

He added the salamander to his spawn inventory. Adjusted count: zero fire-resistant entities.

He sat back down. The clock read 62 hours, 11 minutes.

The Conflict of Interest citation was still in the system, still processing, and the IG had not appealed it. The IG had not responded to it. The IG had simply issued a counter-citation and moved on.

Gerald understood this. It was how municipal bureaucracy worked. You didn't fight the citation. You issued a bigger one.

He needed to find out what the IG's compliance ratings were. If the IG was over the ceiling, the same penalty would apply. If the IG was over the ceiling and had been suppressing the penalty through some mechanism, that mechanism would be a violation.

He walked to the north wall, and the green material was still there. He placed his hand on it.

He felt the pulse.

He felt the boundary. He felt the IG on the other side, and this time he felt something else. A resistance.

Not physical. Structural.

The green material was thicker on the IG's side, and he measured it with his tape measure, holding the end against the boundary and reading the thickness.

22 centimeters.

He measured his own side. 8 centimeters.

The IG's boundary wall was 2.75 times thicker than his. That was not a coincidence.

That was structural compliance. The IG had built their boundary to a higher standard than Gerald's. Which meant the IG's structural code compliance was higher than Gerald's.

Which meant the IG might be over the ceiling.

Gerald pulled out his compliance scanner.

He'd built it himself, a crude thing, a modification of the System's own audit tool, and it could read a target's compliance rating if pointed at a shared boundary. He'd used it on 2201-F once, early on, and gotten a partial reading.

He'd never gotten a full one, and he pointed it at the wall. The scanner flickered. The numbers came in slow, like a signal struggling through interference.

Structural: 127/100. Fire: 131/100.

Electrical: 118/100.

Ventilation: 144/100. Egress: 139/100.

Gerald stared at the numbers. Every category.

The IG was over the ceiling in every category.

By 42, 46, 33, 59, and 54 points respectively.

And the IG had no penalties applied. No inversions.

No reductions. Nothing.

The IG was operating above the ceiling with no consequence.

Gerald's mind moved through the municipal code. How did a regulatory body operate above the ceiling without penalty?

One mechanism existed.

He'd seen it once, in a footnote. A Waiver. Section 3.2.

Applicable when a regulatory official's compliance is deemed necessary for the maintenance of regulatory function.

The IG's office could grant itself a waiver from the ceiling because its compliance was required to enforce the ceiling on others.

A waiver. The IG had waived their own ceiling. And Gerald's Conflict of Interest citation, if processed, would invalidate the waiver. Because a conflicted official could not grant themselves a waiver.

The IG knew this, which was why the IG hadn't appealed the Conflict of Interest citation. The IG needed it to remain unprocessed. As long as it sat in the queue, the waiver stood.

The moment it was processed, the waiver fell, and the IG's penalties would apply across every category simultaneously.

Gerald checked the queue.

His Conflict of Interest citation was in position 4,417.

Behind it were 4,416 other citations. The System processed them in order.

At the current rate, it would be processed in approximately 31 hours.

31 hours. The IG had 31 hours before the waiver fell. The IG had 62 hours before Gerald's own deadline.

Gerald did the math. The IG had 31 hours of immunity.

After that, the IG would be in violation across every category.

The penalties would apply. The IG's dungeon would be weakened. Gerald could finish it in the remaining 31 hours.

Unless the IG did something to prevent the citation from being processed.

Gerald went back to his desk, and he pulled out the municipal code; he turned to the section on citation withdrawal.

Section 8.1.

A citation could be withdrawn by the issuing party at any time before processing; Gerald would not withdraw it. He would never withdraw it.

Another method prevented processing. Section 8.4.

A citation could be suspended if the respondent filed a Jurisdictional Challenge.

The respondent could argue that the issuing party lacked jurisdiction over them.

If the challenge succeeded, the citation was voided.

The IG could file a Jurisdictional Challenge. They could argue that Gerald, as a Core, had no jurisdiction over the Inspector General's office. That a Core could not cite a regulatory body.

Gerald had never tested this. He had issued citations to other Cores. He had issued the Conflict of Interest citation to the IG.

He had assumed it would process, and had not considered that the IG could challenge his authority to issue it.

He looked at the queue.

Position 4,417. Still unprocessed. The IG had not filed a challenge.

Not yet.

But the IG had 31 hours. And the IG had 62 hours.

And the IG was smart.

Gerald could feel it through the wall. The pressing. The waiting.

The pen scratching that had stopped, and the IG was not writing a response; the IG was writing a challenge.

Gerald's scanner pinged.

A new notification.

Form 8-D. Jurisdictional Challenge filed.

Respondent: Inspector General, Office of System Compliance. Challenge: Issuing party (Gerald Hutchins, Core 2201-E) lacks jurisdiction over a regulatory official. Request: Void citation 4,417.

The System had accepted the challenge. It was now in the queue ahead of Gerald's citation. Position 1. The challenge would be processed first.

Gerald sat still.

If the challenge succeeded, the Conflict of Interest citation was voided. The waiver stood. The IG remained immune. And Gerald would have no way to reach the IG before the deadline.

If the challenge failed, the citation would process. The waiver would fall. The IG would be in violation across every category.

The challenge would be decided by the System; the System would evaluate whether a Core had jurisdiction over a regulatory official. Gerald had no way to argue this. The System made its own determination.

Unless.

Gerald opened the municipal code. Section 9.4.

Conflict of Interest, and he read the subsection on jurisdiction. It said: A regulatory official who has a conflict of interest in a matter forfeits their right to challenge the jurisdiction of the party bringing the conflict of interest claim.

Gerald read it again. He read it a third time.

The IG could not challenge Gerald's jurisdiction.

Not because Gerald had jurisdiction. Because the IG had forfeited the right to challenge it.

By filing the challenge, the IG had violated Section 9.4, and the challenge itself was a violation.

Gerald pulled out a new citation form. Form 11-D.

Violation of Conflict of Interest Protocol.

Citation issued to: Inspector General, Office of System Compliance.

Violation: Filed Jurisdictional Challenge against a Conflict of Interest citation, in violation of Section 9.4. Supporting evidence: Citation 4,417 (Conflict of Interest), Challenge 1 (Jurisdictional), Section 9.4 text.

He signed it. He put it in the outgoing pile.

Then he looked at the clock. 61 hours, 58 minutes.

The System's response to his new citation came in four minutes. Form 8-D. Jurisdictional Challenge: Denied.

Basis: Respondent forfeited right to challenge per Section 9.4. Citation 4,417: Cleared for processing.

Gerald exhaled.

Then the System printed something else. A form he had not seen before. Form 2-A. Temporary Structure Classification Review.

He picked it up.

Subject: Core 2201-E (Gerald Hutchins).

Classification: Temporary Structure. Basis: No permanent foundation detected. No municipal permit on file.

No structural inspection within current cycle.

Demolition deadline: 48 hours, 12 minutes.

Gerald read it. He read it again. His hands were steady. They were always steady.

His dungeon was a temporary structure. It had always been a temporary structure. He had built handrails and fire suppression and ventilation systems and egress routes, but none of it mattered because the System had never classified his dungeon as permanent. And temporary structures had demolition deadlines.

48 hours, 12 minutes, and he needed a structural inspection. He needed someone to certify his dungeon as permanent. He needed the Inspector General.

The same Inspector General who was trying to destroy him.

His only appointment was with his enemy.
