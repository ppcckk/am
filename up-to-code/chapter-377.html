Chapter 377: Bigger Table

# Chapter 377

The red tape tightened. It was not metaphorical.

It was a physical constriction of binding authority, thick and fibrous, smelling of old glue and bureaucratic malice.

The Inspector General struggled against it, his white robes crumpling under the pressure of System-enforced compliance. Gerald Hutchins did not look back. He walked toward the hole the rat had dug.

The hole was wrong, which was the first thought. The second was that the hole was too deep.

Gerald adjusted his clipboard.

The rat, still clutching its trowel, had bypassed bedrock entirely. It had tunneled into the conceptual strata that lay beneath the dungeon's physical footprint.

Usually, a Core could not dig this deep.

You needed a permit for anything below the Foundation Layer. You needed three signatures, a surveyor's seal, and a public notice period of thirty days.

This rat had none of those things.

The air grew cold. Not the damp chill of a water leak, but the dry, sterile cold of an archive room that hadn't been opened in centuries.

Gerald's Compliance Rating flickered in the corner of his vision.

It dropped from 47 to 12, and the System was punishing him for the unauthorized excavation.

He kept walking.

Behind him, the Inspector General's voice rose in a shriek that sounded like tearing metal. **

"YOU HAVE INVALIDATED THE AUDIT! THE HEARING IS VOID!"**

"Objection overruled," Gerald muttered, not turning around. "Improper venue."

The tunnel narrowed. The walls were no longer stone. They were parchment.

Thick, yellowed sheets of vellum pressed together with the force of tectonic plates. Ink ran down the rock face in rivers, forming legible lines of legalese that shifted as Gerald passed them.

He didn't read them, and he didn't need to. He recognized the font. It was the same font used on Form 11-B, the form for Unlicensed Biological Entities.

The rat scurried ahead, its claws scratching against the paper walls. It stopped at a junction. Three tunnels branched off, each labeled in faint, glowing script.

*APPEALS DIVISION.* *EXEMPTION REQUESTS.* *FINAL DISPOSITION.*

Gerald took the middle one, and he knew the layout. He had memorized the Ashford Municipal Code, Section 9, Subsection 4, Paragraph C: *All subterranean pathways leading to administrative voids must be marked and shall not be used for egress.*

This tunnel was not marked. It was not egress. it was a private corridor. And Gerald had the right to inspect private corridors if they impacted public safety.

The tunnel opened into a vast, circular chamber. The ceiling was lost in darkness. The floor was a mosaic of shattered stamps and broken seals. In the center stood a pedestal, empty.

Gerald's chest tightened.

A familiar nausea rose in his throat. The void.

The lack of structure.

The absence of a filing cabinet. It felt like an open wound in reality.

"Where is it?" Gerald asked. His voice echoed, flat and dry.

The rat squeaked. It pointed its trowel at the ceiling.

High above, suspended in chains of golden light, hung a massive hourglass. But the sand inside was not sand. It was teeth. Tiny, human teeth, grinding against each other with a sound like hail on a tin roof.

A figure emerged from the shadows behind the pedestal, which was tall, thin, and wore a suit cut from shadow and static. Its face was a smooth, featureless oval of grey glass.

"Visitor," the figure said. Its voice did not come from a mouth. It vibrated the floor. "You are off-site."

Gerald clicked his pen.

"I am within the jurisdiction of the Ashford Municipal Library sub-basement. Specifically, the area directly beneath the biography section. I am conducting an emergency inspection due to structural instability."

The figure tilted its head.

"This is the Court of Last Resort. You have no standing."

"I have a Citation," Gerald said. He held up the red tape that still dangled from his wrist.

"Served to the Inspector General.

Which means I am the plaintiff.

And you are the witness stand.

"

The figure stepped forward. The floor cracked beneath its foot.

"I am the Arbiter. I do not take orders from a rat-handler."

"You are in violation of Code 4.2," Gerald said, writing in the air. The words burned gold against the dark. "No entity shall obstruct a Core's attempt to correct a hazard. You are obstructing."

The Arbiter laughed. It was a sound like paper tearing.

"You think you can cite me?

I am the system that judges the citations. I am the logic that holds the dungeon together.

You are a glitch, Gerald Hutchins.

A bug in the code.

"

Gerald stopped writing. He looked at the Arbiter. He looked at the hourglass of teeth. He looked at the rat.

The rat was digging at the base of the pedestal.

Gerald realized the truth then, and the Arbiter was not guarding the hourglass. The Arbiter *was* the hourglass.

The teeth were its fuel.

The grinding was its heartbeat.

If the hourglass stopped, the Arbiter died.

But the hourglass was running out. The sand was low. The teeth were fewer.

"Your fuel supply is non-compliant," Gerald said softly.

The Arbiter froze.

"Section 7, Subsection 1: *All energy sources within a dungeon must be sustainable and renewable.* You are consuming finite biological matter. You are not recycling. You are not replenishing. You are in a state of terminal decay."

The Arbiter's glass face rippled.

"We have always been so."

"Always is not a defense," Gerald said. "It is an admission of guilt."

He raised his hand. The gold light of the Citation flared.

**"CITATION: ILLEGAL CONSUMPTION OF NON-RENEWABLE RESOURCES. FINE: TERMINAL."**

The air in the chamber exploded.

The Arbiter shrieked. The chains holding the hourglass snapped. The golden light shattered. The hourglass fell.

It did not break. It floated.

The teeth inside stopped grinding. The silence was absolute.

The Arbiter lunged. Its shadow-hand reached for Gerald's throat.

Gerald did not dodge. He did not fight. He stepped aside.

He had calculated the trajectory. He had measured the reach. He had checked the floor for trip hazards.

The Arbiter's hand passed through the space where Gerald's neck had been, grasping only air.

Gerald turned and ran, and he ran not away from the Arbiter, but toward the wall of the chamber. He ran toward the mosaic of shattered stamps. He ran toward the only structural weak point in the room, identified by the crack in the floor where the Arbiter had first stepped.

The rat was already there. It had dug a small hole. Just enough to fit a hand. Just enough to fit a citation.

Gerald slammed his clipboard against the wall. The impact echoed.

**"EMERGENCY VENTILATION PROTOCOL: ACTIVATED."**

The System responded.

The walls of the chamber, made of compressed parchment, sweated. Moisture seeped out. The paper softened. The ink ran.

The Arbiter screamed as its body dissolved. The glass face cracked.

The shadow suit frayed, which was losing cohesion.

Without the hourglass's power, it could not hold its form.

Gerald watched it fall apart. He did not feel triumph. He felt the dull ache of a task completed. He checked his watch.

Three minutes and forty-two seconds.

Fast. Efficient.

But the chamber was destabilizing. The floor was buckling. The ceiling was lowering. The dungeon was trying to collapse.

"Gerald!"

The voice was small. High-pitched.

Gerald looked down. The rat was standing on its hind legs. It held up the trowel. It pointed to the ground.

There was a second hole.

Deeper. Darker.

The hole the rat had dug originally. The one that went through the bedrock.

Gerald looked at the collapsing chamber. The red tape binding the Inspector General outside was tightening, cutting off the external pressure. The dungeon was sealing itself. If Gerald stayed here, he would be buried under tons of wet paper and broken glass.

He had to leave.

But he could not leave through the entrance. The entrance was collapsing.

He looked at the deep hole.

It led down. Further down than the Foundation Layer. Into the Root.

No Core went to the Root. It was forbidden. It was unmeasured. It was the place where the System stored the errors it couldn't delete.

The rat squeaked again. It jumped into the hole.

Gerald hesitated. His stomach churned. The lack of signage.

The lack of a permit. The sheer, terrifying ambiguity of the descent.

He looked at his Compliance Rating, which was dropping. 12. 10. 8.

If it hit zero, he would be deleted.

The Arbiter's remains were forming a vortex, pulling everything toward the center of the room. The walls were closing in.

Gerald made a decision, and he grabbed the rat. It wriggled in his hand, cold and sharp.

"Fine," Gerald said. "But we file the paperwork later."

He jumped.

The fall was short. The hole was deep, but the shaft was narrow. Gerald slid down the smooth, paper-lined tunnel. The air smelled of sulfur and old ink.

He landed on something soft, which was not dirt. It was not stone.

It was a pile of clothes.

Thousands of them. Shirts, pants, suits, uniforms. Piled high in a cavern that stretched beyond the reach of light.

Gerald stood up. The rat scurried from his hand, exploring the mound of fabric.

Gerald looked around. The cavern was silent. But it was not empty.

In the distance, figures moved. They were wrapped in the same clothes. They wore the faces of the clothes.

A man in a firefighter's jacket stood still, staring at nothing. A woman in a nurse's uniform walked in a circle, her feet leaving no footprints. A child in a school uniform held a book that was blank.

They were the Discarded.

The entities that the System had deemed non-compliant. The failed spawns. The bad designs.

The broken Cores, and they had been dumped here. In the Root.

Gerald's hand shook.

He had always believed in order. In rules. In the idea that if you followed the code, you were safe.

But the code had a trash can. And the trash can was full of people.

One of the figures, a large man in a police officer's uniform, turned. His face was a mask of blank porcelain.

"Inspector," the figure said. The voice was a chorus of many voices. "You have come to the end of the line."

Gerald straightened his tie. He pulled out his clipboard.

"I have not," Gerald said. "I have come to inspect."

The police officer stepped forward. The ground trembled.

"Inspection requires authority," the officer said. "You are a Core. But you are not a Core of the Root."

"I am a Core of the Library," Gerald said. "And the Library is above. Therefore, I have jurisdiction over everything that falls through it."

The officer paused. The logic held. It was twisted, but it was code.

The other figures stopped moving. They turned toward Gerald. Hundreds of eyes, blank and watching.

The rat chattered nervously. It climbed onto Gerald's shoulder.

Gerald looked at the pile of clothes. He looked at the broken entities. He realized something that made his blood run cold.

He had cited the Inspector General for being a hazard. He had cited the Arbiter for being unsustainable.

But what if the System itself was the hazard?

What if the entire dungeon system was a violation?

A new window appeared in Gerald's vision. It was not white. It was black.

**SYSTEM ERROR: UNDEFINED ENTITY DETECTED.** **SOURCE: GERALD HUTCHINS.** **ACTION: PURGE?**

Gerald stared at the text. The cursor blinked.

Yes.

The word floated in the darkness.

Gerald's heart hammered against his ribs. He had never felt fear like this.

Not from a dragon.

Not from a rival Core. But from the realization that he was the anomaly.

He looked at the rat. The rat looked back.

Its eyes were not confused anymore, and they were sharp.

Knowing.

The rat opened its mouth.

It did not squeak.

It spoke.

"Run," the rat said.

The pile of clothes shifted. The figures lunged.

Gerald turned. He ran back up the shaft. The paper walls tore. The root climbed.

But the shaft was narrowing. The System was closing the door.

And below him, in the darkness of the Root, a new sound began.

Not a scream.

A stamp.

*Thud.*

*Thud.*

*Thud.*

Something huge was waking up. Something made of every violation the System had ever tried to hide.

Gerald reached the top of the shaft. He pulled himself into the collapsing chamber. The Arbiter was gone.

The hourglass was dust. The hole in the floor was gone.

He was back in the main dungeon.

The red tape that had bound the Inspector General was gone, and the Inspector General stood in the center of the room. His robes were pristine. His face was blank.

He looked at Gerald.

"You have seen the Root," the Inspector General said.

Gerald nodded. He was breathless. His clipboard was torn. His tie was askew.

"What is it?" Gerald asked.

The Inspector General smiled. It was a terrible, thin smile.

"It is the appeal," he said. "And you just filed it."

The air in the dungeon changed. The walls became transparent. Through the stone, Gerald could see the other dungeons in Ashford.

And they were all looking at him.

Hundreds of Cores. Thousands of spawns. All of them turning their heads toward the Library.

The System message flashed across Gerald's vision, burning red.

**GLOBAL HEARING INITIATED.** **APPELANT: GERALD HUTCHINS.** **DEFENDANT: THE SYSTEM.** **TIME LIMIT: 60 MINUTES.**

Gerald looked at the clock on the wall.

Fifty-nine minutes left, and he looked at the rat.

The rat shrugged.

Gerald clicked his pen.

"Fine," he said. "I'll need a bigger table."
