Chapter 301: Survive Without Clipboard

# Chapter 301

The knob turned with a slow, grinding hesitation that set Gerald's teeth on edge. It wasn't the snap of a latch or the click of a lock. It was the sound of metal shearing against rusted hinges, the auditory signature of a door that had not been opened in thirty years.

Gerald did not retreat. He did not check his clipboard, because the pen was broken and the paper was ash. He simply watched the gap widen.

A beam of pale, dusty light spilled onto the asphalt, illuminating floating motes of debris that had been disturbed by the truck's impact. The ceiling above groaned again, a deep, tectonic shift of concrete and rebar. Dust rained down on Gerald's shoulders, coating his suit in a fine, grey powder that looked suspiciously like drywall compound.

Through the opening, a voice emerged. It was not a monster's roar. It was a man's voice, thick with sleep and confusion.

"Hello? Is this the loading dock?"

Gerald straightened his spine. He brushed the dust from his lapel with a sharp, efficient motion.

"This is not a loading dock," Gerald said, his voice level. "This is a sub-basement corridor. And you are standing in a space that does not exist on any municipal blueprint."

The figure in the doorway stepped forward. He was wearing a janitor's uniform, blue and faded, with a name tag that read *BARNABY*. He held a mop bucket in one hand and looked at Gerald with the weary suspicion common to all facility maintenance staff.

"I've been working the third shift for six years," Barnaby said. "I know every corner of this library.

I know the steam pipes in the north wing.

I know the leak in the east stairwell. I do not know a door in the middle of a tunnel.

"

"Neither do I," Gerald replied. "Which is why we have a problem."

Barnaby blinked. He looked past Gerald, at the crushed goblin truck, and then at the shattered support pillar.

"Did you do that?"

"No," Gerald said. "A Class-IV Dungeon Core did that. Specifically, a goblin with a disregard for right-of-way regulations."

"Goblins," Barnaby said, flatly. "Right. And why are you standing there with a broken pen?"

"Because I was in the middle of a citation," Gerald said. "And you are currently obstructing a potential Hazardous Materials containment zone."

Barnaby frowned. He looked at the dark hallway behind Gerald. He looked at the truck. He looked at the door, which remained stubbornly ajar, revealing a glimpse of a room that smelled of old paper and lemon polish.

"You're a cop?" Barnaby asked.

"No," Gerald said. "I am a municipal building inspector. And you are trespassing on private property that is currently undergoing emergency structural assessment."

"Private property," Barnaby muttered.

He set the mop bucket down.

"I've got a key to that door.

I opened it to check for mold.

That's all. Just checking for mold.

"

Gerald's eyes narrowed.

"You have a key to a door that does not exist?"

"I have a master key," Barnaby said. He pulled a large ring of heavy iron keys from his belt.

The keys clinked together, a heavy, metallic sound that echoed in the confined space.

"It fits every door in the Ashford Municipal Library.

Every single one. Except the ones in the sub-basement.

Until now."

Gerald stared at the keys. Then he stared at the door.

**SYSTEM NOTIFICATION:** **NEW ENTITY DETECTED: BARNABY.** **CLASS: NON-MAGICAL CIVILIAN.** **STATUS: UNREGISTERED.** **PENALTY FOR INTERFERENCE WITH CORE OPERATIONS: 500 COMPLIANCE POINTS PER MINUTE.**

Gerald ignored the notification. He stepped closer to the door.

"Hand it over," Gerald said.

"Excuse me?" Barnaby crossed his arms. "This is my key. I earned it."

"That door did not exist five minutes ago," Gerald said. "Therefore, the key could not have fit it five minutes ago. This implies a spatial anomaly or a breach in the dungeon's perimeter integrity. As the responsible party for this structure, I am seizing the instrument of entry to prevent further unauthorized expansion."

Barnaby laughed. It was a dry, humorless sound.

"You're seizing my master key?

You're a bureaucrat. You can't just take people's things.

"

"I have authority," Gerald said. "Under Section 4.2 of the Municipal Code, inspectors have the right to secure all points of entry when immediate danger to life or property is present."

"Show me the badge," Barnaby challenged.

Gerald reached into his pocket. He did not have a badge. He had a laminated ID card that said *Gerald Hutchins, Building Inspector, City of Ashford*. It was bent from being sat on.

He held it out. Barnaby glanced at it.

"That doesn't say 'Authority to Seize Keys'," Barnaby said.

"It implies it," Gerald said.

"Through the broader mandate of public safety. If I let you walk through that door, you might trigger a trap.

If you trigger a trap, you die.

If you die, I am liable for negligent supervision. If I am liable, I get fired. If I get fired, who inspects the library?

You?"

Barnaby hesitated, and he looked at the dark hallway again.

The air in the dungeon felt heavy, charged with static. The dust was settling, but the silence was wrong.

It was too quiet.

"I'm not going through," Barnaby said. "But I'm not giving you the key."

"Then you are obstructing justice," Gerald said. He pointed to the ceiling. "Do you hear that?"

"Hear what?"

"The stress fractures," Gerald said. "The pillar is compromised.

The ceiling is shifting.

If you stand there arguing, and the ceiling comes down, I will cite you for failing to evacuate a hazard zone. That is a misdemeanor. It carries a fine of five hundred dollars.

"

Barnaby looked up. A crack zigzagged across the concrete above them. Small pebbles fell, hitting Barnaby's shoulder.

"Okay," Barnaby said. "Okay. What do you want me to do?"

"Step away from the door," Gerald said. "And answer a question."

"What question?"

"Who else has this key?"

Barnaby shrugged.

"Me. The head of maintenance.

The night security guard, Miller.

And maybe Dave, if he still has his copy. Dave retired two years ago.

"

"Miller," Gerald said. "Is Miller inside the dungeon?"

"I don't know," Barnaby said. "He's on shift. But he's supposed to be at the front desk."

"If he is not at the front desk," Gerald said slowly, "and if he has a key that fits a door that appeared from nowhere, then we have a security breach that is not caused by a dungeon monster. It is caused by a human error."

Gerald pulled a small, crumpled form from his pocket. It was a blank Incident Report, Form 19-C. He had been saving it for a rainy day. Today was not raining, but the situation was sufficiently damp.

"Sign here," Gerald said, holding out the form and the broken pen.

"I can't sign," Barnaby said. "The pen is dead."

Gerald looked at the pen. He looked at Barnaby.

"Then you are acknowledging the incident verbally," Gerald said. "I will record it.

You are Barnaby, Janitor, Third Shift.

You are admitting to the possession of a key that accesses an unregistered spatial anomaly.

You are admitting to potential negligence in securing said anomaly. Do you deny this?

"

"I didn't know it was there!" Barnaby shouted.

"Which is the defense," Gerald said. "But ignorance does not absolve liability. It merely mitigates the penalty. Sign it."

Barnaby stared at the paper. He stared at Gerald. The dust in the air seemed to freeze.

"You're weird," Barnaby said.

"I am thorough," Gerald corrected.

He turned to the door. The handle was still turning, but the person on the other side had not come through. The voice had stopped.

"Hello?" Barnaby called out. "Is anyone in there?"

Silence.

Then, a sound. A click. Not the click of a lock. The click of a mechanism engaging.

**SYSTEM NOTIFICATION:** **NEW HAZARD DETECTED.** **TYPE: TRAP MECHANISM.** **ACTIVATION: PENDING.** **TRIGGER: HUMAN INTERACTION.**

Gerald's eyes widened. He spun toward Barnaby.

"Back away," Gerald said. "Now."

"What?"

"Move!" Gerald shoved Barnaby backward.

Barnaby stumbled, his mop bucket tipping over. Soapy water splashed onto the asphalt.

From the doorway, a thin wire snapped taut. A block of concrete, suspended by a counterweight system that should not have existed in a 1970s library basement, dropped from the ceiling directly where Barnaby had been standing.

It slammed into the asphalt with a force that shook the ground.

Barnaby fell backward, landing in the puddle of soapy water. He scrambled up, eyes wide.

"Did you see that?" Barnaby yelled. "That was a trap! A real trap!"

"It was a pressure plate," Gerald said. He was breathing hard, but his face was impassive. "Standard tripwire configuration.

Poorly calibrated. Too sensitive to weight shifts.

"

"You knew?" Barnaby asked.

"I suspected," Gerald said. "Any unauthorized entry into a non-euclidean space must be treated as a hostile intrusion until proven otherwise."

Gerald walked to the edge of the dropped concrete block. He measured the distance between the block and the door frame.

"Four inches," he muttered. "Clearance violation. Minimum egress width for a single-occupancy trap is six inches. This is non-compliant."

"Non-compliant?" Barnaby stared at him. "It just tried to crush me."

"It failed to crush you because you moved," Gerald said. "But the mechanism is still active. The wire is still connected. If anyone else walks through that door, they will trigger it again."

"So we close it," Barnaby said. He reached for his keys.

"No," Gerald said.

"We cannot close it. The door is not ours. It belongs to the entity inside.

We have to contain it.

"

"How?"

Gerald looked at the broken pen. He looked at the form. He looked at the puddle of soapy water.

"We create a barrier," Gerald said. "We cite the door for operating without a permit."

"You can't cite a door," Barnaby said.

"I can cite the owner of the door," Gerald said. "And I know who owns it now."

Gerald turned to the darkness beyond the fallen concrete block.

"Miller!" he shouted. "I know you are in there!

Your key activated the trigger.

Your presence violates the occupancy limits of this zone. Come out now, or I will file a formal complaint with the Dungeon Core's Inspector General.

"

Silence.

Then, a low groan from behind the door. Not a voice. A sound like grinding stones.

"He's not answering," Barnaby said.

"He is resisting," Gerald said. "This is a Code Violation: Failure to Provide Emergency Egress for Internal Personnel. Miller is trapped."

"Trapped?" Barnaby asked. "Or is he the monster?"

Gerald paused. He looked at the door. The handle was still turning. But it wasn't turning from the inside.

It was turning from the outside.

But there was no one there.

Unless the door itself was the entity.

**SYSTEM NOTIFICATION:** **NEW ENEMY IDENTIFIED.** **NAME: THE UNREGISTERED THRESHOLD.** **TYPE: SENTIENT ARCHITECTURAL ANOMALY.** **STATS: UNKNOWN.** **WEAKNESS: BUREAUCRATIC PUNISHMENT.**

Gerald felt a chill run down his spine. A sentient door? In thirty-one years, he had seen asbestos, black mold, and rats. He had never seen a door that wanted to be opened.

"Barnaby," Gerald said quietly. "Give me the keys."

Barnaby shook his head.

"No. I'm not going near it."

"I don't need you to go near it," Gerald said. "I need you to throw them."

"Throw them?"

"Throw the keys into the room," Gerald said. "If the door is sentient, it is hungry for access.

Give it what it wants.

But do not give it an opening.

Give it a tool.

"

Barnaby looked at the keys. He looked at the dark doorway. He looked at the massive concrete block blocking the path.

"That's insane," Barnaby said.

"It's logical," Gerald said. "The door wants to be opened. It cannot be opened from the outside because the trigger prevents it.

But if a key is thrown inside, the mechanism may attempt to lock the interior, creating a vacuum effect. It will pull air through the gap.

And if it pulls air, it will pull attention.

"

"And what happens when it pulls attention?" Barnaby asked.

"I will be there," Gerald said. He stepped closer to the door. "I will serve the citation. Physically."

"You're crazy," Barnaby said. But he stepped back.

"Throw them," Gerald commanded.

Barnaby swallowed. He wound up his arm. He hurled the master key ring with all his strength.

The keys flew through the air. They arced through the gap between the concrete block and the ceiling.

They disappeared into the darkness.

For a second, nothing happened.

Then, the door slammed shut, and the sound was louder than thunder. It echoed through the tunnel, shaking the dust from the ceiling.

The concrete block shifted. It slid sideways, grinding against the asphalt, revealing a narrow sliver of light.

And from that sliver, a voice whispered.

"Denied."

Gerald froze.

"Denied?" Barnaby asked.

"The door denied entry," Gerald whispered. "It refused the key."

"Then what does it want?"

Gerald looked at his hands. He looked at the broken pen. He looked at the form in his pocket.

"It doesn't want access," Gerald said. "It wants a witness."

The door opened again. Slowly. Deliberately.

Standing in the doorway was not Miller. It was not a monster.

It was a man in a suit. A grey suit. A grey face. And a clipboard.

The man stepped out. He looked at Gerald. He looked at Barnaby. He looked at the fallen concrete.

"Inspector Hutchins," the man said. His voice was like dry paper tearing. "You have filed a report on an illegal structure. But you have not filed a report on the entity that maintains it."

"Who are you?" Gerald asked.

"I am the Auditor," the man said. "And you are late."

The man raised his clipboard. His pen was sharp. His eyes were empty.

"Your Compliance Rating has dropped to 42," the Auditor said. "You have three minutes to explain why you have not submitted the required Form 88-B: Declaration of Sentient Infrastructure. If you fail to submit, I will initiate the Demolition Protocol."

Gerald's heart hammered against his ribs. He looked at Barnaby, who was trembling.

"I didn't know it was sentient," Gerald said.

"Ignorance is not a defense," the Auditor said. "Three minutes. Start."

Gerald looked at the blank form in his pocket. He looked at the Auditor. He looked at the broken pen.

He didn't have a pen. He didn't have a form. He didn't have time.

But Gerald Hutchins had one thing, and he had a lie detector.

**SYSTEM NOTIFICATION:** **ACTION AVAILABLE: FILE APPEAL.** **REQUIREMENT: SUBMIT FALSE TESTIMONY WITH CONVICTION.** **RISK: PERMANENT LOSS OF INSPECTOR LICENSE.**

Gerald's hand shook. He pulled out the form. He tore it in half.

"I appeal," Gerald said.

The Auditor smiled. It was a terrible, jagged smile.

"On what grounds?"

"On the grounds that I am not an inspector," Gerald said.

Barnaby gasped.

The Auditor tilted his head.

"Explain."

"I was fired," Gerald said. His voice was steady. Too steady.

"Yesterday.

By the City Council. For excessive rigidity.

For refusing to sign off on unsafe structures.

I am currently unemployed. I have no authority to issue citations. Therefore, the citations I issued to the goblins were invalid.

And you cannot audit a man who is not in the system.

"

The Auditor stared at him. The air grew cold.

"Is that true?" the Auditor asked.

Gerald looked into the empty eyes of the Auditor. He thought of his pension. He thought of his thirty-one years. He thought of the lie he was telling.

It was the first time he had ever broken the rules to save himself.

"Yes," Gerald said.

The Auditor leaned forward. He raised his pen.

"Then you are no longer protected," the Auditor said. "And you are no longer bound by the Code."

The pen came down.

Gerald flinched.

But the pen did not touch the paper. It touched his forehead.

**SYSTEM NOTIFICATION:** **STATUS CHANGE: CIVILIAN.** **PROTECTION LIFTED.** **NEW OBJECTIVE: SURVIVE.**

The Auditor laughed. It was a dry, rustling sound.

"Now," the Auditor said. "Let's see if you can survive without a clipboard."

The door slammed shut again. And this time, it locked.

Gerald stood alone in the dark. Barnaby was gone. The keys were gone. The pen was gone.

He was just Gerald.

And the ceiling was coming down.
