Chapter 25

The hacksaw bit into the concrete block and Gerald counted the strokes. One, two, three. The blade sang a thin metallic note that set his teeth on edge. He counted anyway. Four, five, six. The block gave way in a jagged line and a chunk of grey fell to the floor, releasing a puff of mineral dust that tasted like chalk and old basements.

He pulled the second block free with his bare hands. His fingers ached. The System had not given him hands that ached, but it had given him hands that were not quite hands, and they ached in a way that was more conceptual than physical. He filed this observation away under "not relevant."

His rating ticked. 3. 4. 5.

One point per ventilation channel. That was the rate. He had cut two channels so far, each one a narrow slot between the concrete blocks that ran from floor to ceiling. The System recognized them as airflow paths. It did not care that they were ugly. It did not care that they were uneven. It cared that they existed and that air could pass through them.

Gerald measured the remaining wall. Forty-three blocks. Twenty-one channels. Twenty-one points. That would put him at 26. Still short of 40.

He needed more categories. He needed fire suppression, but he had no extinguishers and no way to make them. He needed electrical compliance, but he had no wiring. He needed structural certification, but he had no load-bearing elements to certify. The hacksaw was doing what it could.

His rating ticked again. 5. Then it dropped. 4.

Gerald stopped sawing. He looked at the floor.

Three mold spores were moving. Not the usual drift. They were clustering near the base of the wall he had just cut, and the System was reading their presence as something new. A notification appeared in his peripheral vision.

*Violation: Unpermitted Biological Occupancy. Entity count exceeds registered spawn list by 3. Penalty: -1 Compliance Rating per entity.*

Gerald stared at the notification. He had three mold spores on his spawn list. He had always had three mold spores on his spawn list. They were his original spawn, the first things the System had given him when his body dissolved into a dungeon footprint. They were on his list. They had always been on his list.

But the System was now reading them differently. Their location had changed. They had migrated from their original positions to a new cluster near a wall that did not exist forty minutes ago. The System was interpreting that migration as a change in occupancy. As if they had been added to his dungeon rather than moved within it.

He looked at the notification again. The penalty was per entity. Three spores, three points. He had lost one point so far, which meant the System was counting them as a single group for now, but the language said "per entity." If it switched to individual counting, he would lose two more.

Gerald set down the hacksaw. He walked to the cluster. The mold spores were small, grey-green, pulsing faintly. They had been his only company for twenty-four chapters of this absurd situation. He felt something that was not quite grief. He felt something closer to the feeling of discovering that a tenant had sublet a unit without permission.

He tried to move them. He could not. The System had placed them where they were and would not let him relocate them without a Form 9-A (Biological Entity Transfer), which required a reason. The reason he had was "they are costing me Compliance Rating points." The System rejected this reason. The reason had to be structural, not punitive.

Gerald sat on the floor. He thought.

In municipal code, there was a concept called "de minimis." A violation so minor that it was not worth enforcing. A door that was 35 inches wide instead of 36. A handrail that was 33 inches high instead of 34. The code had tolerances. The System's code had tolerances too. He had seen them in the fine print of the Compliance Audit notices. Ventilation channels needed to be at least 6 inches wide. Not 6.001. Six.

The mold spores were 0.3 inches in diameter. They were not structural. They were not load-bearing. They were not a ventilation obstruction. They were biological matter that happened to be present in a space. In municipal code, this was not an occupancy violation. It was a sanitation issue. A different category entirely. A different penalty structure.

Gerald filed a reclassification request. He typed it into the System's interface with the careful, cramped handwriting that had filled out four thousand Form 11-Bs. The language was precise. He cited the entity size, the lack of structural impact, the absence of obstruction to egress or ventilation. He requested reclassification from "Unpermitted Biological Occupancy" to "Non-Structural Biological Contamination, Minor."

The System processed it.

*Reclassification approved. Penalty adjusted to -0.5 total.*

Gerald's rating ticked back up. 4. Then 4.5. Then 5.

He picked up the hacksaw. He had thirty-seven blocks left and four hours.

He was on channel fourteen when the floor shuddered. Not a structural shudder. A deliberate one. Something was pressing against the edge of his footprint from the outside. He could feel it the way he felt a draft: a pressure differential, a boundary being tested.

Marcus.

Gerald looked at his range map. The grey dot was still there, still inside his footprint. But it was vibrating. It was trying to expand. Marcus was pushing his one block outward, trying to claim more of Gerald's square footage. If he succeeded, he would create a second overlap, and the System would have to arbitrate.

Gerald could not let that happen. Not now. Not with the audit clock running.

He tried to push back. He could not. His dungeon was 4,000 square feet and he could not compress it. He could not build a wall around Marcus's block because the block was already inside his footprint. The System had registered it. It was there. It was part of his dungeon now, whether he liked it or not.

But it was not part of his spawn list. It was not a registered structural element. It was an unpermitted construction within his own footprint.

Gerald stopped sawing. He stared at the grey dot on his map.

He had been thinking about this wrong. He had been thinking about Marcus as an external threat, a rival to be cited from a distance. But Marcus was inside his dungeon. He was a structure within Gerald's walls. And Gerald was a building inspector.

He pulled up his citation interface. He began a new document.

*Violation: Unpermitted Structural Element. Entity "Marcus" (Core ID: [redacted]) has been placed within the registered footprint of Core ID: [Gerald's ID] without a building permit, without architectural review, and without compliance with structural load requirements. The element has been present for approximately 3 hours and 47 minutes. It has not been inspected. It has not been certified. It is, by definition, an illegal construction.*

*Requested action: Immediate removal order.*

He checked the requirements. A Citation required the target to be physically present. Marcus was not present. His block was present, but Marcus himself was not.

Gerald looked at the citation rules again. He read them the way he read municipal code, line by line, looking for the exception. There was one. Section 4, Paragraph 12: "A Removal Order for an unpermitted structural element may be served on the responsible party via the element itself, provided the element is registered to the responsible party's Core ID."

Marcus's block was registered to Marcus's Core ID. It was inside Gerald's dungeon. It was an unpermitted structural element. Gerald could serve the Removal Order on the block itself. The System would then compel Marcus to appear in person to contest the order.

Gerald pressed submit.

The block shuddered. The grey dot on his map flared white, then went dark. Marcus's block was being forcibly removed. The System was enforcing the order.

But Marcus did not appear.

Gerald waited. One minute. Two. Three. The block was gone. His footprint was clean. His rating ticked up by two points. 7.

No Marcus.

Gerald checked the Removal Order status. *Served. Accepted by default. No contest filed within 30-second window.*

Marcus had not contested. That was wrong. The rules said a Core could contest a Removal Order within thirty seconds. If they did not contest, the order was executed automatically. But the rules also said that a Core who accepted a Removal Order by default forfeited their right to appeal. And a Core who forfeited appeal rights was subject to a "non-contest penalty" that reduced their Compliance Rating by five points.

Marcus had taken a five-point penalty to avoid appearing in Gerald's dungeon. That meant Marcus knew something Gerald did not. That appearing in Gerald's dungeon was worse than losing five points.

Gerald thought about this for four seconds. Then he picked up the hacksaw.

He was on channel twenty-two when his rating dropped again. Not from mold. Not from Marcus. From something else.

*Notice: Reciprocal Review Initiated. Per Inspector General Protocol 7.1, a formal complaint filed against a Core triggers a review of the complainant's citation history for good-faith compliance. The complainant's prior Citations will be examined for procedural validity. Any Citation found to be improperly served, frivolous, or lacking sufficient evidence will result in a Frivolous Citation Penalty: -10 Compliance Rating per invalid Citation.*

Gerald set down the hacksaw.

He had issued six Citations in his career as a Core. Six. He remembered each one. He had served them personally, as required. He had cited trespassing, unpermitted construction, inadequate egress, improper signage, unlicensed biological entities, and unpermitted structural elements. He had served each one in person. He had documented each one.

But "improperly served" had a specific legal definition in the System's code. It meant the Citation had to be served while the target was within the complainant's operational range, AND the target had to be physically present in a shared space. Not just within range. Present. Standing in the same room.

Gerald's first Citation. The goblin. He had cited it for trespassing. He had served it from across the room. The goblin had been within range. But had it been in a "shared space"? Gerald's dungeon was one room. The goblin was in it. Gerald was in it. That was a shared space. That was fine.

The second Citation. The water leak. He had cited a water leak. A water leak was not a Core. He had cited a physical phenomenon. The System had accepted it, but was it valid? The rules said Citations applied to "any dungeon within range." A water leak was not a dungeon. It was part of a dungeon. Gerald had cited a component of a dungeon, not a dungeon.

His hands went cold.

He had six Citations. If even one was invalid, that was ten points off. He was at 7. If the IG found one invalid, he would be at -3. A negative rating was not a number. It was a shutdown.

Gerald looked at the hacksaw. He looked at the remaining blocks. He looked at the audit timer. Three hours and twelve minutes.

He could not fix the Citations. He could not retroactively make them valid. But he could raise his rating high enough to absorb the penalty. If he got to 40, a -10 penalty would put him at 30. Still alive. Still open.

He needed thirty-three more points. He had three channels left to cut. That was three points. He needed thirty more from somewhere else.

He pulled up the provisional compliance rules. Form 7-C. Partial credit for incomplete work. The System would give him 40% of a category's full score if he filed the form and had begun work on that category. He had begun work on ventilation. That was one category. Full score for ventilation compliance was 15 points. Forty percent was six.

He filed Form 7-C for ventilation. Rating ticked. 13.

He had begun work on structural compliance. The hacksaw had cut channels through load-bearing blocks. Technically, he had modified load-bearing elements without certification. That was a violation. But he had also created new pathways that could be interpreted as structural reinforcement if he framed them correctly. He filed Form 7-C for structural compliance. The System processed it.

*Structural compliance: Provisional. Credit: 4 points.*

Rating: 17.

He had not begun work on fire suppression. He had not begun work on electrical compliance. He had not begun work on accessibility. He had not begun work on sanitation beyond the mold reclassification.

He needed categories he could claim he had begun. He needed to start something in the next three hours that could plausibly be called "work in progress."

He looked at the mold spores. They were still clustered near the wall. He had reclassified them as a sanitation issue. A sanitation issue required a sanitation plan. He did not have a sanitation plan. But he could write one. He could write a sanitation plan right now, file it, and claim that the plan itself constituted "work begun" on the sanitation category.

He opened a new document. He began to write.

He was halfway through the plan when his rating dropped again.

*Violation: Frivolous Citation Detected. Citation #2 (Water Leak) reviewed by Inspector General preliminary scan. Finding: Citation served against a non-Core entity. Invalid. Penalty applied: -10.*

Rating: 7.

Gerald stopped writing. He looked at the number. 7. He had three hours and nine minutes. He needed thirty-three points. He had three channels, one provisional ventilation claim already filed, one provisional structural claim already filed, and a sanitation plan that was not finished.

The System's preliminary scan was still running. It was reviewing the other five Citations. If it found another invalid one, he would be at -3. Shutdown. Immediate. No appeal.

He looked at his remaining Citations. The trespassing goblin. The unpermitted construction. The inadequate egress. The improper signage. The unlicensed biological entities.

The unlicensed biological entities. That was the mold spores. He had cited them in chapter three. He had cited them for being in his dungeon without a permit. But they were his spawn. They had been given to him by the System. They were licensed by definition. The System had spawned them.

That Citation was invalid. He knew it was invalid. He had known it was invalid when he filed it. He had filed it because he was confused and frightened and trying to treat the System like a municipal code, and the System had not corrected him because the System did not correct. It only enforced.

Rating: 7. Pending: one more review. If it found the biological entities Citation invalid, he would be at -3.

Gerald closed his eyes. He opened them. He picked up the hacksaw.

He had three hours and nine minutes to find twenty more points before the IG finished scanning his fifth Citation. He did not know if the fifth Citation was valid. He did not know if he could stop the scan. He did not know if he could file a retraction before the penalty applied.

He began sawing. Block twenty-three. Block twenty-four. Block twenty-five. Each one gave him one point. He needed twenty more. He had three blocks left in this wall.

He turned to the next wall. He began cutting.

The hacksaw sang its thin note. The audit timer counted down. And somewhere in the dark, the Inspector General read his file.
