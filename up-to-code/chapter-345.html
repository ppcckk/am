Chapter 345: Violation Code Zero

# Chapter 345

The pen snapped in Gerald's hand.

Ink bled across the palm, black and permanent. The shard of plastic cut into the webbing between his thumb and index finger.

He didn't flinch.

Pain was just another variable, another non-compliant element in a chaotic system. He dropped the ruined barrel of the pen onto the asphalt and picked up the backup from his pocket.

Martha was already in the driver's seat.

The engine idled, a low, angry growl that vibrated through the soles of Gerald's shoes. He opened the passenger door.

The interior smelled of stale coffee and ozone.

The air conditioning vent rattled, a rhythmic clatter that sounded like a metronome counting down to a detonation.

"Get in," Martha said. She didn't look at him. Her eyes were fixed on the rearview mirror, watching the drone that hovered three stories above the library roof. "We have maybe four minutes before it locks onto the library's main egress route."

Gerald slid into the seat. He buckled the belt. It clicked with a satisfying, mechanical precision. That click was the only order left in Ashford.

"Where are we going?" Gerald asked. His voice was flat, stripped of inflection. He was cataloging his options.

"The old textile mill on Fourth," Martha said. "Local 402 filed a stop-work order on it ten years ago.

Asbestos, lead paint, structural instability.

It's abandoned.

Legally, it's a dead zone. System-wise?

"

"High violation density," Gerald finished. "Perfect for a shield."

"It's not a shield," Martha said, shifting into drive. The car lurched forward. "It's a distraction.

The drone is tracking a warrant for *you*, Gerald. If you're standing next to a Class-4 Hazard Zone, the System has to prioritize the hazard over the suspect.

It's a bureaucratic paradox.

The drone can't arrest you if the air in the room is currently trying to kill everyone in it.

"

Gerald nodded. He pulled a small, laminated card from his breast pocket. It was his union card, faded at the edges. He held it up so the drone's red lens could see it.

The drone dipped lower.

Its lens widened, focusing. A beam of red light scanned Gerald's chest, then Martha, then the car's registration plate.

The beam moved with predatory smoothness, which was calculating risk. It was deciding if the potential for a citation outweighed the potential for a capture.

Gerald checked his tablet. The Compliance Audit timer had dropped to 00:13:42. Fourteen hours until the Inspector General arrived. Fourteen hours until Gerald would have to stand before the System and explain why his dungeon was still running.

But the fire wasn't waiting.

To the east, the skyline was a jagged silhouette of smoke. The library wasn't the only target.

Other dungeons were waking up, their cores destabilizing, their spawn lists overflowing with entities that had no place in a modern city.

A minotaur had been spotted in the parking lot of the grocery store.

A swarm of clockwork beetles was chewing through the fiber-optic cables of the phone company.

The city was failing its inspection.

"Hold on," Martha said.

She gunned the engine. The blue sedan peeled away from the curb, tires screeching against the wet pavement. The drone followed, a silent, hovering shark.

It didn't accelerate. It didn't need to.

It existed in a higher plane of logic, waiting for Gerald to make a move that violated the fundamental code of the world.

They drove through the downtown grid. Traffic was sparse. Most people had fled, or they were dead. The few cars that remained were abandoned, doors swinging open like broken ribs.

Gerald watched the scenery blur. He wasn't looking at the destruction. He was looking at the buildings.

He noted the cracked foundations. He noted the lack of proper zoning signage. He noted the overgrown ivy climbing up the brickwork of a bank, roots digging into the mortar, compromising the structural integrity.

*Violation,* he thought. *Structural compromise due to biological encroachment. Code 7.2-B.*

He pulled out his pen again. He opened a fresh page in his notebook. He wrote.

Not a narrative.

A list.

*Building 1: Corner of 5th and Main. Zoning violation. Unauthorized expansion. Penalty: Immediate cessation of operations.*

*Building 2: The old mill. Asbestos exposure. Failure to abate. Penalty: Evacuation order.*

Martha glanced at him.

"You're writing?"

"Documentation," Gerald said. "If we survive this, I'm filing a collective grievance with the System's Inspector General. This entire city is a non-compliant construction project."

Martha snorted. It was a dry, humorless sound.

"You're insane."

"I'm thorough."

The car skidded to a halt, and they were there. The old textile mill on Fourth Street. It was a hulking beast of red brick and rusted iron, three stories high.

The windows were shattered, dark holes gaping in the facade. Chains hung from the perimeter fence, but they had been cut.

Someone had been here recently.

Someone had broken in.

The drone hovered above the mill, its red eye pulsing. It was confused.

The area was saturated with high-level toxicity. The System's algorithms were struggling to parse the threat level. Was the asbestos the primary threat?

Or was Gerald the primary threat?

Gerald stepped out of the car. He didn't walk toward the mill. He walked toward the chain-link fence.

"Gerald," Martha warned.

"Stay in the car. Let me go in. It's union property.

It's mine."

"No," Gerald said. "It's not yours. It's unlicensed biological entity territory."

He approached the fence. He reached through the holes in the chain-link and grabbed a loose strand of wire. He twisted it. He tested its tension.

*Non-compliant barrier,* he thought. *Failure to maintain perimeter security. Code 1.4-A.*

He pulled. The wire didn't snap. It held.

Then he did something else. He didn't break the lock.

He didn't cut the chain, and he reached into his pocket and pulled out a small, folded piece of paper. It was a pre-filled Form 11-B, the Unlicensed Biological Entity citation. He had prepared it in the car.

He had filled it out with the drone's ID number, which he had copied from the System notification.

He slid the paper through the fence.

"Citizenship is not a right," Gerald said, his voice carrying across the empty street. "It is a privilege granted by compliance."

The drone's lens flickered. The red light intensified. It was processing the form. It was checking the validity of the citation.

A new line appeared on Gerald's tablet.

**[SYSTEM ALERT: CITATION PENDING. TARGET: UNLICENSED CONSTRUCT (DRONE). VIOLATION: OPERATING IN HAZARDOUS ZONE WITHOUT PROPER VENTILATION AND PPE.]**

The drone froze.

It hovered, motionless.

Its logic loops were crashing, and a drone was a construct. It was not a biological entity.

It could not be cited for PPE violations. But it was operating in a Zone 4 Hazard Area. And the citation had been served by a registered Core.

The System's rules were absolute. But they were also literal.

The drone tried to move. It failed.

A low hum emanated from its chassis, growing louder, then stopping abruptly, which was locked. Stunned. Not by damage, but by legal paralysis.

Gerald exhaled. His hands were shaking. He hid them in his pockets.

"Martha," he said. "Drive us to the side door. The one with the padlock."

Martha jumped out, and she ran to the car. She unlocked the door.

She got in.

Gerald followed, scrambling into the back seat. He felt sick. Not from fear, but from the exertion of improvising.

He had used a loophole. He had used a technicality that shouldn't have worked.

The System had allowed it because the drone was technically a piece of municipal infrastructure, and municipal infrastructure was subject to the same code as municipal buildings.

It was a tiny, fragile crack in the armor of the System. And Gerald had just kicked it open.

The mill was dark.

The air inside smelled of wet rot and rust. Water dripped from the ceiling in a steady, rhythmic beat. *Drip.

Drip. Drip.*

Gerald pulled out his flashlight. The beam cut through the dust motes.

He scanned the walls, and he looked for load-bearing columns. He looked for exit signs.

There were no exit signs.

He walked deeper into the building. The floor was uneven, buckled by years of water damage. His boots crunched on broken glass.

*Structural failure,* he thought. *Imminent collapse. Code 3.1.*

He reached the center of the main factory floor. In the middle of the room stood a single object. It wasn't a machine. It wasn't a pile of debris.

It was a chair.

A simple, wooden chair. And sitting on it was a figure.

The figure was tall, thin, and made of shadows. It had no face, only a smooth, blank surface where a face should be. It wore a suit that looked like it had been tailored from smoke.

Gerald stopped. His flashlight beam trembled.

The figure stood up. It moved with a fluidity that defied physics. It turned its blank face toward Gerald.

A voice echoed in the room. It wasn't heard with ears. It was felt in the bones. A vibration in the teeth.

**[CITATION ACCEPTED.]**

The text appeared on Gerald's tablet. Then it disappeared.

**[APPEAL FILED.]**

Gerald's heart stopped, and he hadn't filed an appeal. The drone hadn't filed an appeal.

Who had?

The shadow figure stepped forward. It didn't walk. It slid across the floor, leaving no footprints.

Gerald looked at the tablet. A new window had opened. A Hearing Interface.

**[CASE NO: 894-B]** **[DEFENDANT: GERALD HUTCHINS, CORE ID 001]** **[CHALLENGER: UNIDENTIFIED ENTITY, CORE ID 9999]** **[ISSUE: VALIDITY OF CITATION AGAINST MUNICIPAL INFRASTRUCTURE]**

Gerald stared at the screen.

Core ID 9999. That was a null ID.

That was an error code, which was something that wasn't supposed to exist.

The shadow figure raised a hand. It pointed at Gerald.

"You broke the rules," the voice said. It was calm. It was amused. "And the System rewarded you."

Gerald gripped his flashlight. His knuckles were white.

"I cited a violation," Gerald said. "The drone was operating without proper ventilation."

"The drone is not a building," the shadow said. "It is a tool. You cannot cite a tool. You can only replace it."

Gerald felt a cold sweat break out on his neck. He had been wrong.

The loophole hadn't worked because it was valid, which had worked because the System was glitching.

The drone had locked itself because it couldn't understand the citation, not because it was legally bound by it.

And now, the glitch had drawn attention.

"Who are you?" Gerald asked.

"I am the reason the audit is tomorrow," the shadow said.

The figure snapped its fingers.

The flashlight died.

Darkness swallowed the room.

Gerald heard the sound of chains rattling. Not from the fence. From above.

He looked up. The ceiling of the mill was opening.

Not breaking. *Unfolding.*

The concrete slabs lifted, revealing a sky that was not the sky of Ashford. It was a grid. A infinite, blue grid stretching into the void.

And on the grid, hovering above the mill, was not the drone, which was a hand.

A massive, translucent hand, made of light, reaching down from the heavens.

It wasn't trying to crush the building.

It was trying to pluck Gerald out of it.

Martha screamed. She was trying to run, but the floor beneath her had turned to liquid. She sank to her waist in the concrete, which was now behaving like quicksand.

Gerald grabbed a support column. He held on. The hand descended, its fingers brushing the top of the mill.

The air pressure dropped. Ears popped.

Gerald looked at his tablet. The battery was dying. The screen flickered.

**[WARNING: REALITY STABILITY CRITICAL.]**

He looked at the hand. He looked at the grid above.

He realized then that the Inspector General wasn't coming to check his dungeon.

The Inspector General had already arrived.

And he was holding the city in his palm.

Gerald tightened his grip on the column. He pulled out his pen. He wrote, in the dark, on his trembling hand:

*Violation: Improper extraction of registered Core. Code 0.0.*

The hand paused.

It hovered, just inches above Gerald's head, which was listening.

He wrote the second line, and the fingers twitched.
