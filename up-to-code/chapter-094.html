Chapter 94: Built to Code

# Chapter 94

The form accepted the entry. Slope gradient: 1:8.

Required: 1:12.

Handrail deficiency: one side only. Required: both sides where drop exceeds thirty inches. Drop measured: forty-two inches.

Gerald hit submit.

The corridor groaned.

Not the subtle shudder of the first Citation. This was structural.

The floor tiles along the new passage shifted, a hairline fracture splitting the grout line from the wall junction to the far end.

The single handrail on the left wall pulled free at one bracket, dangling by two screws.

Compliance Rating adjusted from 83 to 68.

Same fifteen-point cap. Gerald had known it would be.

He'd calculated the ceiling before he typed, and the point was not the rating.

The point was what happened next.

The Acting Inspector's corridor was a System-generated structure. It had been spawned as part of the audit enforcement action, which meant it carried the same structural integrity parameters as any Core-built construction. And Gerald had just cited it for three separate violations in the same structural category. Three citations in one category triggered something the System called a Cascade Threshold.

He'd found it in the fine print three chapters ago, buried in a footnote so small it required zooming in on the System interface to read. Cascade Threshold: when a structure accumulates three or more valid citations within the same code category, the structure's material integrity rating drops by one tier per citation beyond the first. First citation: no structural effect.

Second: one tier reduction. Third: two tier reduction.

Fourth and beyond: two tier reduction each, and the corridor had three citations. Fire egress. Load-bearing wall penetration.

Slope and handrail.

All three fell under Structural Code, category 4.7.

Two tier reduction. The corridor's material integrity had just dropped from Tier 3 to Tier 1.

Gerald watched it happen, and the floor tiles were no longer shifting. They were sinking. The corridor's foundation, designed for Tier 3 stability, was now operating at Tier 1.

The slope was increasing on its own. The handrail bracket that had pulled free was now bending.

The far end of the corridor, where it met the Acting Inspector's main dungeon, was visibly sagging.

And then the System flashed a notification Gerald had never seen before.

STRUCTURAL CASCADE FAILURE IMMINENT. Core penalty pending upon collapse. Estimated time to failure: 4 minutes, 20 seconds.

Demolition Penalty. He'd read about it once, in a forum post from a Core who'd lost an entire wing to a poorly-placed support column. The penalty was proportional to the structure's original material tier, and a Tier 3 structure, collapsed through Cascade Failure, would cost the Core twenty-five Compliance points.

Twenty-five. The Acting Inspector would drop to forty-three.

Gerald was at thirty-two, and he did the math again.

He did it three times. Forty-three versus thirty-two.

He was still losing. The audit was still active. The corridor was still pushing toward his core chamber.

But the corridor was dying. It was dying faster than it was advancing.

He watched the sag accelerate. A tile at the far end cracked and fell into the gap between the corridor floor and the underlying substrate. The gap was widening. The corridor was losing its foundation.

And then the Acting Inspector did something Gerald had not expected.

The corridor sped up.

Not structurally. The structure was still failing.

The Acting Inspector started spawning entities into the corridor.

Small things. Crawling things. They swarmed the walls, ceiling, and handrails, reinforcing the failing sections.

Not with proper materials.

With themselves. Bodies packed into the gaps, holding the tiles up.

Holding the handrail in place.

Holding the structure together through sheer mass.

The estimated time to failure jumped from four minutes to eight.

Gerald stared at the notification. The Acting Inspector was using spawns as structural reinforcement.

It was crude, which was desperate.

And it was working.

He checked the corridor's advance rate. The sag was still happening, but slower now. The corridor was still pushing forward, still eating into Gerald's wall, but the rate had dropped by half.

It would reach his core chamber in roughly six minutes. The structure would fail in eight.

Two minutes of margin.

Gerald ran to his own wall.

The one the corridor was eating through. He pressed his interface against it and measured. The wall was his.

His load-bearing wall.

Category 4.1, Structural Code, load-bearing partitions. His dungeon was fully compliant with this category.

He had installed the wall himself, to spec, with proper anchoring and bracing.

The corridor was pushing through it. Slowly. The wall was holding.

But Gerald looked at the wall's junction with the floor. The floor was his too. And the floor was not the problem. The problem was the corridor's foundation, which was sinking into the shared substrate between their dungeons.

He looked at the substrate. It was common ground. Neither dungeon owned it.

It was unassigned space, the void between dungeon footprints. And it was settling.

The corridor's weight was pulling it down.

The wall above it was, technically, still standing.

But its foundation was the substrate. And the substrate was failing.

Gerald pulled up his own Compliance sheet. He scanned it. He found what he needed.

Seismic and Vibration Code. Category 4.9. His dungeon was compliant.

No uncontrolled vibration sources. All mechanical systems isolated.

All structural joints rated for seismic activity.

If he created an uncontrolled vibration source adjacent to the shared substrate, the substrate would accelerate its failure. The corridor would collapse faster. The Acting Inspector would take the Demolition Penalty before the corridor reached his core.

But creating an uncontrolled vibration source would violate his own Category 4.9 compliance. It would drop his Compliance Rating. It would give the Acting Inspector grounds for a counter-Citation. It would give the Inspector General grounds for a violation flag on Gerald's own audit.

Gerald looked at his dungeon. His beautiful, compliant, up-to-code dungeon. Every handrail installed.

Every egress route marked.

Every ventilation duct sized to spec. His Compliance Rating was thirty-two, which was low, but it was low because the System kept moving the goalposts.

His actual code compliance was perfect.

Every category he could control, he had brought to full compliance.

He would have to break it, and he opened a new form. Not a Citation.

A self-declaration.

Form 22-C: Voluntary Non-Compliance Acknowledgment.

He'd never seen this form used. He'd never even seen it referenced.

It existed in the System's form library, filed under "Administrative Exceptions," which was a category Gerald had never opened because it had always made him feel physically ill.

The form had one field. Describe the violation.

Gerald typed: Uncontrolled vibration source, adjacent to shared substrate, estimated amplitude 0.3g, duration approximately four minutes.

The form accepted it. A warning appeared.

WARNING: Filing Form 22-C will reduce your Compliance Rating by 8 points. You will be flagged for audit. This action cannot be reversed within the current audit cycle.

Eight points. He would drop to twenty-four. The Acting Inspector would be at forty-three. The gap would widen to nineteen points.

But the corridor would collapse. The Demolition Penalty would hit. The Acting Inspector would drop to eighteen.

Eighteen versus twenty-four.

Gerald would still be ahead, and he stared at the form. His finger hovered over the submit button.

His stomach turned.

His hands were shaking. He had never filed a non-compliance form in his life. He had never filed one for himself.

He had never filed one for anyone; non-compliance was not filed but found. Something you cited.

Something you punished.

He had never once, in thirty-one years, created a violation.

The corridor groaned again. The spawns were still reinforcing it. The handrail bracket was now detached, hanging by a screw. A tile fell and shattered on the substrate below.

Gerald looked at the estimated failure time. Six minutes. The corridor's advance rate. Four minutes to his core.

Two minutes of margin. Shrinking.

He looked at the form. He looked at his dungeon. The handrails he'd installed himself.

The egress signs he'd printed and mounted. The ventilation ducts he'd sized with a tape measure and a calculator.

The fire suppression system he'd designed from a library book because the System didn't provide one.

All of it.

Every inch. Built to code.

Built to last. Built because it was right.

He typed the submit command.

His finger stopped.

Not because he was afraid. Not because he was uncertain.

Because his hand had simply stopped.

His body had made the decision before his mind did. The same thing that happened when he stood in front of a building that was not up to code and could not bring himself to write the citation because the violation was so obvious, so egregious, so wrong, that the act of documenting it felt like a personal failure.

The corridor was two minutes from his core.

Gerald forced his finger forward. The form submitted.

The floor shook.

Not the corridor.

His floor. A vibration source appeared in his dungeon adjacent to the shared substrate, generated by the System in response to his self-declaration.

It was a mechanical thrum, low-frequency, relentless.

The kind that would set a building inspector's teeth on edge for a hundred meters.

His Compliance Rating adjusted. Thirty-two to twenty-four.

The substrate started to fail.

The corridor's remaining tiles cracked in sequence, one after another, like dominoes falling, and the spawns reinforcing the structure lost their grip. The handrail bracket snapped. The far end of the corridor dropped six inches in a single, sickening lurch.

STRUCTURAL CASCADE FAILURE IN PROGRESS. Core penalty applying upon final collapse.

Gerald watched the corridor die. He watched the Acting Inspector's Compliance Rating begin its descent.

Eighty-three.

Sixty-eight, and the numbers were still there, frozen from his Citations. The penalty was coming.

Twenty-five points.

The corridor's final tile fell.

The notification flashed.

DEMO LITION PENALTY APPLIED. Acting Inspector Compliance Rating adjusted from 68 to 43.

Gerald's rating: 24, and he was ahead. Nineteen points.

The audit was still active, but the Acting Inspector was now below him.

Below the threshold. Below the minimum required to maintain an active audit against a Core with a higher rating.

The audit should have ended, which did not end.

A new notification appeared.

AUDIT STATUS: TRANSFERRED. Primary auditor no longer meets minimum rating threshold. Audit reassigned to: Secondary Inspector.

Gerald read it twice, and he had never heard of a Secondary Inspector. He had never seen the role in any forum post, any System documentation, any audit cycle. He had conducted or been subject to forty-seven audits in his career as a municipal inspector, and he had never once encountered a secondary assignment protocol.

A new corridor started forming in his wall.

This one was different.

The material was darker. The handrails were already installed.

The slope was correct, and the egress signage was already mounted. The ventilation was already ducted.

It was compliant.

It was built to code.

And it was coming faster than the last one.
