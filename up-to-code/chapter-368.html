Chapter 368: Start from Scratch

# Chapter 368

The woman with the steel mask did not blink. Her eyes, visible through the narrow slits in the polished metal, remained fixed on the pen hovering above the signature line. The silence in the Appeals Court was absolute, a vacuum that sucked the sound from Gerald's throat. He could hear the blood rushing in his ears, a frantic, wet thumping that competed with the dry rustle of the document beneath his hand.

Dale's scream had faded into the distance, a thinning echo swallowed by the descent, but the anxiety it left behind lingered in Gerald's chest. It felt like a knot of wet rope, tight and heavy.

"Sign here," the woman said again. Her voice was flat, devoid of the bureaucratic warmth Gerald usually tried to project, or even the hostility he was used to receiving.

It was the voice of a machine that had been calibrated to enforce a single, immutable rule.

"Or I will cite you for loitering. The penalty for loitering in a Judicial Void is immediate deletion of your Core ID."

Gerald lowered his pen. The nib hovered a millimeter above the paper. The ink on the document shimmered, shifting from black to a deep, warning red.

He looked at the clause she had highlighted. *Standard Waiver of Jurisdiction.*

He looked at the bottom of the page. *Effective Immediately.*

This was a trap.

A classic procedural snare. If he signed, he acknowledged that the Architect's erasure of his citations was legal.

He acknowledged that his authority was gone.

He acknowledged that the Appeals Court was the final arbiter of his fate, not the code he had spent thirty-one years memorizing.

But if he didn't sign, he was loitering. And loitering was a Class 4 Infraction.

Gerald's wrist throbbed. The bone was set poorly, jagged against the splint he had fashioned from a broken ruler.

He shifted his weight, and the pain spiked, a white-hot lance driving up his arm.

He gritted his teeth, forcing his breath into a steady, rhythmic count. *In for four.

Hold for four. Out for four.*

The woman tapped a finger on the desk. The sound was sharp, like a hammer striking an anvil.

"Time is wasting, Inspector," she said. "The docket is full."

Gerald looked around the room. The desk was floating in the white void, untethered. There were no walls.

There was no ceiling. There was only the woman, the desk, the document, and the abyss behind her.

And the stairs.

The stairs.

Gerald's eyes narrowed. He looked at the steps leading down from the desk, disappearing into the darkness. They were white, pristine, and perfectly uniform. Too uniform.

He looked back at the woman.

"Excuse me," Gerald said. His voice was raspy, but steady.

The woman paused. Her head tilted, a mechanical motion.

"This is an Appeals Court. Questions are not permitted during the signing phase."

"I am not asking a question," Gerald said. "I am filing a preliminary objection to the venue."

The woman's mask remained still.

"Explain."

Gerald turned away from the desk. He walked to the edge of the platform. The wind howled up from below, carrying the scent of ozone and old, damp paper.

He leaned over the railing, which was not present.

There was no railing. There was only a smooth, polished edge where the platform ended and the void began.

He looked down. The stairs descended into the black.

"Where do these stairs lead?" Gerald asked.

"Down," the woman said.

"To what jurisdiction?"

"The lower courts," she said. "The Deep Cuts. The Abyssal Wards."

Gerald nodded slowly. He turned back to her.

"And the stairs up?"

"Sealed," she said. "Per the Architect's decree."

"And the stairs themselves?"

"Standard issue. Judicial Grade."

Gerald walked back to the desk, and he placed his clipboard on the edge. He picked up a pen from the rack beside the woman's hand. It was a simple ballpoint, standard issue.

He clicked it. Once.

Twice.

"This staircase," Gerald said, "is a violation."

The woman went still. The air in the room seemed to thicken.

"State your claim," she said.

"Section 9.4, Subsection C of the System Structural Code," Gerald said, reciting from memory. The words flowed like water, natural and effortless. "Public access stairwells in judicial or administrative spaces must feature a continuous handrail on both sides, with a minimum clearance of four inches from the wall and a grip diameter between 1.25 and 2 inches. Furthermore, the rise and run of each step must be uniform, with a tolerance of no more than 1/8 inch."

The woman's fingers tightened on her red stamp.

"The stairs are not for public access. They are for appellate movement only."

"Appealants are members of the public until they sign," Gerald said. "I have not signed. Therefore, I am a member of the public. And this staircase has no handrails."

"There are no handrails," the woman said. "There is no structure to attach them to."

"Exactly," Gerald said.

"That is the violation. A structure without a foundation is unsafe.

A staircase without a railing is a hazard. By System Rule 102, any hazard identified in a legal venue invalidates the venue's authority to conduct proceedings until remedied.

"

The woman stared at him. Behind the steel mask, there was a flicker of something.

Confusion?

Annoyance? It was hard to tell.

"That is a creative interpretation," she said.

"It is the letter of the law," Gerald said.

"You cited me for loitering. But you have failed to provide a safe environment for a defendant to exercise their right to appeal.

That is a Class 2 Safety Violation.

The penalty for the presiding officer is suspension of proceedings.

"

The woman stood up. The movement was stiff, jerky.

"You are delaying the inevitable, Hutchins."

"I am correcting a deficiency," Gerald said. "Fix the stairs. Install the handrails. Then I will sign."

"Or?"

"Or I file a Motion for Dismissal based on Unsafe Conditions. And if that fails, I file a Motion for Contempt of Code against you, personally."

The woman froze. The red stamp in her hand glowed brighter.

"You do not have authority to cite me," she said.

"I have authority because I am a citizen of the System," Gerald said. "And because I am currently standing in a venue that is not up to code.

Until it is, I am not under your jurisdiction.

I am under the jurisdiction of the Code itself.

And the Code says this place is illegal.

"

The woman sat back down. Slowly.

She looked at the document. Then she looked at Gerald.

"If you delay this hearing, the Architect will accelerate the purge."

"Then the purge will be halted by a structural injunction," Gerald said. "I am filing Form 77-Z: Emergency Structural Injunction. It requires no signature from the opposing party. It requires only proof of hazard."

Gerald pointed to the edge of the platform.

"Show me the railing," he said.

The woman did not move.

Gerald waited. He kept his hand on his clipboard. His breathing was shallow, controlled. The pain in his wrist was a dull ache now, a background hum.

Minutes passed. The void below remained silent. The wind shifted, changing direction, blowing from the stairs up toward the desk.

It carried a new scent. Not paper.

Not ozone.

Metal. Cold, sharp, industrial metal.

The woman's head snapped up. She looked at the stairs.

Something was coming up, which was not a monster. It was not a person.

It was a machine. A drone, spherical and black, with six articulated arms that unfolded like the legs of a spider. It rose from the abyss, silent and sleek. It hovered over the landing, its optical sensors glowing a soft, pulsing blue.

The woman stood up. Her posture changed. The arrogance drained from her frame, replaced by a rigid, fearful attention.

"Inspector," she said. Her voice was different. Smaller.

"Please.

Do not sign."

Gerald looked at the drone. He looked at the woman.

"Who sent that?" he asked.

"The Architect," she said. "It is an Enforcer. It is here to remove you."

"Remove me how?"

"Deletion," she said. "Physical and digital."

The drone unfolded its arms. From the center of its body, a red laser sight appeared, locking onto Gerald's chest.

Gerald did not move. He looked at the drone. He looked at the empty space where the handrail should have been.

"If it fires," Gerald said, "it violates Section 12.1: Use of Lethal Force in Administrative Proceedings. That requires a signed warrant and a verified threat level of Alpha."

"It is an Architect Enforcer," the woman said. "It does not require warrants."

"It requires a code," Gerald said.

"And this room is not up to code. Therefore, any action taken within it is null and void. If that drone kills me, it is an illegal act.

And the System will punish the issuer.

"

The drone paused. Its optical sensors whirred, adjusting focus.

The woman looked at Gerald. Her steel mask reflected the red laser dot.

"You are gambling with your life," she said.

"I am betting on your incompetence," Gerald said. "Or the Architect's. Which is it?"

The drone tilted. It scanned the platform. It scanned the stairs.

A text box appeared in Gerald's vision.

**SYSTEM ALERT: STRUCTURAL INTEGRITY CHECK IN PROGRESS.**

**DETECTED: MISSING HANDRAIL.**

**DETECTED: MISSING EGRESS SIGNAGE.**

**DETECTED: NON-COMPLIANT LIGHTING.**

**STATUS: VENUE INVALID.**

The red laser sight disappeared.

The drone retracted its arms. It hovered, waiting.

The woman slumped back into her chair. The red stamp in her hand dimmed.

Gerald exhaled. The knot in his chest loosened, just a fraction.

"Fix it," Gerald said. "Install the rail. Put up the sign. Then we can talk about my appeal."

"I cannot build," the woman said. "I am a judge, not a contractor."

"Then summon one," Gerald said.

"That is not in the protocol."

"Then I will cite you for Obstruction of Justice," Gerald said. "And I will appeal to the Inspector General directly. He likes me. We have a history."

It was a bluff.

A massive, terrifying bluff, and the Inspector General had been auditing Cores for months. He had never mentioned Gerald.

But Gerald knew the General's reputation. He was a stickler. A pedant.

A man who would pause an execution to check the thread count on the hangman's noose.

The woman looked at the drone. The drone looked at her.

For a long moment, nothing happened.

Then, the drone moved. It did not attack. It turned. It flew toward the edge of the platform.

And it worked.

One of its arms unfolded, extending a laser cutter. Another arm unfolded, holding a coil of metal tubing.

Sparks flew. White-hot and bright.

The woman watched, paralyzed.

Gerald watched too, and he kept his clipboard close. His broken wrist throbbed, but he ignored it. He was watching the weld.

He was watching the angle.

The drone was installing a handrail.

It was crude, hasty work. The welds were ugly. The angle was off. But it was there.

*Click.*

A sign appeared on the wall. **EXIT.**

The lighting flickered, then stabilized to a standard lumens output.

The drone retracted its arms. It hovered, waiting for orders.

The woman looked at the handrail. She looked at Gerald.

"It is done," she said. Her voice was cold.

"Is it?" Gerald asked. He walked to the edge.

He touched the new rail, which was warm. The weld was solid.

He looked at the angle.

He looked at the woman.

"It is compliant," Gerald said. "Barely."

"Sign," the woman said. She held out the pen.

Gerald took the pen. He looked at the document.

He looked at the drone, and he looked at the abyss.

"If I sign," Gerald said, "I lose my citations."

"Yes."

"But if I don't sign, and I walk away, I am still in violation of the venue's authority to detain me without probable cause, which you have also failed to provide."

The woman's mask shifted. The steel plates rearranged, forming a new expression. Anger? Frustration?

"You are exhausting me, Hutchins."

"That is my job," Gerald said.

He put the pen to the paper, and he did not sign.

Instead, he drew a line through the signature block.

He wrote underneath it: *CONDITIONAL ACCEPTANCE PENDING JURISDICTIONAL REVIEW.*

He signed his name.

But he didn't sign the waiver. He signed a counter-offer.

The document glowed. Then it shattered.

Shards of light exploded outward, hitting the drone, the desk, the woman.

The woman screamed. It was a sound of digital static, tearing and raw.

The drone sparked, its optics cracking.

Gerald fell back, shielding his face with his clipboard.

The room dissolved, and the white stairs were gone. The desk was gone. The woman was gone.

Gerald was falling.

But he was not falling into the abyss.

He was falling into a new space. A space that smelled of wet concrete and rust.

He hit the ground. Hard.

Pain exploded in his wrist. He groaned, rolling onto his side.

He opened his eyes, and he was in a basement. A real basement. Not a void. Not a court.

It was a small, cramped room. The walls were bare concrete. The ceiling was low, lined with exposed pipes. The air was thick with humidity.

And it was not empty.

Standing in the corner, looking at Gerald with wide, terrified eyes, was a rat, which was not just any rat. It was large.

The size of a cat.

It wore a tiny, leather vest.

And in its hand, it held a wrench.

The rat looked at Gerald.

Gerald looked at the rat.

"You're late," the rat said.

Gerald blinked.

"Excuse me?"

"You were scheduled for audit at 09:00," the rat said. "It is 09:05. You are tardy."

Gerald tried to sit up. His body screamed in protest. He forced himself upright, leaning against the wall.

"Who are you?" Gerald asked.

"I am your Compliance Officer," the rat said. "I am here to inspect your dungeon."

Gerald looked around. The room was small. Damp. Crumbling.

"This is not my dungeon," Gerald said. "This is..."

"This is the site of your next project," the rat said. "You have been assigned to renovate the Abandoned Water Treatment Plant. You have forty-eight hours to bring it up to code. If you fail, you will be deleted."

The rat held up the wrench.

"Get to work, Inspector."

Gerald looked at the wrench. He looked at the damp, rotting walls.

He looked at the System notification that appeared in his vision.

**TASK STARTED: CODE COMPLIANCE.**

**CITATION WINDOW: CLOSED.**

**APPEAL WINDOW: OPEN.**

**DEADLINE: 48 HOURS.**

Gerald closed his eyes. He breathed in. He breathed out.

He opened his eyes.

"Where are the blueprints?" Gerald asked.

The rat grinned, showing sharp, yellow teeth.

"Burned," it said. "Start from scratch."
